# The Ten Failure Modes

Applied in Pass 2 to every point produced by Pass 1. Each entry states the mode, the
diagnostic question, and the correction. A point that trips a mode is cut or downgraded — not
softened. Softening produces a hedge, which is worse than either the point or its absence.

---

## 1. Firing at conceded facts

**The mode.** The attack disputes what nobody disputes: an account the opposing institution has
already admitted, a sequence confirmed in the public record, an event whose occurrence is not
in issue.

**Diagnostic.** Has anyone on my side, or any body whose position I represent, already
conceded this? Is the disputed fact doing any work in the argument I am attacking?

**Correction.** Cut, and say so. A closing note stating what is not contested converts the
absence of these attacks from a gap into a display of judgement. Attacking an admitted fact
tells the tribunal that counsel could not find anything better.

**Why it costs.** The heaviest fire is aimed at the strongest part of the opponent's document.
A judge who watches counsel lose a fight he did not need to have discounts everything after.

---

## 2. Wrong-forum objections

**The mode.** Objections drawn from a body of rules that does not govern the proceeding:
hearsay and authentication where there is no fact-finder, pleading standards where there is no
pleading, filing formalities where the document is correspondence.

**Diagnostic.** Which rules actually apply to this forum and this document? Would the tribunal
recognise the objection as available to me, or as imported?

**Correction.** Cut. Where the underlying concern is real — an assertion resting on nothing —
restate it as an evidential gap in ordinary terms rather than as a rule-based objection.

---

## 3. Invented facts and speculative inference

**The mode.** Hypothesised documents ("the registration agreement presumably provides"),
imputed motives, asserted drafting histories, claims that a submission is a circulated
template. The attack supplies what the record does not.

**Diagnostic.** Could I produce a source for this if asked from the bench? Am I inferring, or
am I supposing?

**Correction.** Cut. This mode is the direct violation of the discipline's own prohibition on
inventing facts, and it appears precisely because adversarial momentum makes the invention
feel like inference.

---

## 4. The gotcha with an innocent explanation

**The mode.** Internal chronology, dates, typographical inconsistency, formatting, a shift in
tense. The attack treats a mundane artefact of composition as evidence of bad faith.

**Diagnostic.** What is the first explanation a reasonable reader reaches? If it is innocent —
the author began drafting one day and sent it the next — the point is dead.

**Correction.** Cut. If a genuine reliability concern survives (the document is unsworn, the
author is not identified), state that concern in one line and abandon the gotcha.

**Why it costs.** This mode is seductive because it feels like detection. It is the single
most reliable way to hand the opponent a free reply about the standard of fairness counsel
brings to the exercise.

---

## 5. Self-refuting assertion

**The mode.** The attack denies the opponent an unsupported empirical claim and advances the
opposite, equally unsupported, in the same breath: "there is no evidence for X, and X is at
least as likely false as true."

**Diagnostic.** Have I made a claim of the same kind I have just disqualified?

**Correction.** Keep the objection, delete the counter-claim. If the opponent may not assert
it, neither may I. The objection is stronger alone.

---

## 6. Overclaim on a small point

**The mode.** "Omits entirely," "nowhere addresses," "conceals," "does not mention" — where
the text addresses the matter partially, obliquely, or under a different name.

**Diagnostic.** Search the target text for the thing I say is absent. Is it absent, or merely
underdeveloped?

**Correction.** Downgrade to what is literally true, or cut. An overclaim that the opponent can
refute by quoting one line of his own document destroys the credibility of every other absence
I allege — including the ones that are real.

---

## 7. Form over substance

**The mode.** Objecting to the vehicle rather than the content: that a letter is not a
petition, that a submission is unsworn, that relief was sought informally.

**Diagnostic.** Would this objection, if accepted, have defeated the authority or precedent I
am relying on elsewhere in my own attack?

**Correction.** Cut. It signals fear of the merits, and where the answer to the diagnostic is
yes, it is also incoherent.

---

## 8. Petty demands

**The mode.** Faulting the author for not supplying material no writer of that genre would
supply: credentials, underlying agreements, documentary exhibits, procedural history.

**Diagnostic.** Would a competent author of this kind of document have included this? Does its
absence bear on anything actually in issue?

**Correction.** Cut. Two of these in a submission establish a tone the tribunal will not
forget.

---

## 9. Strawman of the harm

**The mode.** Restating the opponent's injury in its least sympathetic form and defeating that
version: reducing a claim about the loss of a fair opportunity to a claim about airfare.

**Diagnostic.** Would the opponent recognise my statement of his own case? What is the
strongest true description of what he says he lost?

**Correction.** Restate the harm at its highest, then attack the fit between that harm and the
remedy sought. The mismatch argument is stronger against the real harm than against the
caricature, because the caricature invites a correction that costs a page of credibility to
absorb.

---

## 10. Dilution

**The mode.** Four load-bearing points carrying nine passengers. Every additional head lowers
the average quality of the submission and the tribunal's estimate of counsel's judgement.

**Diagnostic.** Rank every surviving point by how much of the outcome it decides. Which three
do I want the tribunal to decide? What is each of the others doing?

**Correction.** Keep the load-bearing points and the free ones — a free point is one that
costs no credibility and requires no defence. Cut the rest. Ceiling of five heads. If more
than five survive the ranking, the ranking was not performed.

---

## Applying the modes

Work point by point, not mode by mode. For each point in Pass 1, run all ten questions, then
the two sustainability tests in the SKILL.md. Record the disposition — kept, downgraded, cut
— and the reason, because the user may ask for the working.

A point may trip a mode and still survive if the correction is available and cheap: an
overclaim downgraded to what is true is a kept point, not a cut one. A point that trips modes
1, 3, 4, or 7 is almost never salvageable, because in each case the defect is that the point
should not have been made at all.
