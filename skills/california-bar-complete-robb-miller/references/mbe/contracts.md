# Contracts — MBE High-Yield & Traps

> The MBE tests general/majority contract law and UCC Article 2. This file lists the most-tested rules, classic distractor traps, and the common-law-vs-UCC splits the MBE loves. For full black-letter rules see `references/essays/contracts.md` and `references/standard-paragraphs/contracts.md`.

## Most-tested MBE topics

- **Offer/acceptance — definiteness:** Common law requires reasonably definite terms (parties, subject matter, price, quantity, time); UCC gap-fillers supply missing price and delivery terms, so a sale-of-goods contract can survive indefiniteness.
- **UCC §2-207 — battle of the forms:** A definite expression of acceptance operates as acceptance even if it states additional or different terms; between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract unless the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, the offeror objects within a reasonable time, or the terms materially alter the deal.
- **Consideration & adequacy:** Adequacy is irrelevant; courts don't police bargains. Look for illusory promises (no consideration), past consideration (no consideration), and pre-existing legal duty (no consideration under common law).
- **Modification:** Common law requires new consideration for contract modification; UCC §2-209 allows modification without consideration if made in good faith.
- **Statute of Frauds:** Contracts within the SOF (land, surety, one-year-impossibility, goods ≥ $500 under UCC §2-201, marriage) must be in a signed writing sufficient to indicate a contract. UCC §2-201 exceptions: merchant confirmation rule, part performance (for goods received/accepted or paid for), specially manufactured goods, judicial admission.
- **Parol evidence rule:** A fully integrated written contract bars extrinsic evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements that contradict or vary its terms; partial integration bars contradiction only. Exceptions let in evidence of fraud, mistake, condition precedent, ambiguity, and subsequent modification.
- **Conditions:** Distinguish condition precedent (must occur for a duty to arise) from condition subsequent (extinguishes a duty already owed) from concurrent conditions. Divisibility and substantial performance doctrines apply under common law; perfect tender applies to UCC goods.
- **UCC implied warranties:** Implied warranty of merchantability (seller must be a merchant of goods of that kind; goods must be fit for ordinary purpose); implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose (any seller with reason to know buyer's particular purpose and buyer's reliance). Both can be disclaimed but only with conspicuous language.
- **Anticipatory repudiation:** An unequivocal statement before performance is due that a party will not perform allows the non-breaching party to treat it as an immediate breach, suspend its own performance, and sue at once or await the time for performance.
- **Remedies/damages:** Expectation damages = benefit of the bargain (put plaintiff in the position performance would have caused); reliance and restitution are alternatives. Consequential damages require foreseeability at time of contract formation (Hadley v. Baxendale rule). Duty to mitigate. UCC buyer's remedies: cover (§2-712), market-price differential (§2-713), specific performance for unique goods.
- **Third-party beneficiaries:** Intended beneficiaries (creditor or donee) can enforce the contract; incidental beneficiaries cannot. Vesting: once an intended beneficiary's rights vest (reliance, assent, or suit), the original parties cannot modify or rescind without consent.
- **Assignment and delegation:** Rights are generally assignable unless the assignment materially changes the obligor's duty, increases burden, or is prohibited by contract or law. Duties can be delegated but the delegator remains liable unless the obligee agrees to a novation. Anti-assignment clauses are construed narrowly.

## Classic distractor traps

- **Applying common law where UCC governs:** Any contract for the sale of movable goods is governed by UCC Article 2 regardless of the parties' sophistication. The MBE will set up a goods transaction and offer a common-law answer as a distractor.
- **Applying UCC where common law governs:** Services contracts, real property, and mixed contracts (dominant-purpose test applies — if the predominant purpose is services, common law governs even if goods are incidentally involved).
- **Mirror-image rule vs. §2-207:** Under common law, any deviation in the acceptance is a counter-offer, not an acceptance. Under UCC §2-207, a non-identical acceptance can still form a contract; the distractor answer applies mirror-image to a goods transaction.
- **Pre-existing duty trap in goods modifications:** Students apply the pre-existing-duty rule to kill a modification of a goods contract — but UCC §2-209 eliminates the consideration requirement for good-faith modifications of sale-of-goods contracts.
- **Consequential damages without foreseeability:** A party breaches and the other suffers a large consequential loss. The MBE distractor awards those damages automatically; the correct rule bars recovery unless the damages were foreseeable at the time of contracting.
- **Perfect tender vs. substantial performance:** Under the UCC, a buyer may reject goods that fail to conform in any respect (perfect tender rule, §2-601), subject to the seller's right to cure. Under common law, substantial performance by a contractor discharges the owner's duty to pay (minus offset for defect). Wrong answers often apply substantial performance to a goods delivery or perfect tender to a construction contract.
- **Promissory estoppel as a substitute for consideration:** Detrimental reliance can make a promise enforceable even without consideration, but only when the promisor should reasonably have expected reliance. MBE distractors either ignore it entirely or over-apply it to arm's-length commercial deals where consideration analysis controls.
- **Oral modifications and the SOF:** A written contract that requires modifications to be in writing is not automatically enforced — a subsequent oral modification may override such a clause under common law (but UCC §2-209(2) enforces such clauses between merchants).
- **Duty to mitigate treated as optional:** The non-breaching party must take reasonable steps to avoid losses; damages that could have been avoided are not recoverable. Distractors award the full unmitigated loss.

## Common law vs. UCC — keep them straight

- **Subject matter:** Common law governs services, real property, and intangibles. UCC Article 2 governs transactions in goods (tangible, movable property). Mixed contracts: apply the dominant-purpose test.
- **Offer/acceptance — definiteness:** Common law demands all material terms; UCC gap-fills price, time, place of delivery, and payment.
- **Acceptance with variance:** Common law — mirror-image rule, any variance = counter-offer. UCC §2-207 — seasonable expression of acceptance forms a contract; additional terms are an offer to modify (between merchants, they become part of the contract unless objected to or materially altering).
- **Modification:** Common law — needs new consideration. UCC §2-209 — no consideration required; only good faith.
- **Breach standard:** Common law — substantial performance discharges the other party's duty (minus damages for defect). UCC — perfect tender rule; buyer may reject for any nonconformity, but seller has a right to cure.
- **Implied warranties:** Common law does not imply warranty of merchantability in service contracts. UCC implies merchantability and fitness for particular purpose in goods transactions; both are disclaimable.
- **Statute of Frauds threshold:** Common law SOF: one year, land, surety, marriage. UCC SOF: goods priced at $500 or more (note: revision proposes $5,000 but traditional MBE tests $500).
- **Anticipatory repudiation — retraction:** Both CL and UCC allow retraction of anticipatory repudiation before the non-breaching party has materially changed position; UCC additionally allows a party with reasonable grounds for insecurity to demand adequate assurance of performance (§2-609).
- **Output and requirements contracts:** Illusory under common law if no quantity stated; UCC §2-306 validates output and requirements contracts measured by good-faith actual output/requirements.

## Sample original MBE questions

---

**Question 1**

A hardware distributor sent a written purchase order to a tool manufacturer for 500 power drills at $80 each, stating: "This order is limited to the exact terms stated herein; no additional terms will be accepted." The manufacturer replied with a signed acknowledgment form that mirrored the quantity and price but added a clause requiring any disputes to be resolved by arbitration. The manufacturer then shipped all 500 drills, which the distributor accepted.

Is there a contract, and if so, does the arbitration clause apply?

- (A) There is no contract because the acknowledgment added a term, making it a counter-offer that the distributor never accepted.
- (B) There is a contract, and the arbitration clause is part of the contract because both parties are merchants and the clause does not materially alter the deal.
- (C) There is a contract, but the arbitration clause does not apply because the distributor's purchase order expressly limited acceptance to its exact terms.
- (D) There is a contract, and the arbitration clause applies because the distributor accepted the drills without objecting to the acknowledgment.

**Correct answer: (C)**

**Rule:** UCC §2-207 governs this transaction in goods. A definite expression of acceptance forms a contract even with additional terms. Between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract *unless* the offer expressly limits acceptance to its own terms — in which case the additional terms drop out.

**Application:** The distributor's purchase order expressly limited acceptance to its exact terms. The manufacturer's acknowledgment was still a valid acceptance (sufficient for contract formation), but the arbitration clause — an additional term — is knocked out by that limitation. The contract exists on the distributor's original terms.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** Applies the common-law mirror-image rule to a goods transaction — incorrect; UCC §2-207 governs.
- **(B)** Correctly identifies a contract exists but misapplies the merchant rule; it overlooks that the offer expressly limited acceptance to its own terms, which prevents additional terms from sneaking in.
- **(D)** Acceptance of the goods confirms the contract but does not constitute assent to additional terms that were already excluded by the express limitation in the offer.

**Question 2**

A homeowner hired a contractor to renovate a kitchen for a fixed price of $40,000. Midway through the project the contractor discovered unexpectedly difficult plumbing that would cost an additional $4,000 to address properly. The homeowner orally agreed to pay the extra $4,000. The contractor completed the renovation. The homeowner paid $40,000 but refused to pay the additional $4,000, arguing no new consideration supported the modification.

Can the contractor recover the additional $4,000?

- (A) Yes, because the unexpected difficulty constitutes sufficient consideration for the modification.
- (B) Yes, because promissory estoppel prevents the homeowner from denying the oral promise.
- (C) No, because under the pre-existing duty rule the contractor was already obligated to complete the renovation for $40,000, and the oral modification lacked new consideration.
- (D) No, because contracts for home renovation must be modified in a signed writing to be enforceable.

**Correct answer: (A)**

**Rule:** The pre-existing duty rule bars enforcement of a modification unsupported by new consideration — *unless* the modification was caused by circumstances that were not anticipated when the original contract was made. An unanticipated difficulty exception allows courts to enforce the modified price because the contractor is providing something beyond what was originally bargained for.

**Application:** The contractor and homeowner formed a services contract (common law governs, not UCC). The plumbing difficulty was genuinely unanticipated; the contractor's agreement to address it goes beyond the original scope, providing fresh consideration (or at minimum satisfying the unanticipated-difficulty exception to the pre-existing duty rule). The $4,000 modification is enforceable.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(B)** Promissory estoppel can substitute for consideration but is a weaker ground here; the cleaner answer is the unanticipated-difficulty exception, and (B) sidesteps the consideration analysis the MBE is testing.
- **(C)** States the pre-existing duty rule correctly but ignores the well-established unanticipated-difficulty exception — the classic MBE trap of knowing the rule but missing the exception.
- **(D)** No such blanket writing requirement applies to modifications of home-renovation contracts under majority common law; this distractor fabricates a SOF rule that does not exist for this category of contract.
