# Criminal Law & Procedure — MBE High-Yield & Traps

> The MBE tests majority/common-law criminal law and FEDERAL constitutional criminal procedure. This file lists the most-tested rules, classic distractor traps, and MBE-vs-California splits. For full black-letter rules see `references/essays/criminal-law-procedure.md` and `references/standard-paragraphs/criminal-law-procedure.md`.

## Most-tested MBE topics

- **Murder (common law):** Unlawful killing with malice aforethought — express (intent to kill) or implied (intent to cause serious bodily harm, depraved-heart recklessness, or felony murder).
- **First-degree murder (modern majority):** Premeditated & deliberate killings, plus killings in enumerated felonies; everything else is second-degree.
- **Felony murder:** Killing during commission of an inherently dangerous felony (BARRK: Burglary, Arson, Robbery, Rape, Kidnapping) is first-degree murder; malice is imputed — intent to kill irrelevant.
- **Voluntary manslaughter:** Intentional killing in the heat of passion on adequate provocation (words alone never sufficient) with no cooling time.
- **Involuntary manslaughter:** Killing through criminal negligence or during commission of an unlawful act (misdemeanor-manslaughter rule).
- **Specific vs. general intent:** Specific-intent crimes require a further subjective purpose beyond the act (murder, theft, attempt, solicitation, conspiracy, assault); general-intent crimes require only intent to do the act.
- **Accomplice liability:** Principal in the second degree (present, aids/abets) and accessory before the fact are liable equally as principal; must share the intent of the principal; liability extends to all natural and probable consequences.
- **Attempt:** Substantial step beyond mere preparation, with specific intent to commit the target crime; legal impossibility is a defense; factual impossibility is not.
- **Conspiracy:** Agreement between two or more persons with intent to agree and intent to achieve an unlawful objective; overt act required in most modern/majority jurisdictions; co-conspirators liable for all foreseeable crimes in furtherance.
- **Solicitation:** Asking another to commit a crime with intent they do the act; completed at the moment of asking — rejection or impossibility is irrelevant.
- **Larceny:** Trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive.
- **Embezzlement:** Fraudulent conversion of property already lawfully in defendant's possession (different from larceny — lawful initial possession).
- **False pretenses:** Obtaining title to property by a known false representation of a material present or past fact with intent to defraud.
- **Robbery:** Larceny from person or presence by force or threat of immediate force; merger of larceny into robbery — no separate larceny conviction.
- **Burglary (common law):** Breaking and entering a dwelling house of another at nighttime with intent to commit a felony therein.
- **Fourth Amendment:** Protects against unreasonable searches and seizures; requires government actor, reasonable expectation of privacy (REP), and warrant unless exception applies.
- **Warrant exceptions:** Search incident to lawful arrest (SILA), exigent circumstances, automobile exception (probable cause), plain view (lawful vantage, inadvertent discovery under some circuits), consent, inventory search, stop-and-frisk (Terry — reasonable suspicion).
- **Exclusionary rule:** Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is suppressed; extends to "fruit of the poisonous tree" (Wong Sun).
- **Good-faith exception:** Evidence not excluded where officers reasonably relied on a warrant later found defective (Leon), unless warrant was facially insufficient or obtained by officer's own fraud.
- **Miranda:** Custodial interrogation requires warnings (right to silence, anything said can be used, right to counsel, appointed counsel if indigent); invocation of right to counsel cuts off all questioning until counsel present; invocation of right to silence requires scrupulous honoring.
- **Sixth Amendment right to counsel:** Attaches at the initiation of formal adversarial proceedings (indictment, arraignment, preliminary hearing); offense-specific; deliberately-elicited statements are suppressed post-attachment.
- **Double jeopardy:** Same offense cannot be prosecuted twice by the same sovereign; Blockburger same-elements test determines whether two charges are the "same offense"; no DJ bar to retrial after successful appeal or hung jury.

## Classic distractor traps

- **Felony-murder merger rule:** If the underlying felony is "merged" into the killing (e.g., assault), it cannot serve as the predicate felony — watch for assault used as the felony.
- **Specific-intent defenses applied to general-intent crimes:** Voluntary intoxication negates specific intent but NOT general intent; mistake of fact negates specific intent only if honest (specific) vs. reasonable and honest (general).
- **Larceny vs. embezzlement:** If defendant had lawful possession at the outset, it is embezzlement — the MBE will call the property "entrusted" as a signal.
- **Larceny vs. false pretenses:** If the victim passed only possession, not title, it is larceny by trick, not false pretenses — look at what the victim intended to transfer.
- **Standing to challenge a search:** Only the person whose Fourth Amendment rights were violated can suppress; co-defendant cannot suppress evidence found in a third party's car/home absent their own REP.
- **Miranda custody vs. interrogation:** Miranda applies only when BOTH custody (reasonable person would not feel free to leave) AND interrogation (express questioning or functional equivalent) are present; volunteered statements need no warning.
- **Miranda waiver:** Waiver must be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary; silence alone is not a waiver; defendant may waive after initially invoking right to silence if a scrupulous-honor break occurred.
- **Sixth Amendment offense-specific rule:** Right to counsel attaches to the charged offense; police may question on a different uncharged offense without counsel present — the MBE will cross offenses.
- **Conspiracy withdrawal:** Withdrawal stops further liability but does not erase the original conspiracy charge; effective withdrawal requires affirmative act to communicate disavowal and undo harm where possible.
- **Attempt abandonment:** Complete and voluntary abandonment is a defense under modern majority; abandonment prompted by fear of detection or victim resistance is NOT voluntary.
- **Double jeopardy — dual sovereignty:** The same conduct may be prosecuted by both federal and state governments (or two states if conduct spanned both) without DJ violation.
- **Depraved-heart murder vs. involuntary manslaughter:** Both involve recklessness; the MBE distinguishes by degree — extreme/conscious disregard of substantial risk = murder; criminal negligence or lesser recklessness = manslaughter.

## MBE vs California — keep two answers straight

- **MBE answer:** Follows majority/common-law rules and federal constitutional doctrine — no SB 1437, no Prop 8, no CA Penal Code specifics.
- **CA felony-murder reform (SB 1437):** CA has narrowed felony murder — liability now requires defendant was actual killer, aided with intent to kill, or was major participant acting with reckless indifference to human life. MBE still tests the broad traditional rule.
- **CA Prop 8 Truth-in-Evidence:** CA constitution generally requires admission of all relevant evidence; CA has fewer exclusionary-rule-based suppression grounds than federal doctrine, though Fourth Amendment federal floor still applies. On the MBE, apply federal exclusionary rule fully.
- **CA murder degrees:** CA Penal Code defines first-degree murder to include lying-in-wait, torture, and certain poison killings. MBE uses generic premeditation/deliberation standard only.
- **CA manslaughter:** CA recognizes "imperfect self-defense" as reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter (unreasonable but honest belief in necessity). MBE majority rule does not universally recognize this doctrine — pick the MBE answer on the MBE.
- **CA conspiracy:** CA does not require an overt act for conspiracy to commit certain serious felonies. MBE majority requires an overt act.
- **Burglary:** CA has first- and second-degree burglary based on inhabited dwelling; common-law and MBE burglary requires nighttime + dwelling + breaking & entering + felony intent.

## Sample original MBE questions

### Question 1 — Criminal Law (Homicide)

A man and his neighbor got into a heated argument over a property line. In a rage, the man grabbed a heavy stone and hurled it at the neighbor, striking him in the head. The neighbor died two days later from the head injury. The man claims he only intended to scare the neighbor, not to kill him.

The man is most likely guilty of:

**(A)** First-degree murder, because he premeditated the act by picking up the stone.
**(B)** Second-degree murder, because throwing a heavy stone at someone's head demonstrates intent to cause serious bodily harm.
**(C)** Voluntary manslaughter, because the killing occurred during a heated argument that constitutes adequate provocation.
**(D)** Involuntary manslaughter, because the man lacked any intent to kill.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

**Rule:** Malice aforethought includes intent to cause serious bodily harm, even absent an intent to kill. Throwing a heavy stone at someone's head objectively reflects intent to cause serious bodily harm, satisfying the malice element for second-degree murder.

**Application:** The man intentionally threw a deadly-weight object at a vital body part. His claimed subjective intent "only to scare" is irrelevant — the conduct objectively establishes SBH intent.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** Premeditation/deliberation requires cool reflection on the decision to kill, not merely picking up an object in rage — this was an impulsive act.
- **(C)** Adequate provocation (words alone, or a neighbor dispute) does not reach the level that would reduce murder to manslaughter; a mere argument over a property line is insufficient.
- **(D)** Involuntary manslaughter requires criminal negligence or an unlawful act; here the intentional violent act and its likely lethal consequence exceed negligence and establish malice.

### Question 2 — Criminal Procedure (Fourth Amendment)

Police received an anonymous tip that drugs were being sold from a blue sedan parked on a public street. An officer approached the car, and through the open window observed a clear plastic bag containing white powder on the passenger seat in plain view. The officer reached in through the open window, seized the bag, and arrested the driver.

The driver moves to suppress the bag as the product of an unlawful search.

The court should:

**(A)** Grant the motion, because the officer had no warrant to seize property from inside the vehicle.
**(B)** Grant the motion, because an anonymous tip alone cannot establish probable cause.
**(C)** Deny the motion, because the plain-view doctrine permitted the warrantless seizure once the officer lawfully observed the contraband.
**(D)** Deny the motion, because the automobile exception authorized both search and seizure without a warrant.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Rule:** Under the plain-view doctrine, a warrantless seizure is valid when (1) the officer is lawfully positioned, (2) the incriminating character of the item is immediately apparent, and (3) the officer has lawful right of access to the object. An officer standing on a public street has a lawful vantage point; observing contraband through an open window in plain view satisfies all three prongs.

**Application:** The officer stood on a public street (lawful position), saw the bag through the open window without any intrusion, and the white powder in a clear bag gave probable cause to believe it was contraband — immediately apparent. The reach through the open window was minimally intrusive and constituted lawful access.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** The warrant requirement has exceptions; plain view is a well-established one, and no warrant is needed to seize an item in plain view with probable cause.
- **(B)** The anonymous tip is what prompted the officer's approach, but the suppression analysis turns on what the officer independently observed — the tip goes to why the officer was there, not whether the seizure was valid.
- **(D)** The automobile exception requires probable cause that contraband is somewhere in the vehicle; here the plain-view doctrine is the cleaner, more direct fit because the specific item was already visible — the automobile exception would apply if the officer needed to search further.
