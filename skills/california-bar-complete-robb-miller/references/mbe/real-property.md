# Real Property — MBE High-Yield & Traps

> The MBE tests majority/common-law property. This file lists the most-tested rules, classic distractor traps, and MBE-vs-California splits. For full black-letter rules see `references/essays/real-property.md` and `references/standard-paragraphs/real-property.md`.

---

## Most-tested MBE topics

**Estates & Future Interests**
- Fee simple absolute: "to A and her heirs" — no condition, fully alienable.
- Fee simple determinable: "so long as / while / until" — possibility of reverter in grantor, automatic forfeiture.
- Fee simple subject to condition subsequent: "but if / provided that / on condition that" — right of entry in grantor, no automatic forfeiture; must be exercised.
- Fee tail: abolished in most states; MBE treats it as fee simple.
- Life estate: measured by a life; waste doctrine applies (voluntary, permissive, ameliorative).
- Reversion (grantor), remainder (third party), executory interest (divests someone).
- Vested remainder vs. contingent remainder: contingent = condition precedent or unascertained taker.

**Rule Against Perpetuities (RAP)**
- Classic RAP: no interest is valid unless it must vest or fail within 21 years after some life in being at creation.
- "Wait and see" and USRAP (90-year saving period) adopted in many states — MBE tests classic RAP unless stated otherwise.
- Fertile octogenarian, unborn widow, and infectious invalidity are classic traps.
- RAP does not apply to reversions, possibilities of reverter, or rights of entry (vested in grantor).

**Concurrent Estates**
- Joint tenancy: four unities (TTIP — time, title, interest, possession) + right of survivorship; must be expressly created.
- Tenancy in common: presumed when unity is missing or grant is ambiguous; no survivorship.
- Tenancy by the entirety: married couples only; neither spouse can unilaterally sever; creditors of one spouse cannot reach it.
- Partition: any co-tenant may seek partition in kind (preferred) or by sale.

**Landlord-Tenant**
- Term of years: fixed end date; no notice to terminate; lease governs.
- Periodic tenancy: continues until proper notice (equal to the period, max 6 months for year-to-year).
- Tenancy at will: terminable by either party; reasonable notice required at common law.
- Tenancy at sufferance: holdover after lawful tenancy; landlord can treat as trespasser or new periodic tenant.
- Implied warranty of habitability (residential): landlord must maintain premises fit for human habitation; tenant remedies = repair-and-deduct, rent withholding, rent abatement, terminate.
- Constructive eviction: landlord's substantial interference renders premises untenantable; tenant must vacate.
- Assignment transfers entire remaining term (privity of estate + contract with L if L consents); sublease transfers less than entire term (original tenant retains reversion, no privity between L and sublessee).

**Easements**
- Appurtenant: benefits dominant estate, burdens servient estate; runs with land automatically.
- In gross: personal benefit; commercial easements in gross are assignable; personal ones are not.
- Creation: express grant/reservation, implication (prior use or necessity), prescription (COAH: continuous, open, actual, hostile — no permission).
- Termination: express release, merger, abandonment (act + intent), estoppel, prescription, condemnation.
- License: revocable permission; irrevocable only by estoppel (licensee invests in reliance).

**Real Covenants & Equitable Servitudes**
- Real covenant (runs at law): writing, intent to run, privity (horizontal + vertical for burden; vertical for benefit), touch and concern, notice.
- Equitable servitude (runs in equity): writing (or implied from common plan), intent, notice, touch and concern — no privity required.
- Common plan/scheme: implied reciprocal negative easements arise when developer sells lots with common restrictions and a buyer has notice.
- Termination: changed conditions (must affect entire restricted area, not just one lot), release, merger, condemnation.

**Land Sale Contracts**
- Statute of frauds: writing, signed, price, parties, property description.
- Part performance (exception): payment + possession, or payment + improvements, or possession + improvements.
- Marketable title: title free from reasonable doubt; encumbrances, adverse claims, zoning violations (if on record) = unmarketable.
- Time of performance: time is not of the essence unless expressly stated or circumstances so imply; buyer gets reasonable time.
- Equitable conversion: once contract signed, buyer holds equitable title; risk of loss shifts to buyer (majority rule) unless seller caused loss or contract says otherwise.
- Merger doctrine: contract obligations merge into deed at closing; only deed covenants survive.

**Deeds & Delivery**
- Types: general warranty (covenants — seisin, right to convey, against encumbrances, quiet enjoyment, warranty, further assurances); special warranty (limits to grantor's own acts); quitclaim (no covenants).
- Delivery: present intent to transfer; physical delivery to grantee creates rebuttable presumption; delivery to agent of grantor ≠ delivery; conditional delivery to third party = effective delivery with condition.
- Acceptance: presumed for beneficial conveyances.
- Void vs. voidable deeds: forged deed = void (BFP gets nothing); deed by fraud in the factum = void; deed by fraud in the inducement = voidable (BFP prevails).

**Recording Acts**
- Race: first to record wins, regardless of notice — rare.
- Notice: subsequent BFP without notice prevails over prior unrecorded interest, even without recording first.
- Race-notice: subsequent purchaser must both lack notice AND record first to prevail.
- BFP elements: purchaser, for valuable consideration (donees and heirs are not BFPs), without notice (actual, constructive/record, or inquiry notice) at time of conveyance.
- Shelter rule: a person who takes from a BFP acquires the BFP's status even if that person had notice.
- Wild deed: deed recorded outside the chain of title provides no constructive notice.

**Mortgages**
- Lien theory (majority/MBE): mortgage is a lien only; mortgagor retains title and possession.
- Title theory (minority): mortgagee holds title until debt paid.
- Due-on-sale clause: lender may accelerate if property sold; "subject to" vs. "assuming" — assuming buyer is personally liable, subject-to buyer is not.
- Foreclosure: judicial foreclosure (majority); mortgagor has statutory right of redemption after sale in many states; equitable right of redemption before sale always exists.
- Priorities: first in time, first in right (subject to recording act); purchase money mortgage (PMM) has superpriority over other liens arising at same time; subordination agreements can alter priority.
- Deficiency judgment: if foreclosure sale price < debt, lender may sue for deficiency (absent anti-deficiency statute).

**Adverse Possession**
- Elements: actual entry, exclusive, open and notorious, hostile/adverse (under claim of right), continuous for statutory period.
- Tacking: permitted if privity of possession between successive possessors (deed, devise, descent, or oral transfer + delivery).
- Disability: statutory period tolled if owner is under disability (minority, insanity, imprisonment) at time adverse possession begins.

## Classic distractor traps

- **Recording act type misapplication:** In a notice state, a subsequent BFP prevails without recording — exam choices often include "A prevails because she recorded first" to lure you into applying race-notice to a pure notice state.
- **Mortgage in lien vs. title theory for joint tenancy severance:** Majority/MBE lien theory = mortgage does not sever joint tenancy; minority title theory = mortgage severs. MBE answer is no severance unless deed or other conveyance.
- **RAP — measuring lives vs. arbitrary lives:** Only lives in being at creation of the interest count; a class gift to "children of A who reach 25" fails if A is alive because a child born after creation could reach 25 more than 21 years after all lives in being die.
- **Touch and concern:** An affirmative covenant to pay money (HOA dues) can touch and concern — don't reflexively say it doesn't run because it's affirmative.
- **Horizontal privity for burden of real covenant:** Majority requires grantor-grantee relationship (deed) or mutual interest (easement); mere neighbors entering a covenant lack it — so covenant may run only in equity, not at law.
- **Equitable conversion risk of loss:** Many exams plant a fire between contract and closing; buyer bears risk (majority) even though not yet in possession — wrong answers assign risk to seller because seller still holds legal title.
- **Shelter rule overlooked:** A party who took from a BFP gets BFP protection. Choices often ignore this and say the shelter-rule beneficiary loses because they had notice.
- **Constructive eviction requires vacancy:** Tenant must actually vacate to claim constructive eviction; choices frequently let tenant stay and still claim it.
- **Sublease vs. assignment misclassification:** If tenant transfers "all remaining months," that is an assignment even if labeled "sublease" — the label is not controlling; substance governs.
- **Voidable vs. void deed and BFP:** A forged signature renders a deed void — BFP cannot prevail; fraud in the inducement renders it voidable — BFP can prevail. Examiners swap the scenarios.
- **Part performance and the Statute of Frauds:** Payment alone is not enough in most states; need at least one additional factor (possession or improvements).
- **Inquiry notice from visible possession:** A buyer who sees someone in possession of the property has inquiry notice and must investigate, even if nothing is recorded — a common trap in recording-act questions.

## MBE vs. California — keep two answers straight

**Recording Act**
- MBE: many questions use a race-notice state (most common U.S. type) but may specify race or notice — read carefully.
- CA: race-notice statute (Civil Code § 1214); a subsequent purchaser must take without notice AND record first to prevail.

**Mortgage Theory & Foreclosure**
- MBE: lien theory state; judicial foreclosure is the standard vehicle tested.
- CA: also lien theory, but the primary foreclosure mechanism is nonjudicial trustee's sale under a deed of trust (not a mortgage); foreclosure by trustee's sale is faster and extinguishes junior liens.

**Anti-deficiency & One-Action Rule**
- MBE: deficiency judgment generally available after foreclosure sale shortfall.
- CA ESSAY: Code of Civil Procedure §§ 580a–580d and § 726 impose (1) one-action rule (lender must exhaust security before suing on the note) and (2) anti-deficiency protection for purchase-money loans on 1–4 unit owner-occupied dwellings; also bars deficiency after nonjudicial trustee's sale regardless of loan type.

**Adverse Possession**
- MBE: standard elements; statutory period varies by state in hypotheticals.
- CA: 5-year period AND claimant must have paid property taxes for those 5 years (Civil Code § 1007); missing either element defeats the claim.

**Joint Tenancy Severance**
- MBE: mortgage = no severance (lien theory); unilateral deed to self (straw party) or to third party = severance.
- CA: same lien-theory result for mortgages; CA also allows severance by a written declaration recorded by the joint tenant (Civil Code § 683.2) — MBE does not test this.

**Landlord-Tenant**
- MBE: implied warranty of habitability applies to residential leases; tenant has standard remedies.
- CA: broader tenant protections (AB 1482 rent caps, just-cause eviction for covered units, right to repair and deduct up to one month's rent); not tested on MBE but critical for CA essays.

**Waste**
- MBE and CA: same general doctrine; CA additionally recognizes that removing fixtures can constitute waste even when arguably beneficial.

### Question 1 — Recording Act / BFP

Owen owned Blackacre in fee simple. On March 1, Owen conveyed Blackacre by warranty deed to Alma. Alma did not record the deed. On April 15, Owen conveyed Blackacre by warranty deed to Bart, who paid fair market value and had no knowledge of Alma's deed. Bart recorded his deed on April 16. On May 1, Alma recorded her deed.

The jurisdiction has the following recording statute: "No conveyance or mortgage of real property shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless the same be recorded."

Who has superior title to Blackacre?

- (A) Alma, because she received her deed first and Owen had no interest left to convey to Bart.
- (B) Alma, because she recorded before any court adjudicated the dispute.
- (C) Bart, because he paid valuable consideration, had no notice of Alma's prior deed, and recorded before Alma.
- (D) Bart, because he recorded before Alma regardless of whether he had notice.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Rule:** This is a notice statute. A subsequent purchaser for value without notice of a prior unrecorded interest prevails over that prior interest. Because Bart paid fair market value and had no actual, constructive, or inquiry notice of Alma's unrecorded deed when he received his conveyance on April 15, he qualifies as a bona fide purchaser. He prevails even though Alma later recorded first.

**Application:** Alma's deed was unrecorded when Bart took. Bart paid value and had no notice. The notice statute protects him the moment he took his deed — subsequent recording by Alma does not defeat his already-vested protection.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- (A) Wrong — the "first in time" principle is displaced by recording acts; recording acts exist precisely to protect subsequent purchasers against prior unrecorded interests.
- (B) Wrong — subsequent recording by a prior grantee does not retroactively defeat a BFP who had already taken without notice; litigation timing is irrelevant to title priority under the recording act.
- (D) Wrong — this describes a race statute (first to record wins regardless of notice); this jurisdiction's statute is a notice statute, so recording sequence without notice analysis is insufficient; if Bart had known of Alma's deed, he would not be a BFP and recording first would not save him.

### Question 2 — Joint Tenancy Severance

Portia and Quinn took title to Greenacre as joint tenants with right of survivorship. Several years later, Quinn executed a promissory note secured by a mortgage on her undivided half-interest in Greenacre and delivered it to Lender. Quinn later died, survived by Portia. The jurisdiction follows the lien theory of mortgages.

What is the state of title to Greenacre?

- (A) Portia owns Greenacre in fee simple, free of Lender's mortgage, because Quinn's death extinguished her interest before Lender could foreclose.
- (B) Portia and Lender own Greenacre as tenants in common, because the mortgage severed the joint tenancy when Quinn granted it.
- (C) Lender holds a mortgage lien on an undivided half of Greenacre as a tenant in common with Portia, because the mortgage severed the joint tenancy.
- (D) Portia owns Greenacre in fee simple because Quinn held title theory title, which passed to Portia on survivorship.

**Correct Answer: (A)**

**Rule:** In a lien-theory state, a mortgage is only a lien on the property — it does not transfer title or an ownership interest to the mortgagee. Because a joint tenancy is severed only by a transfer of a joint tenant's ownership interest (e.g., by deed), a mortgage alone does not sever the joint tenancy. Quinn's interest therefore remained a joint tenancy interest. Upon Quinn's death, the right of survivorship operated: Portia took Greenacre in full. The lien, having attached only to Quinn's defeasible joint tenancy interest, is extinguished when that interest is extinguished by death.

**Application:** Quinn mortgaged her interest but did not deed it away. The joint tenancy survived intact. Quinn's death triggered survivorship. Lender's lien had nothing to attach to once Quinn's interest vanished, because in lien theory the lien never became an ownership stake.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- (B) Wrong on two counts: lien theory means the mortgage is not a conveyance of title and thus cannot sever the joint tenancy; even if severance had occurred, Lender would hold a lien, not an ownership interest as a tenant in common.
- (C) Wrong — same lien-theory error; the mortgage does not sever the joint tenancy in a lien-theory state, so there is no tenancy in common to be in.
- (D) Wrong — inverts the rule; title theory is the minority approach that would sever the joint tenancy; the question states lien theory applies, making (D) both legally wrong and factually backwards.
