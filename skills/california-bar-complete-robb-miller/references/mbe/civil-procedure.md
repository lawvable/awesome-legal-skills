# Civil Procedure — MBE High-Yield & Traps

> The MBE tests FEDERAL civil procedure (national/majority law). This file lists the most-tested rules, classic distractor traps, and MBE-vs-California splits. For full black-letter rules see `references/essays/civil-procedure.md` and `references/standard-paragraphs/civil-procedure.md`.

---

## Most-tested MBE topics

- **Subject-matter jurisdiction — diversity (28 U.S.C. § 1332):** Complete diversity required (every plaintiff diverse from every defendant at time of filing); amount in controversy must exceed $75,000 (not equal it); citizenship of a corporation = state of incorporation PLUS principal place of business (nerve-center test); unincorporated entities take the citizenship of every member.
- **Subject-matter jurisdiction — federal question (28 U.S.C. § 1331):** Cause of action must arise under federal law on the face of a well-pleaded complaint; a federal defense or anticipated federal counter-argument does NOT create federal-question jurisdiction.
- **Supplemental jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1367):** Court may exercise supplemental jurisdiction over state claims that share a common nucleus of operative fact; § 1367(b) strips supplemental jurisdiction in diversity-only cases when a plaintiff asserts claims against persons joined under Rules 14, 19, 20, or 24, or when persons are joined as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or intervene under Rule 24.
- **Personal jurisdiction:** Specific jurisdiction requires purposeful availment of the forum state and claims arising out of or relating to those forum contacts (Bristol-Myers Squibb); general jurisdiction only where defendant is "at home" (domicile for individuals; state of incorporation or principal place of business for corporations).
- **Venue (28 U.S.C. § 1391):** Proper in any district where any defendant resides if all defendants reside in the same state, where a substantial part of the events giving rise to the claim occurred, or (as fallback in diversity) where any defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction.
- **Erie doctrine:** Federal court sitting in diversity applies state substantive law and federal procedural law; outcome-determinative test refined by balancing federal interests — statutes of limitations, elements of a claim, and burden of proof are substantive; pleading form and discovery mechanics are procedural.
- **Notice pleading (Rule 8):** Complaint requires a short and plain statement showing entitlement to relief (Twombly/Iqbal plausibility standard); special matters (fraud, mistake) must be pleaded with particularity under Rule 9(b).
- **Personal service and sufficiency of process (Rule 4):** Service must be made within 90 days of filing or complaint is dismissed without prejudice; waiver of service available; proper service is a prerequisite to personal jurisdiction.
- **Rule 12 motions:** Defenses of lack of personal jurisdiction, improper venue, insufficient process, and insufficient service of process are waived if omitted from first Rule 12 motion or responsive pleading; failure to state a claim and failure to join a required party under Rule 19 are never waived before trial; lack of SMJ is never waived.
- **Amended pleadings (Rule 15):** Amendment as of right within 21 days of serving the pleading or 21 days after a Rule 12 motion/responsive pleading; thereafter, leave freely granted when justice requires; relation-back requires same transaction/occurrence and, for new parties, notice within Rule 4(m) period plus knowledge that but for mistake the action would have been brought against them.
- **Summary judgment (Rule 56):** No genuine dispute of material fact and moving party entitled to judgment as a matter of law; non-moving party must cite specific record evidence, not rest on pleadings; court views evidence in light most favorable to non-movant.
- **Class actions (Rule 23):** Must satisfy all four Rule 23(a) requirements (numerosity, commonality, typicality, adequacy) plus at least one Rule 23(b) category; (b)(3) class requires predominance and superiority; notice and opt-out rights attach to (b)(3) only.
- **Claim preclusion (res judicata):** Final judgment on the merits by a court of competent jurisdiction bars re-litigation of all claims that were or could have been raised in the same transaction or occurrence; default judgments and consent judgments are "on the merits."
- **Issue preclusion (collateral estoppel):** Issue actually litigated, necessarily decided, and essential to judgment; party against whom it is asserted must have had a full and fair opportunity to litigate; offensive non-mutual collateral estoppel permitted in federal courts at court's discretion (Parklane Hosiery).
- **Joinder — compulsory counterclaims (Rule 13(a)):** Counterclaim arising from the same transaction or occurrence as the opposing party's claim must be asserted or is waived; permissive counterclaims (different transaction) may be brought but require independent basis for jurisdiction.
- **Impleader (Rule 14):** Defendant may implead a third party who is or may be liable to the defendant for all or part of the plaintiff's claim; plaintiff may not use Rule 14 directly.
- **Intervention (Rule 24):** Intervention of right if applicant claims interest in property/transaction, disposition may impair that interest, and existing parties may not adequately protect it; permissive intervention if applicant's claim/defense shares common question of law or fact with main action.
- **Discovery scope (Rule 26):** Parties may obtain discovery of any nonprivileged matter relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to needs of the case; attorney work product is conditionally protected (overcome by substantial need and inability to obtain equivalent without undue hardship); opinion work product is absolutely protected.
- **Appellate jurisdiction — final judgment rule (28 U.S.C. § 1291):** Courts of appeals have jurisdiction only over final judgments; exceptions include interlocutory injunctions (§ 1292(a)), certified questions (§ 1292(b)), collateral-order doctrine, and writs of mandamus.

## Classic distractor traps

- **PJ vs. venue confusion:** A court can have personal jurisdiction but improper venue, or proper venue but no personal jurisdiction — they are independent requirements. Improper venue is waivable; lack of PJ is also waivable but goes to constitutional power over the defendant.
- **SMJ treated as waivable:** SMJ cannot be waived, cannot be conferred by consent, and can be raised for the first time on appeal or even by the court sua sponte. Distractors often suggest a party "waived" a SMJ defect by not raising it early.
- **Diversity citizenship of corporations:** Many wrong answers use only state of incorporation, ignoring the principal-place-of-business prong. Both states count; if a corporation's PPB is in the same state as the plaintiff, diversity is destroyed.
- **Amount-in-controversy aggregation:** A single plaintiff may aggregate multiple claims against a single defendant to reach $75,000+. Multiple plaintiffs generally cannot aggregate separate claims to satisfy the threshold unless the claims are joint (Zahn rule); under § 1367, once the anchor claim meets the threshold, other class members may join without independently satisfying the amount.
- **§ 1367(b) carve-out scope:** The strip-back applies only when the original claim rests solely on § 1332 diversity and only when plaintiffs (not defendants) are asserting claims against Rule 14/19/20/24 parties. A defendant's cross-claim or counterclaim is not restricted by § 1367(b).
- **Well-pleaded complaint rule:** Federal-question jurisdiction is not created by a federal defense, a federal counterclaim, or a plaintiff's anticipation that a federal issue will be raised. The federal question must appear in the plaintiff's own claim.
- **12(b)(6) vs. summary judgment standard:** On a 12(b)(6) motion the court looks only at the pleadings (and documents incorporated by reference); on summary judgment the court looks at the full record. Distractors often credit evidence outside the pleadings to a Rule 12(b)(6) analysis.
- **Relation-back for new parties — "mistake" requirement:** Relation-back under Rule 15(c)(1)(C) requires that the new party knew of the action AND knew that but for a mistake concerning identity, the action would have been brought against them. Mere ignorance of a party's existence is not a "mistake" about identity — distractors blur this distinction.
- **Compulsory counterclaim waiver:** Failing to plead a compulsory counterclaim in the pending action bars it later (claim preclusion effect). Distractors offer scenarios where the party brings the omitted claim as a new action and ask whether it is barred — answer is yes for compulsory, no for permissive.
- **Claim preclusion "could have been raised":** The bar extends to all claims arising from the same transaction, not only those actually litigated. Distractors present a new legal theory on the same facts and imply it is a different claim.
- **Issue preclusion — "necessarily decided" requirement:** If a prior judgment could have rested on either of two independent grounds, neither issue is necessarily decided; neither is entitled to preclusive effect.
- **Erie — confusing substantive/procedural:** Statutes of limitations, choice-of-law rules, and burden-of-proof allocations are substantive under Erie. Federal pleading standards under Rule 8 are procedural. Distractors ask which body of law applies to something like a statute of limitations and offer "federal" as an answer.

## MBE vs California — keep two answers straight

- **Subject-matter jurisdiction:** Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction requiring a basis (diversity, federal question, etc.). California state courts are courts of general jurisdiction — no need to establish a SMJ basis for a state-court complaint. On the MBE always ask "is there a federal hook?"; on a CA essay presume jurisdiction unless the question raises a specific bar.
- **Pleading standard:** Federal courts use notice pleading under Rule 8 with the Twombly/Iqbal plausibility gloss. California uses fact pleading — ultimate facts must be alleged, not mere conclusions, and a demurrer (not a Rule 12(b)(6) motion) is the vehicle to challenge a legally insufficient pleading.
- **Erie doctrine:** Tested only on the MBE/federal side. California essays test California substantive law directly; Erie is irrelevant to a pure California essay question unless the fact pattern places you in federal court.
- **7th Amendment jury-trial right:** The 7th Amendment right to a civil jury applies in federal court (law side of the docket) and is not incorporated against the states. California has its own constitutional jury right (Cal. Const. Art. I § 16) which is tested on CA essays — the source and scope differ; treat them independently.
- **Claim preclusion — transaction vs. primary rights:** Federal courts use the transactional test: all claims from the same transaction or occurrence are merged in the judgment. California uses the primary-rights theory: each distinct primary right gives rise to a separate cause of action, so a plaintiff may bring successive suits if different primary rights are violated (e.g., personal injury and property damage from the same accident can be separate suits under CA law, but would be barred as a single transaction in federal court).
- **Compulsory joinder and indispensable parties:** Federal Rule 19 uses a two-step (necessary then indispensable) analysis. California's equivalent (CCP § 389) mirrors the federal approach but CA essays should cite the California Code, not Rule 19.
- **Removal and remand:** Purely a federal concept tested on the MBE. California essays do not test removal procedure; if a CA essay fact pattern is set in state court, stay there.
- **Discovery — work-product protection:** Federal Rule 26(b)(3) codifies work-product doctrine. California follows a parallel statutory scheme (CCP § 2018.030) with similar but not identical scope — on the MBE apply Rule 26; on a CA essay cite the CCP.
- **Class actions:** Federal Rule 23 is the MBE standard. California has its own class-action statute (CCP § 382) and different certification criteria — tested separately on CA essays.

## Sample original MBE questions

### Question 1

Pemberton, a citizen of Oregon, filed suit in federal district court in Nevada against Drayco Corp., a corporation incorporated in Delaware with its principal place of business in Nevada, and against Solis, a citizen of Nevada. Pemberton seeks $90,000 in compensatory damages from both defendants jointly for injuries sustained when a Drayco product allegedly malfunctioned. Drayco and Solis move to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

How should the court rule?

- (A) Deny the motion, because Pemberton is diverse from Drayco and the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000.
- (B) Grant the motion, because Solis is a Nevada citizen and Pemberton's claims are jointly asserted, destroying complete diversity.
- (C) Grant the motion, because Drayco's principal place of business in Nevada makes it a Nevada citizen, and Solis is also a Nevada citizen, destroying complete diversity regardless of how the claims are framed.
- (D) Deny the motion, because venue is proper in Nevada and venue and subject-matter jurisdiction are the same inquiry.

**Correct: (C).**

Complete diversity requires that no plaintiff share citizenship with any defendant. Drayco is a citizen of both Delaware (state of incorporation) and Nevada (principal place of business). Because Drayco is a Nevada citizen and Solis is also a Nevada citizen, diversity is destroyed on two independent grounds. The amount in controversy is irrelevant once diversity fails.

- (A) fails because it ignores the principal-place-of-business prong of corporate citizenship — Drayco is also a Nevada citizen.
- (B) reaches the right result but for an incomplete reason; complete diversity fails because of Drayco's Nevada citizenship as well, not only because of Solis.
- (D) conflates venue with SMJ; they are wholly independent requirements, and proper venue cannot cure an SMJ defect.

### Question 2

Hartwell sued Fenwick in federal district court, asserting a state-law negligence claim. The court entered judgment for Hartwell. Fenwick then filed a new federal action against Hartwell asserting that Hartwell had negligently damaged Fenwick's vehicle in the same collision that gave rise to Hartwell's suit. Hartwell moves to dismiss on claim-preclusion grounds.

Should the court grant Hartwell's motion?

- (A) No, because Fenwick's claim is a separate legal theory arising from a different legal right.
- (B) No, because Fenwick was the defendant in the prior action and defendants are never required to bring counterclaims.
- (C) Yes, because Fenwick's vehicle-damage claim arises from the same transaction and could have been raised as a compulsory counterclaim in the prior action, and the failure to do so bars it now.
- (D) Yes, because issue preclusion prevents Fenwick from relitigating any issue decided in the prior action.

**Correct: (C).**

Under Rule 13(a), a counterclaim arising from the same transaction or occurrence as the opposing party's claim is compulsory — it must be asserted or it is lost. The vehicle-damage claim arises directly from the same collision. Because Fenwick failed to raise it as a compulsory counterclaim, it is barred when Fenwick attempts to assert it in a subsequent suit.

- (A) is wrong because claim preclusion (and the compulsory-counterclaim rule) bars claims based on the same transaction regardless of the legal theory invoked — a "different right" framing is the California primary-rights approach, not the federal transactional test.
- (B) is wrong because while defendants are not always required to bring counterclaims, they are required to bring compulsory ones or lose them; "defendants are never required" misstates Rule 13(a).
- (D) is wrong in its characterization: the correct doctrine here is claim preclusion (via the compulsory-counterclaim rule), not issue preclusion. Issue preclusion applies to specific findings actually and necessarily decided, not to entire claims that were never litigated.
