# Constitutional Law — MBE High-Yield & Traps

> The MBE tests FEDERAL constitutional law. This file lists the most-tested rules, classic distractor traps, and MBE-vs-California notes. For full black-letter rules see `references/essays/constitutional-law.md` and `references/standard-paragraphs/constitutional-law.md`.

## Most-tested MBE topics

- **Standing** — Plaintiff needs injury-in-fact (concrete, particularized, actual or imminent), causation (traceable to defendant's conduct), and redressability. Taxpayer standing fails unless the Establishment Clause + spending-power nexus applies (*Flast* exception).
- **State action** — The Constitution constrains government actors, not private parties. Exceptions: public function (running elections, company towns) and entanglement (government compels or encourages private discrimination).
- **Commerce power** — Congress may regulate (1) channels of interstate commerce, (2) instrumentalities and persons/things in interstate commerce, (3) activities with a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Non-economic intrastate activity aggregated only if part of a broader economic scheme.
- **Dormant Commerce Clause (DCC)** — Absent congressional authorization, states may not discriminate against or unduly burden interstate commerce. Discriminatory laws are near-per-se invalid unless narrowly tailored to a legitimate local interest. Non-discriminatory laws survive Pike balancing (local benefit vs. burden on commerce).
- **Privileges & Immunities (Art. IV §2)** — States may not discriminate against out-of-staters regarding fundamental rights (livelihoods, civil rights) without substantial justification. Applies only to citizens/natural persons — corporations are excluded.
- **Levels of scrutiny** — Strict: fundamental rights or suspect classifications (race, national origin, alienage*); government must show compelling interest + narrow tailoring. Intermediate: quasi-suspect classifications (sex, legitimacy); substantially related to important interest. Rational basis: everything else; law upheld if rationally related to any legitimate interest; challenger bears burden.
- **Equal Protection** — Applies to states via 14th Amendment; applies to federal government via 5th Amendment due process. Key triggers: facial discrimination vs. discriminatory purpose + disparate impact required for strict scrutiny.
- **Due Process — Procedural** — Requires a protected life, liberty, or property interest before process is due. Balancing: private interest + risk of erroneous deprivation + government's fiscal/administrative burden (*Mathews v. Eldridge*).
- **Due Process — Substantive** — Fundamental rights (marriage, procreation, contraception, family relations, parental rights, interstate travel, voting) trigger strict scrutiny. Non-fundamental economic/social interests get rational basis.
- **First Amendment — Free Speech** — Content-based restrictions: strict scrutiny. Content-neutral time/place/manner: intermediate scrutiny (significant interest, narrowly tailored, leaves open alternative channels). True threats, incitement to imminent lawless action, obscenity, defamation, and child pornography are unprotected categories.
- **Prior restraints** — Presumptively unconstitutional; require procedural safeguards (prompt judicial review, government bears burden).
- **First Amendment — Establishment Clause** — Government neutrality toward religion. No official endorsement, no coercive religious activity, no excessive entanglement. *Lemon* test still appears on MBE; *Kennedy* and *Dobbs*-era cases add coercion and historical-practice analysis.
- **Free Exercise Clause** — Neutral, generally applicable laws need only rational basis (*Smith*). Laws targeting religion or not neutral/generally applicable get strict scrutiny.
- **Takings** — Per se taking: permanent physical occupation or regulation eliminating all economic value. Regulatory taking: *Penn Central* factors (economic impact, investment-backed expectations, character of government action). Public use = public purpose (broad deference). Just compensation = fair market value.
- **Presidential/executive power** — *Youngstown* framework: presidential power highest when Congress authorizes; in a twilight zone when Congress is silent; lowest when Congress prohibits. Non-delegation doctrine tested rarely but doctrinally live.
- **11th Amendment / sovereign immunity** — States immune from suits in federal court by private parties unless state consents, Congress abrogates under §5 of 14th Amendment (not Article I powers), or Ex parte Young allows prospective injunctive relief against state officers.

## Classic distractor traps

- **Wrong scrutiny tier** — The most common MBE trap. Race/national origin → strict. Sex → intermediate. Economic regulation or age → rational basis. When a law targets a "vulnerable" group but the group is not suspect/quasi-suspect, examiners expect rational basis, not strict scrutiny.
- **Missing the state-action requirement** — A private actor's discriminatory conduct is not a constitutional violation. Watch for facts where a private club, employer, or individual acts badly — the Constitution simply does not apply without a government nexus.
- **DCC vs. Privileges & Immunities confusion** — DCC applies to all commerce; P&I Art. IV applies only to fundamental rights of natural persons. Corporations cannot invoke P&I. A license fee discriminating against out-of-state commercial fishermen implicates both; a zoning restriction that happens to affect commerce usually only implicates DCC.
- **Rational basis burden** — Under rational basis, the challenger must negate every conceivable legitimate justification; the government need not even articulate one. Distractors often present rational basis as if the government must prove its justification — it does not.
- **Content-neutral vs. content-based misclassification** — A law regulating the time, place, or manner of speech is content-neutral even if it incidentally limits speech on one topic. A law that singles out speech "because of its message" is content-based even if the government calls it neutral. Noise ordinances, permit systems tied to crowd control, and secondary-effects regulations are content-neutral traps.
- **Overreading the spending power** — Congress may attach conditions to federal funds, but conditions must: (1) be unambiguous, (2) relate to the federal interest in the program, (3) not be coercive (unconstitutional gun-to-the-head). Distractors label a condition unconstitutional simply because it influences state policy.
- **Calling dicta "the rule"** — Several MBE options misstate *Roe*/*Dobbs*, *Lochner*, or *Korematsu* as current operative law. Know what the current doctrine actually is.
- **Procedural due process without a protected interest** — Students overlook that there must be a cognizable property or liberty interest before any process is required. Government employment at-will creates no property interest; a for-cause-only tenure arrangement does.
- **Prior restraint vs. subsequent punishment** — A licensing scheme that requires advance approval is a prior restraint even if it could have been prosecuted after the fact. Distractors frame it as a valid time/place/manner rule.
- **11th Amendment overreach** — Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity under Article I commerce power (*Seminole Tribe*). Abrogation requires §5 of the 14th Amendment + congruent and proportional remedy.

## MBE vs. California — notes

- The MBE tests only **federal** constitutional law; CA bar essays also test federal con law (not a separate California constitution subject on essays). Divergence is minimal.
- **Speech broader under CA Constitution** — The California Constitution's free-speech clause (Art. I, §2) is independently broader than the First Amendment and can apply to large private shopping centers (*Robins v. Pruneyard*). The MBE will not test this; flag it only if an essay specifically raises the CA constitution.
- **Equal protection — CA Prop 47 and criminal justice** — California has voter-initiative constitutional amendments (e.g., Prop 8 on marriage, litigated under federal EP) that created unique CA-specific equal protection questions in essay history. MBE does not test state-level EP variations.
- **Jury trial** — CA Constitution Art. I, §16 guarantees jury trial in civil cases more broadly than the federal 7th Amendment (which does not apply to states). MBE tests the federal rule only.
- **Takings / exactions** — *Nollan/Dolan* nexus-and-proportionality test applies both federally and in California. No meaningful divergence for MBE purposes.
- **Bottom line** — For the MBE, apply federal doctrine exclusively. For CA essays, apply the same federal doctrine unless the question explicitly invokes the California Constitution.

## Sample original MBE questions

**Question 1**

A state legislature, concerned about election integrity, enacts a law requiring any organization that runs voter-registration drives to post a $50,000 surety bond before canvassing in the state. The law applies equally to all organizations regardless of their political affiliation or the content of their speech. A nonprofit that conducts nonpartisan voter-registration drives challenges the law as a violation of the First Amendment.

Which of the following best describes the appropriate constitutional standard?

- (A) Strict scrutiny, because the law burdens the fundamental right to vote.
- (B) Strict scrutiny, because the law is a content-based restriction on political speech.
- (C) Intermediate scrutiny, because the law is a content-neutral regulation of speech-related conduct that must be narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest and leave open alternative channels.
- (D) Rational basis, because the law regulates only the administrative mechanics of elections, not the content of any speech.

**Correct answer: C**

**Rule:** A content-neutral law that regulates the time, place, or manner of speech or speech-related conduct is evaluated under intermediate scrutiny — the government must show a significant interest, the regulation must be narrowly tailored to serve that interest, and alternative channels must remain available.

**Application:** The bond requirement is facially content-neutral (applies to all canvassing organizations regardless of message). It does, however, directly burden the nonprofit's expressive conduct (voter-registration outreach). The court applies intermediate scrutiny, not rational basis, because the law regulates a protected activity.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** The law does not restrict anyone from voting; it regulates an organization's canvassing operations. The "fundamental right to vote" trigger for strict scrutiny applies when the right to cast a ballot is burdened, not when ancillary electoral activities face regulation.
- **(B)** Content-based strict scrutiny applies when the government targets speech because of its message. Here the law applies to all canvassing regardless of political viewpoint or topic — it is content-neutral on its face and in purpose.
- **(D)** Rational basis is too deferential when a law directly burdens First Amendment activity. Even a content-neutral law touching protected expression must at minimum survive intermediate scrutiny.

**Question 2**

Congress passes a statute directing the Department of Labor to withhold 15% of federal highway funds from any state that does not adopt a specific prevailing-wage schedule for all public construction projects. A state that relies on highway funds for 38% of its annual road budget refuses to comply and sues, arguing the condition is unconstitutional.

Which argument provides the state's strongest constitutional challenge?

- (A) The condition exceeds Congress's commerce power because it regulates intrastate wage-setting.
- (B) The condition violates the Tenth Amendment because it commandeers state legislatures to enact federal policy.
- (C) The condition is unconstitutionally coercive under the Spending Clause because the threatened loss of funds is so substantial that states have no real choice but to comply.
- (D) The condition violates the Dormant Commerce Clause because it discriminates against states that set their own wage scales.

**Correct answer: C**

**Rule:** Congress may attach conditions to federal spending grants, but the conditions must not be so coercive that they amount to a "gun to the head," leaving states no genuine choice (*South Dakota v. Dole*; *NFIB v. Sebelius*). When threatened loss of existing funding is so large that consent is not voluntary, the condition exceeds the Spending Clause.

**Application:** Losing 15% of highway funds may or may not clear the coercion threshold on its own, but the fact pattern notes the state depends on those funds for 38% of its road budget. Under *NFIB v. Sebelius*, where threatened loss would devastate a state's ability to provide core services, a court may find unconstitutional coercion. This is the state's strongest argument.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** Congress does not need to justify a spending condition under the Commerce Clause. The Spending Clause independently authorizes conditional grants, and the condition need only relate to the federal interest in the funded program — not independently regulate intrastate commerce.
- **(B)** Anti-commandeering (*Printz*) prohibits Congress from directing state executive officers to enforce federal law. A spending condition, however, leaves the state free to refuse the funds; it does not order the legislature to enact anything. Commandeering requires a direct mandate — a conditional offer is different in kind.
- **(D)** The Dormant Commerce Clause limits state laws that discriminate against interstate commerce. It has no application to an act of Congress; Congress has plenary authority to authorize or override the DCC. A federal spending statute cannot violate the DCC.
