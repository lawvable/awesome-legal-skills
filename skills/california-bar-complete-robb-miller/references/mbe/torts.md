# Torts — MBE High-Yield & Traps

> The MBE tests majority tort law. This file lists the most-tested rules, classic distractor traps, and MBE-vs-California splits. For full black-letter rules see `references/essays/torts.md` and `references/standard-paragraphs/torts.md`.

## Most-tested MBE topics

**Intentional Torts & Defenses**
- **Battery**: intentional harmful or offensive contact with another's person; contact need not cause physical injury; plaintiff need not be aware at the moment of contact.
- **Assault**: intentional act causing reasonable apprehension of imminent harmful/offensive contact; words alone insufficient without conduct.
- **False imprisonment**: intentional confinement of plaintiff within bounded area; plaintiff must know of confinement or be harmed by it; reasonable means of escape defeats the claim.
- **Intentional infliction of emotional distress (IIED)**: extreme and outrageous conduct; intent or recklessness; severe emotional distress; bystander liability requires presence + knowledge by defendant.
- **Trespass to land**: intentional entry onto another's land without consent; no damage required; extends above and below surface.
- **Trespass to chattels / conversion**: intermeddling (trespass) vs. serious dispossession or destruction (conversion); conversion allows full fair-market-value damages.
- **Consent defense**: must be within scope; invalidated by duress, fraud, incapacity, or mistake defendant induced.
- **Self-defense / defense of others**: reasonable belief of imminent threat; proportionate force; majority allows mistaken defense of others if belief was reasonable.
- **Necessity**: private necessity (incomplete privilege, must pay for harm caused); public necessity (complete privilege, no liability).

**Negligence**
- **Duty**: reasonable-person standard for foreseeable plaintiffs; special duties for professionals, landowners, and affirmative-act creators of risk.
- **Breach**: custom is evidence but not dispositive; cost-benefit (Hand formula) analysis; res ipsa loquitur allows inference of breach when: (1) accident normally does not occur absent negligence, (2) instrumentality in defendant's exclusive control, (3) plaintiff did not contribute.
- **Actual causation**: but-for test (single cause); substantial factor test (multiple sufficient causes — each defendant liable); loss-of-chance doctrine in some jurisdictions.
- **Proximate causation**: foreseeable type of harm; defendant need not foresee exact manner or extent; superseding intervening causes break chain only if unforeseeable; foreseeable third-party negligence does not supersede.
- **Damages**: actual damages required for negligence (unlike intentional torts); eggshell-plaintiff rule — take plaintiff as found; no recovery for pure economic loss without physical harm (economic loss rule).

**Negligence Per Se & Res Ipsa**
- **Negligence per se**: statutory violation establishes duty and breach if plaintiff is in class statute protects and harm is of type statute prevents; excuse can negate per se.
- **Res ipsa**: establishes inference of breach; defendant may rebut; plaintiff still must prove causation and damages.

**Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress (NIED)**
- **Zone-of-danger (majority)**: plaintiff must be in physical danger and suffer physical manifestation of emotional distress.
- **Bystander NIED**: close relationship + presence at scene + contemporaneous perception (some jurisdictions relax physical-harm requirement for bystanders).

**Strict Liability**
- **Wild animals**: owners strictly liable for harm caused by dangerous propensities; no negligence showing required.
- **Domestic animals**: strict liability only if owner had prior knowledge of dangerous propensity ("one bite rule" majority).
- **Abnormally dangerous activities**: six-factor Restatement test; high gravity of harm + inappropriate location key factors; proximate cause still required; harm must be within the risk that makes activity abnormally dangerous.

**Products Liability**
- **Manufacturing defect**: deviation from intended design; strict liability applies.
- **Design defect**: consumer-expectations test (majority) or risk-utility/feasible-alternative-design test (Restatement Third); negligence and strict liability both available.
- **Warning defect (failure to warn)**: duty to warn of non-obvious risks; learned-intermediary doctrine for prescription drugs.
- **Parties in the chain of distribution**: strict liability for commercial sellers; occasional sellers and service providers generally excluded.

**Defamation**
- **Elements**: defamatory statement of fact; of or concerning plaintiff; publication to third party; damages (libel = presumed per se; slander = special damages unless slander per se).
- **Constitutional overlay — fault requirements**: public official/public figure = actual malice (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard); private figure/public concern = negligence (majority); private figure/private concern = strict liability possible.
- **Defenses**: truth (absolute); absolute privilege (judicial, legislative, spousal); qualified privilege (lost if abused).

**Privacy Torts**
- Intrusion upon seclusion: reasonable expectation of privacy; highly offensive to reasonable person.
- False light: false impression; public concern requires actual malice.
- Appropriation: unauthorized use of name/likeness for commercial advantage.
- Public disclosure of private facts: public disclosure; not of legitimate public concern; highly offensive.

**Nuisance**
- **Private nuisance**: unreasonable and substantial interference with use and enjoyment of land; intentional, negligent, or abnormally dangerous conduct.
- **Public nuisance**: unreasonable interference with a right common to the public; private plaintiff must show special harm distinct from general public.

**Vicarious Liability**
- **Respondeat superior**: employee acting within scope of employment; frolic vs. detour distinction.
- **Independent contractors**: general rule — no vicarious liability; exceptions: inherently dangerous activity, non-delegable duty, apparent authority.
- **Joint enterprise**: mutual agency among participants; each liable for others' torts.

## Classic distractor traps

- **Palsgraf / zone of danger**: if plaintiff was outside the foreseeable zone of danger, no duty runs to her — a distractor will make facts seem unfair but the legal issue is the duty boundary, not the defendant's general carelessness.
- **Actual cause vs. proximate cause**: always analyze both separately; "but-for" establishes actual cause but does not resolve proximate cause — the MBE loves presenting an unforeseeable plaintiff or unforeseeable manner of harm as a "no liability" trap.
- **Substantial-factor trap**: use it only when two independent sufficient causes each would have caused the harm alone; do not use it as a substitute for but-for whenever causation is uncertain.
- **Transferred intent**: transfers across the five original intentional torts (battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land, trespass to chattels); does NOT transfer to IIED — a distractor answer will apply transferred intent to emotional distress claims.
- **Products liability theory confusion**: strict liability for manufacturing defect is easy; for design defect and warning defect the MBE will offer a negligence answer and a strict liability answer — both can be correct, but the question asks for the BEST theory; manufacturing defect = strict liability; design/warning = both viable, but identify the correct elements.
- **Defamation public vs. private figure fault**: a private figure plaintiff on a public-concern matter needs only negligence, not actual malice — distractors will apply the higher actual-malice standard or conversely suggest no fault is needed.
- **Defamation opinion vs. fact**: pure opinion not actionable; mixed opinion that implies undisclosed defamatory facts IS actionable — do not reflexively choose "opinion" just because the statement seems evaluative.
- **Slander per se vs. slander**: loathsome disease, criminal conduct, business/trade unfitness, and serious sexual misconduct are slander per se (no special damages) — distractors will require special damages where per se applies.
- **Defense mismatch**: consent is a defense to battery but not negligence; assumption of risk in its primary form is a complete defense only to certain claims — check whether the defense applies to the specific tort charged.
- **Res ipsa and exclusive control**: modern courts relax "exclusive control" to allow it against multiple defendants; a distractor will eliminate res ipsa because multiple parties touched the instrumentality.
- **Contributory negligence distractor**: many MBE choices frame contributory negligence as a complete bar — that is the minority rule; unless the question specifies jurisdiction, apply comparative fault.
- **Strict liability animal knowledge**: the "one bite rule" trap — owner of domestic animal without prior notice of dangerous propensity is NOT strictly liable; a distractor will impose strict liability based on the severity of the attack alone.
- **Necessity damages**: private necessity is not a complete defense — defendant still owes compensation for damage caused; the MBE will offer "no liability" as a distractor when the issue is measure of damages, not liability.

## MBE vs California — keep two answers straight

- **Comparative fault system**: MBE distractors use contributory negligence (complete bar) or 50% modified comparative fault as wrong answers — the MBE default for questions asking you to apportion is pure comparative fault; **CA essay answer = pure comparative negligence (Li v. Yellow Cab Co., 1975)**.
- **Assumption of risk**: MBE treats express assumption of risk as a complete defense and implied assumption of risk as potentially reducing or barring recovery depending on the jurisdiction; **CA essay answer = implied assumption of risk is fully folded into comparative fault analysis (Knight v. Jewett); express assumption of risk survives as a separate complete defense**.
- **Landowner duty**: MBE uses the traditional trespasser/licensee/invitee trichotomy with specific duties for each class; **CA essay answer = general reasonable-care duty to all entrants (Rowland v. Christian, 1968); retain trichotomy only for trespassers under Calvillo-Silva nuance**.
- **Products liability — bystanders**: Restatement Third (MBE trend) extends strict liability to foreseeable bystanders; **CA follows this approach but confirm under current CA Products Liability doctrine**.
- **NIED bystander**: MBE majority requires zone-of-danger or physical manifestation; **CA allows bystander NIED without physical injury if close relationship, contemporaneous sensory awareness, and severe distress (Thing v. La Chusa)**.
- **Defamation — private figure**: MBE applies negligence for private figure/public concern; **CA follows same constitutional floor but note CA Civil Code anti-SLAPP (§ 425.16) is an essay procedure issue, not MBE-tested**.
- **Punitive damages**: MBE requires malice, oppression, or fraud for punitives; **CA Civil Code § 3294 codifies same standard — consistent, but watch for the essay instruction to cite the statute**.

## Sample original MBE questions

### Question 1 — Negligence Causation

A highway contractor left an unmarked trench alongside a rural road after completing work for the evening. A driver swerved to avoid a dog that ran into the road, left the pavement, and struck an unmarked barrier at the trench edge, causing injuries. The driver sued the contractor for negligence.

Which of the following best describes the contractor's liability?

- **(A)** The contractor is not liable because the dog's entry onto the road was an unforeseeable superseding cause that broke the chain of proximate causation.
- **(B)** The contractor is not liable because the driver was contributorily negligent in failing to maintain lane control.
- **(C)** The contractor is liable because leaving an unmarked trench beside a road creates a foreseeable risk that drivers diverted by road hazards will collide with it.
- **(D)** The contractor is liable only if the driver can prove the contractor had actual notice that animals frequently crossed at that location.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Rule & Application**: A negligent actor is liable for foreseeable types of harm, even if the precise sequence is unexpected. Drivers veering off a road due to sudden hazards is a foreseeable risk that the duty to mark an open trench is designed to prevent. The dog is a foreseeable intervening cause — it does not supersede because it is within the class of events that makes leaving an unmarked trench dangerous.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** Wrong: A foreseeable intervening cause (animal darting into road) does not supersede the original negligence; it is precisely the kind of event that makes an unmarked roadside trench dangerous.
- **(B)** Wrong: Contributory negligence is a minority complete-bar rule and on these facts the driver's reaction to an animal is not clearly negligent; comparative fault analysis would reduce, not eliminate, recovery.
- **(D)** Wrong: The contractor's duty does not require actual prior notice of the specific hazard; the foreseeable risk of vehicles leaving the road surface suffices.

### Question 2 — Intentional Tort / Products Liability

A manufacturer produced an industrial cleaning solution intended for commercial floor maintenance. Due to an error at the filling station, a batch was bottled at three times the labeled concentration. A janitor used the product according to label directions and suffered severe chemical burns. The janitor sued the manufacturer.

Under which theory does the janitor have the strongest strict liability claim?

- **(A)** Design defect, because the product's formula created an unreasonable risk of harm.
- **(B)** Failure to warn, because the label did not describe the enhanced concentration.
- **(C)** Manufacturing defect, because the product deviated from the manufacturer's intended design.
- **(D)** Negligence per se, because the mislabeled concentration violated federal labeling regulations.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Rule & Application**: A manufacturing defect exists when the specific unit deviates from the manufacturer's own specifications or intended design. Here the product was filled at three times the intended concentration — a paradigm manufacturing defect. Strict liability attaches without proof of negligence. The janitor used the product correctly, so comparative fault is not in play.

**Why each distractor fails:**
- **(A)** Wrong: Design defect challenges the product line as a whole; here the design (correct concentration) was safe — one aberrant batch does not implicate the design.
- **(B)** Wrong: Failure to warn addresses omitted safety information for an inherent risk; the label was inaccurate because of the manufacturing error, not because of an omitted warning about a known risk — manufacturing defect better fits.
- **(D)** Wrong: Negligence per se is a negligence theory, not strict liability; it establishes duty and breach via statutory violation but requires proof of causation and damages and does not eliminate the fault element the way strict products liability does.
