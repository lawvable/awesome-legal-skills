# Torts — Essay Bank

> CA tests general (multistate) tort law. The big anchor is **negligence** (duty →
> breach → causation → damages), with intentional torts, strict/products liability,
> defamation/privacy, and nuisance as satellites. Watch CA's **pure comparative
> negligence**. All rule language original. Flag uncertain law `> ⚠️ VERIFY`.

**High-frequency note:** Negligence is the most-tested structure — run all four elements
for every defendant, then defenses. Intentional torts come in clusters (run each of the
seven against each actor + transferred intent). Always end with damages and the relevant
**Remedies** analysis.

## Part 1 — Attack Outline (condensed, one-screen)

```
INTENTIONAL TORTS (intent + causation; transferred intent for the first 5)
   ├─ battery — harmful/offensive contact to P's person
   ├─ assault — reasonable apprehension of imminent contact
   ├─ false imprisonment — confinement to a bounded area
   ├─ IIED — extreme & outrageous conduct → severe distress (recklessness ok)
   ├─ trespass to land — physical invasion of real property
   ├─ trespass to chattels — minor interference with possession
   ├─ conversion — serious interference → pay full value
   DEFENSES — consent, self-defense/others/property, necessity (public/private)
ECONOMIC / DIGNITY TORTS
   ├─ defamation — defamatory statement, of/concerning P, published, damages (+ falsity & fault if public concern)
   ├─ privacy — appropriation, intrusion, false light, public disclosure
   ├─ misrepresentation — intentional (fraud) / negligent
   ├─ malicious prosecution; abuse of process
   ├─ interference with business relations
NEGLIGENCE — duty → breach → actual cause → proximate cause → damages
   ├─ duty — foreseeable P (Cardozo zone); standard = reasonable person; special standards
   ├─ breach — custom, statute (negligence per se), res ipsa loquitur
   ├─ causation — actual (but-for / substantial factor / alternative); proximate (foreseeability, intervening)
   ├─ damages — actual harm required; eggshell P
   ├─ defenses — comparative negligence (CA = pure), assumption of risk
STRICT LIABILITY — animals, abnormally dangerous activities
PRODUCTS LIABILITY — manufacturing / design / warning defects; theories
NUISANCE — private (substantial & unreasonable interference) / public
VICARIOUS LIABILITY — respondeat superior, independent contractors, joint enterprise
```

## Part 2 — IRAC Rule Blocks (full, memorizable)

### Intentional Tort — General Prima Facie Case
🟢

**Rule.** Every intentional tort requires (1) a volitional act by the defendant, (2)
intent — either the purpose to bring about the result (specific) or knowledge with
substantial certainty that it will occur (general), and (3) causation. Under **transferred
intent**, intent transfers between the torts of battery, assault, false imprisonment,
trespass to land, and trespass to chattels, and between victims. The plaintiff's
hypersensitivity is not considered, and there are no incapacity defenses.

**Here.** Here, [defendant] acted with [intent] and caused [result].

**Thus.** Thus, the intentional-tort threshold is met.

### Battery / Assault / False Imprisonment
🟢

**Rule.** **Battery** is an intentional harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff's
person (judged by a reasonable-person standard of offensiveness). **Assault** is an
intentional act creating a reasonable apprehension of imminent harmful or offensive
contact (words alone usually insufficient). **False imprisonment** is an intentional act
or omission that confines the plaintiff to a bounded area, of which the plaintiff is aware
or harmed by, with no reasonable means of escape.

**Here.** Here, [defendant] [contacted / threatened / confined] [plaintiff].

**Thus.** Thus, [defendant] is liable for [tort].

### Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress
🟡

**Rule.** IIED requires (1) extreme and outrageous conduct exceeding all bounds of decency,
(2) intent or recklessness, (3) causation, and (4) severe emotional distress. For a
bystander, the plaintiff recovers by satisfying the elements or by showing presence at the
scene, a close relationship to the victim, and the defendant's knowledge of both.

**Here.** Here, [defendant]'s conduct was extreme and outrageous, causing [plaintiff]
severe distress.

**Thus.** Thus, IIED is established.

### Trespass to Land / Chattels / Conversion
🟡

**Rule.** **Trespass to land** is an intentional physical invasion of another's real
property (including by a tangible object). **Trespass to chattels** is an intentional
interference with another's possessory right causing minor damage or dispossession.
**Conversion** is an interference so serious that the defendant must pay the chattel's full
value.

**Here.** Here, [defendant] [entered the land / interfered with the chattel] to the degree
of [trespass/conversion].

**Thus.** Thus, [defendant] is liable for [tort].

### Defenses to Intentional Torts
🟡

**Rule.** **Consent** (express or implied by custom/objective conduct, valid only within its
scope and by one with capacity) is a defense. The **protective privileges** — self-defense,
defense of others, and defense of property — allow reasonable force to prevent a tort about
to be or being committed (deadly force only against a threat of serious harm; no deadly force
solely to protect property). **Necessity** (public or private) justifies interference with
property to avoid a greater threatened harm.

**Here.** Here, [defendant] [reasonably defended / acted out of necessity].

**Thus.** Thus, the defense [bars/reduces] liability.

### Negligence — Duty
🟢  ·  *the master structure*

**Rule.** A defendant owes a duty of reasonable care to all foreseeable plaintiffs within
the zone of danger created by the defendant's conduct (Cardozo majority view; Andrews would
extend to all). The default standard is that of a reasonably prudent person under the
circumstances; special standards apply to professionals (custom of the profession), children
(like-age/experience, except adult activities), and common carriers/innkeepers (high care).
Landowner duties vary by entrant status (trespasser, licensee, invitee), though many
jurisdictions and California use a general reasonableness standard.

**Here.** Here, [defendant] owed [plaintiff], a foreseeable victim, a duty of reasonable
care.

**Thus.** Thus, a duty existed.

> **CA divergence:** California (Rowland v. Christian) rejects the rigid common-law entrant
> categories and applies a **general duty of reasonable care** to all entrants, weighing
> foreseeability factors.

### Negligence — Breach
🟢

**Rule.** Breach is conduct falling below the standard of care, shown by the facts, by
violation of a statute (**negligence per se** — plaintiff in the protected class, harm of the
type the statute aimed to prevent), or by **res ipsa loquitur** (the injury would not
ordinarily occur absent negligence, the instrumentality was in the defendant's exclusive
control, and the plaintiff did not contribute), which permits an inference of breach.

**Here.** Here, [defendant]'s conduct fell below the reasonable standard because [facts /
statutory violation / res ipsa].

**Thus.** Thus, [defendant] breached.

### Negligence — Causation
🟢

**Rule.** The plaintiff must prove **actual cause** — the harm would not have occurred but
for the defendant's conduct (or, with multiple sufficient causes, the conduct was a
substantial factor; with indivisible harm from unknown defendants, alternative liability
shifts the burden) — and **proximate cause** — the harm was a foreseeable result, with
liability cut off by unforeseeable superseding intervening forces (foreseeable intervening
forces, like ordinary medical negligence or rescue, do not break the chain).

**Here.** Here, the harm would not have occurred but for [defendant]'s act and was a
foreseeable consequence.

**Thus.** Thus, both actual and proximate cause are satisfied.

### Negligence — Damages & Defenses
🟢  ·  *CA = pure comparative*

**Rule.** Negligence requires actual damages (nominal damages are unavailable); the
defendant takes the plaintiff as found (**eggshell** rule). Contributory/comparative fault
reduces or bars recovery: under **pure comparative negligence** the plaintiff's recovery is
reduced by her percentage of fault even if greater than the defendant's; under modified
comparative she is barred at 50/51%. **Assumption of risk** (knowing, voluntary) may reduce
or bar recovery.

**Here.** Here, [plaintiff] suffered [harm] and was [X]% at fault.

**Thus.** Thus, recovery is reduced by [X]%.

> **CA divergence:** California follows **pure comparative negligence** (Li v. Yellow Cab) —
> a plaintiff 90% at fault still recovers 10%. CA has also largely merged assumption of risk
> into comparative fault (primary vs. secondary assumption of risk).

### Strict Liability
🟡

**Rule.** A defendant is strictly liable, without regard to fault, for harm caused by (1)
wild animals or known dangerous propensities of domestic animals, and (2) **abnormally
dangerous activities** — those posing a foreseeable, highly significant risk that cannot be
eliminated by reasonable care and that are not common in the community. Liability extends to
the kind of harm that makes the activity dangerous.

**Here.** Here, [defendant]'s [activity] is abnormally dangerous and caused the kind of harm
that risk entails.

**Thus.** Thus, strict liability applies.

### Products Liability
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Contracts (warranties)*

**Rule.** A commercial supplier is strictly liable for a product that is defective when it
leaves the defendant's control and causes injury to a foreseeable user. Defects are (1)
**manufacturing** (the product departs from its intended design), (2) **design** (a
reasonable alternative design would have reduced foreseeable risk — risk/utility or consumer-
expectation test), or (3) **warning** (inadequate warnings or instructions about non-obvious
risks). Negligence, implied warranty of merchantability, and misrepresentation are
alternative theories.

**Here.** Here, the product had a [manufacturing/design/warning] defect that caused [injury].

**Thus.** Thus, the supplier is strictly liable.

### Defamation
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Con Law (First Amendment)*

**Rule.** Defamation requires (1) a defamatory statement of or concerning the plaintiff, (2)
publication to a third party, and (3) damage to reputation (libel is presumed; slander
requires special damages unless slander per se). Where the matter is of **public concern**,
the Constitution adds (4) **falsity** and (5) **fault** — actual malice (knowledge or reckless
disregard of falsity) for public officials/figures, and at least negligence for private
plaintiffs. Defenses include truth, consent, and absolute or qualified privilege (the latter
lost by abuse or malice).

**Here.** Here, [defendant] published a false defamatory statement about [plaintiff] with the
requisite fault.

**Thus.** Thus, defamation is established.

### Invasion of Privacy
🟡

**Rule.** Four privacy torts: (1) **appropriation** of the plaintiff's name or likeness for
commercial advantage, (2) **intrusion** upon seclusion that is highly offensive to a
reasonable person, (3) **false light** — widespread publication attributing to the plaintiff
views or actions, objectionable to a reasonable person, and (4) **public disclosure** of
private facts objectionable to a reasonable person. Causation and damages (emotional distress
suffices) are required.

**Here.** Here, [defendant] [appropriated / intruded / publicized] as to [plaintiff].

**Thus.** Thus, [the privacy tort] is established.

### Nuisance
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Real Property*

**Rule.** A **private nuisance** is a substantial and unreasonable interference with another's
use and enjoyment of land (balancing the gravity of harm against the utility of the conduct).
A **public nuisance** unreasonably interferes with the health, safety, or property rights of
the community; a private party recovers only by showing a harm different in kind from the
public.

**Here.** Here, [defendant]'s [activity] substantially and unreasonably interferes with
[plaintiff]'s land use.

**Thus.** Thus, a nuisance exists.

### Vicarious Liability
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Business Associations (agency)*

**Rule.** Under **respondeat superior**, an employer is vicariously liable for torts committed
by an employee within the scope of employment (not for substantial frolics, but yes for minor
detours; generally not for intentional torts unless job-related). A principal is generally not
liable for the torts of an **independent contractor**, except for non-delegable duties or
inherently dangerous activities.

**Here.** Here, [employee] committed the tort within the scope of employment.

**Thus.** Thus, [employer] is vicariously liable.

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:** Torts ↔ **Remedies** (always finish with the damages/injunction
  analysis); ↔ **Con Law** (defamation/privacy free-speech limits); ↔ **Business Associations**
  (respondeat superior, agency); nuisance ↔ **Real Property**.
- **Sleeper issues:** transferred intent across torts; negligence per se class/harm fit; res
  ipsa exclusive control; superseding vs. foreseeable intervening causes; NIED categories
  (zone of danger, bystander); pure comparative fault reducing — not barring — recovery in CA.
- **CA-divergence cheat list:** pure comparative negligence (Li); Rowland general-duty for
  land entrants; assumption of risk folded into comparative fault; CA recognizes negligent
  infliction with the bystander (Dillon/Thing) factors.
- **Call-of-the-question patterns:** "What torts has D committed / what are P's claims?" → run
  every actor through each tort + defenses + damages. "Is D liable to P?" → full negligence
  IRAC then alternative theories.
