# Constitutional Law — Essay Bank

> All rule language is original. This file covers **federal** constitutional law,
> which is what the California Bar Exam tests. CA constitutional distinctions are noted
> where they appear as grader-rewarded divergences. Flag uncertain or
> recently-changed law with `> ⚠️ VERIFY`.

**High-frequency note:** Con Law essays typically open with a justiciability
screen (standing is the #1 tested gateway), pivot to a power question (usually
Commerce Clause or separation of powers), and then run individual-rights analysis
(equal protection and due process are the perennial closers). The subject almost
always crosses with itself — layers of doctrine appear in one fact pattern. Examinees
who skip justiciability and go straight to the merits lose easy points; examinees who
forget to identify the correct level of scrutiny lose the whole individual-rights
analysis.

## Part 2 — IRAC Rule Blocks (full, memorizable)

### Justiciability — Overview
🟢  ·  *gate every con law essay — run this first*

**Rule.** Article III limits federal court jurisdiction to actual cases or controversies.
A court must decline to hear a matter that is not justiciable, regardless of the merits.
The justiciability doctrines — standing, ripeness, mootness, political question, and the
bar on advisory opinions — all derive from this structural limitation.

**Here.** Here, before reaching the constitutionality of [the statute], the court must
confirm that [plaintiff] presents a justiciable controversy.

**Thus.** Thus, if any justiciability defect exists, the court lacks power to adjudicate.

---

### Standing — Constitutional Minimum
🟢

**Rule.** A plaintiff must satisfy three constitutional requirements to establish standing:
(1) an **injury-in-fact** that is concrete and particularized, and actual or imminent —
not speculative; (2) a **causal connection** (traceability) between the injury and the
defendant's challenged conduct; and (3) **redressability** — a likelihood that a
favorable decision will remedy the injury. The plaintiff bears the burden of establishing
standing.

**Here.** Here, [plaintiff] claims [the regulation caused its cost to rise / the law
exposed it to prosecution], which is [concrete/speculative]; the harm traces to
[defendant's action]; and [injunctive relief striking the law would eliminate] the harm.

**Thus.** Thus, [plaintiff] [has/lacks] constitutional standing.

---

### Standing — Taxpayer Standing
🟡

**Rule.** A federal taxpayer generally lacks standing to challenge how Congress spends
federal funds, because a taxpayer's interest in the general fisc is too diffuse to
constitute a particularized injury. The narrow exception established in *Flast v. Cohen*
allows taxpayer standing only to challenge congressional spending under the Taxing and
Spending Clause that violates the Establishment Clause.

**Here.** Here, [plaintiff] sues as a federal taxpayer challenging [appropriations to
fund religious schools], which [falls within / outside] the *Flast* exception.

**Thus.** Thus, taxpayer standing [exists/fails].

> ⚠️ VERIFY: *Hein v. Freedom from Religion Foundation* (2007) confined *Flast* to
> legislative appropriations, not executive discretionary spending — confirm current
> boundaries.

---

### Standing — Third-Party (Jus Tertii) and Associational Standing
🟡

**Rule.** As a general rule, a plaintiff must assert her own legal rights. A party may
assert the rights of a third party only if: (1) the plaintiff has suffered an independent
injury, and (2) a special relationship exists between plaintiff and the third party, or
the third party faces practical obstacles to asserting her own rights. An organization
has associational standing to sue on behalf of its members when: (a) at least one member
would have standing to sue in her own right, (b) the interest at stake is germane to the
organization's purpose, and (c) neither the claim nor the relief requires individual
member participation.

**Here.** Here, [the doctor-patient relationship / the practical chilling effect on
patients] justifies [plaintiff's] assertion of [third party's] rights; or, [organization]
meets all three prongs because [one member is injured, the claim is germane, and
declaratory relief is sought].

**Thus.** Thus, third-party standing [is/is not] available.

### Ripeness
🟡

**Rule.** A case is ripe when the injury is sufficiently concrete that judicial resolution
is appropriate. Courts consider (1) the fitness of the issues for judicial decision — are
the relevant facts fully developed, is the question purely legal — and (2) the hardship
to the parties from withholding review. Pre-enforcement challenges to a statute are ripe
when the plaintiff faces a credible threat of prosecution.

**Here.** Here, [the regulation has been enacted and plaintiff faces immediate compliance
costs / the law has not been applied and no concrete facts are in dispute], so the claim
is [ripe/premature].

**Thus.** Thus, the case is [ripe for review / not yet ripe].

---

### Mootness
🟡

**Rule.** A case becomes moot when the controversy ceases to be live — no longer any
real dispute that a court can resolve. Exceptions preserve jurisdiction when: (1) the
issue is **capable of repetition yet evading review** (short duration relative to
litigation cycle, and the same plaintiff is likely to face it again); (2) the defendant
**voluntarily ceased** the challenged conduct but retains the ability to resume it; or
(3) a **class action** where the named plaintiff's claim becomes moot but the class
claim remains live.

**Here.** Here, [the injunction was lifted before the appeal / the challenged election
has passed], but the issue [is capable of repetition / was voluntarily ceased].

**Thus.** Thus, the case is [moot, with no applicable exception / not moot because the
voluntary-cessation exception applies].

### Political Question Doctrine
🟡

**Rule.** A political question is a constitutional issue that a court will not adjudicate
because the Constitution commits it to another branch, or there are no judicially
manageable standards for its resolution. Classic political questions include challenges
to the conduct of foreign affairs, the impeachment process, political gerrymandering
(per *Rucho v. Common Cause*), and questions of whether a constitutional amendment was
properly ratified.

**Here.** Here, [plaintiff challenges Congress's refusal to seat a member / the
President's termination of a treaty], which is committed to [Congress/the Executive] and
presents no manageable judicial standard.

**Thus.** Thus, the court must dismiss the claim as a nonjusticiable political question.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) held partisan gerrymandering claims are
> non-justiciable in federal courts; racial gerrymandering remains justiciable.

### Advisory Opinions
🟢

**Rule.** Article III courts may not issue advisory opinions — rulings on abstract
questions of law disconnected from a concrete dispute between adverse parties seeking
specific relief. An advisory opinion lacks the concrete adverseness needed to sharpen
the issues and lacks the finality that gives judicial decisions precedential force.

**Here.** Here, [the state executive asked the federal court for guidance on whether a
proposed regulation would be constitutional before enacting it], which presents no
adverse parties and no ripe controversy.

**Thus.** Thus, the court may not render an opinion, as doing so would be advisory.

### 11th Amendment / Sovereign Immunity
🟢

**Rule.** The Eleventh Amendment bars suits against a state in federal court brought by
private parties — whether the plaintiff is a citizen of that state or another state.
Three main exceptions exist: (1) Congress abrogates state immunity pursuant to a valid
exercise of §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment (not under the Commerce Clause alone);
(2) the state itself consents to suit; and (3) *Ex parte Young* — a private party may
sue a **state officer** in federal court for **prospective injunctive relief** to end an
ongoing violation of federal law (the suit is treated as not being against the state).
The doctrine does not bar suits against local governments (counties, cities).

**Here.** Here, [plaintiff sues the State of X for money damages, not a state officer
for prospective relief], and [Congress abrogated immunity under §5 / the state has not
consented], so the 11th Amendment [bars / does not bar] this suit.

**Thus.** Thus, the action [must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction / may proceed
under *Ex parte Young*].

### State Action
🟢

**Rule.** Constitutional limitations on governmental power apply only to **governmental
actors**, not private parties. State action exists when a private entity exercises
powers traditionally and exclusively reserved to the government (the **public function
doctrine**), or when the government is so entangled in or has sufficiently encouraged
the private conduct that the private actor's actions are fairly attributable to the state
(the **nexus/entanglement** doctrine). Mere government licensing or regulation of a
private entity does not alone constitute state action.

**Here.** Here, [the company operated the only town in the area / the government directed
the private hospital's exclusion policy], so its conduct [does/does not] constitute state
action.

**Thus.** Thus, constitutional scrutiny [applies/does not apply] to [the private entity's]
conduct.

### Commerce Clause — Congressional Power
🟢  ·  *most-tested federal power on the exam*

**Rule.** Congress may regulate under the Commerce Clause in three categories: (1)
**channels** of interstate commerce (roads, airways, waterways); (2)
**instrumentalities** of interstate commerce and persons or things in interstate commerce;
and (3) activities that, taken in the aggregate, **substantially affect** interstate
commerce. For economic activity, the aggregation principle permits Congress to regulate
even purely local instances when the class of activity has a substantial economic effect
nationally. Congress may not, however, use the Commerce Clause to regulate inactivity
(the individual mandate logic in *NFIB v. Sebelius*).

**Here.** Here, [the law regulates the interstate shipment of X / a local activity that,
in the aggregate, has a substantial effect on the national market for X], so Congress
[has/lacks] Commerce Clause power.

**Thus.** Thus, the statute [is/is not] a valid exercise of the commerce power.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: *Lopez* and *Morrison* limits (non-economic activity, no jurisdictional
> element, no substantial-effects showing) remain good law; confirm no further narrowing.

### Taxing and Spending Power
🟡

**Rule.** Congress may lay and collect taxes and spend for the general welfare of the
United States. The power is broad, but a regulatory "penalty" will be analyzed as a tax
if it operates like one — raising revenue, applying to all persons, and imposing no
criminal sanction. Conditions attached to federal spending to states must be: (1) in
pursuit of the general welfare; (2) unambiguous so states can knowingly choose; (3)
germane to the federal program; (4) not unconstitutional independently; and (5) not
so coercive as to leave states no real choice — beyond the "point of a gun."

**Here.** Here, [Congress conditioned Medicaid expansion funding on states' full
compliance], and [the threatened loss of all Medicaid funding / 10% of state budget]
is [coercive/acceptable].

**Thus.** Thus, the spending condition [is/is not] a valid exercise of the spending power.

### Necessary and Proper Clause
🟡

**Rule.** The Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress the power to enact any law
that is a rational, plainly adapted means to execute an enumerated power. It is not an
independent source of legislative authority — it must be used in conjunction with another
enumerated power. "Necessary" means useful or conducive, not indispensable; "proper"
imposes a structural limit that the means must be consistent with the letter and spirit
of the Constitution.

**Here.** Here, [the statute lacks a direct Commerce Clause basis], but it [is reasonably
adapted to the regulation of interstate banking], making it [a valid / not a valid] use
of Necessary and Proper in conjunction with [the commerce power].

**Thus.** Thus, the law [is/is not] within Congress's Necessary and Proper authority.

### 10th Amendment / Anti-Commandeering
🟢

**Rule.** The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not delegated to the federal government to
the states or the people. It prohibits the federal government from **commandeering**
state legislatures to enact federal regulatory programs (*New York v. United States*) or
from directing state executive officers to administer federal law (*Printz v. United
States*). Congress may, however, **preempt** state law (rendering it void) and may
induce state cooperation through the spending power. The commandeering prohibition does
not prevent Congress from placing conditions on states that choose to participate in
federal programs.

**Here.** Here, [the federal statute directly ordered state sheriffs to conduct background
checks], which conscripts state executive officers into federal service without their
consent.

**Thus.** Thus, the statute violates the anti-commandeering principle and is
unconstitutional.

---

### §5 Fourteenth Amendment Enforcement Power
🟡

**Rule.** Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment grants Congress the power to enforce
the Amendment's substantive guarantees by appropriate legislation. Congressional
legislation under §5 must be **congruent and proportional** to the constitutional
violation it seeks to remedy — there must be a documented pattern of 14th Amendment
violations by states, and the legislative remedy must be tailored to redress that
pattern. Congress cannot use §5 to create new substantive rights or expand existing
ones beyond those recognized by the courts.

**Here.** Here, Congress invoked §5 to abrogate state sovereign immunity, and [the
legislative record does/does not demonstrate] a pattern of state constitutional
violations that the statute's sweep proportionally addresses.

**Thus.** Thus, the statute [is/is not] a valid exercise of §5 enforcement power.

### Separation of Powers — Presidential Action (Youngstown)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Executive privilege, appointment/removal*

**Rule.** Presidential action is analyzed under the framework of *Youngstown Sheet &
Tube Co. v. Sawyer*. Justice Jackson's concurrence identifies three zones: (1) when the
President acts **pursuant to congressional authorization**, he has maximum power —
the express or implied will of Congress added to his own; (2) when the President acts
in a **congressional vacuum** (silence or ambiguous delegation), he relies on his own
independent constitutional powers and the outcome is uncertain ("twilight zone"); (3)
when the President acts **contrary to the will of Congress**, he is at the "lowest ebb"
of his authority and can act only on inherent constitutional powers.

**Here.** Here, the President's order [seized steel mills / imposed tariffs] without
statutory authority, and Congress had implicitly refused to grant that power, placing the
action in [category three / the twilight zone].

**Thus.** Thus, the executive action [is/is not] within the President's constitutional
authority.

### Appointment and Removal Power
🟡

**Rule.** The Appointments Clause (Art. II, §2) requires that **principal officers** —
those with significant authority exercised pursuant to federal law — be appointed by the
President with Senate confirmation. **Inferior officers** — those supervised by and
removable by a principal officer — may, by statute, be appointed by the President alone,
courts of law, or department heads. Congress may not itself appoint an officer who
exercises executive power. The President generally has an implied power to remove
executive officers, but Congress may restrict removal of heads of independent agencies
to "for-cause" dismissal; Congress may not impose multi-layer for-cause removal
protections that insulate officers from presidential oversight (*Seila Law*).

**Here.** Here, [the agency director was appointed without Senate confirmation / the
removal restriction allows dismissal only for cause], and [the director exercises
significant authority / two layers of for-cause protection exist].

**Thus.** Thus, the appointment [violates / comports with] the Appointments Clause, and
the removal restriction [is/is not] constitutional.

### Legislative Veto
🟡

**Rule.** A legislative veto — a mechanism by which one or both houses of Congress
override executive action without presenting a bill to the President — is unconstitutional.
Every exercise of legislative power requires passage by both Houses (bicameralism) and
presentment to the President for signature or veto. Any congressional action that has
the purpose and effect of altering legal rights and duties outside this process violates
the Constitution's law-making design (*INS v. Chadha*).

**Here.** Here, [a one-house resolution purported to invalidate the executive agency
regulation], which is an exercise of legislative power outside bicameralism and
presentment.

**Thus.** Thus, the legislative veto is unconstitutional.

### Non-Delegation
🟡

**Rule.** Congress may not delegate its legislative power without providing an
**intelligible principle** to guide and constrain the delegatee's exercise of that power.
The intelligible principle standard is permissive — broad standards such as "public
interest, convenience, and necessity" have survived. A delegation with no discernible
limiting principle would be invalid, but the Court has not struck down a statute on
non-delegation grounds since 1935.

**Here.** Here, the statute directed [the agency] to set prices [as it sees fit / in the
public interest], which [provides / fails to provide] an intelligible guiding principle.

**Thus.** Thus, the delegation [survives / violates] the non-delegation doctrine.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: *West Virginia v. EPA* (2022) introduced the "major questions doctrine"
> (agencies need clear congressional authorization for major economic/political questions)
> as a separate canon of statutory construction that overlaps with non-delegation
> concerns — flag both where relevant.

### Presidential Immunity
🟡

**Rule.** A President has **absolute immunity** from criminal prosecution for actions
taken within his core constitutional powers (e.g., commanding the military, pardoning
offenders, removing officers). For other official acts, there is a **presumptive
immunity** that may be overcome by a showing that prosecution would not unduly impair
the President's exercise of executive functions. For **unofficial acts** outside the
scope of presidential duties, there is no immunity. Courts must determine whether an
act is official or unofficial by examining whether it falls within the outer perimeter
of the President's official responsibilities (*Trump v. United States*, 2024).

**Here.** Here, [the indictment alleged that the President pressured the Vice President
to reject certified votes], which [falls within / outside] the President's core
constitutional authority, placing it in the [absolute / presumptive / no-immunity]
category.

**Thus.** Thus, the President [does/does not] enjoy immunity from prosecution for that
conduct.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: *Trump v. United States* (2024) is newly decided; the full contours of
> official vs. unofficial act analysis are still developing through lower-court
> application — treat the rule statement carefully on the exam.

### Supremacy Clause — Preemption
🟢

**Rule.** Under the Supremacy Clause (Art. VI), federal law is supreme and state laws
that conflict are void. Preemption may be: (1) **express** — Congress explicitly states
that federal law displaces state law; (2) **field** — the scope and density of federal

### Dormant Commerce Clause — Discriminatory Laws
🟢

**Rule.** Even absent federal legislation, the Commerce Clause implicitly restricts state

discriminates** against out-of-state economic interests — or is discriminatory in purpose

### Dormant Commerce Clause — Undue Burden (Pike Balancing)
🟡

**Rule.** A nondiscriminatory state regulation that incidentally burdens interstate

magnitude of the burden against the strength of the state interest and the directness of

### Market-Participant Exception
🟡

**Rule.** The Dormant Commerce Clause applies only to a state acting in its regulatory

DCC. The exception does not extend to downstream conditions imposed on buyers of
state-produced goods once they leave the state's direct commercial relationship.

### Levels of Scrutiny — Rational Basis
🟢

**Rule.** Under rational basis review — the default standard for economic and social
legislation, and for laws that burden no suspect class and no fundamental right — a law
is constitutional if it is **rationally related** to a **legitimate** government interest.
The challenger bears the burden; any conceivable basis for the law suffices even if not
stated by the legislature. Laws rarely fail this test, though the Court sometimes applies
a "bite" to laws animated by bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group (*Romer
v. Evans*).

### Levels of Scrutiny — Intermediate Scrutiny
🟢

**Rule.** Intermediate scrutiny applies to laws that classify on the basis of **sex** or
**legitimacy** (also to content-neutral speech restrictions and certain other contexts).
The government must show the law is **substantially related** to an **important**
government interest. The government bears the burden, and the means must be more than
rationally related — the connection must be real and substantial, not merely hypothetical.

### Levels of Scrutiny — Strict Scrutiny
🟢

**Rule.** Strict scrutiny applies when the government classifies by a **suspect class**
(race, national origin, alienage — with exceptions for alienage by states) or burdens a
**fundamental right**. The government must show the law is **necessary** to achieve a
**compelling** government interest, and narrowly tailored — no less restrictive alternative
achieves the same end. The government bears the burden. The law almost always fails.

and effect], and the government [cannot demonstrate a compelling interest free of

### Procedural Due Process
🟢

**Rule.** The Fifth (federal) and Fourteenth (state) Amendments bar government
deprivation of **life, liberty, or property** without due process of law. The analysis
has two steps: (1) identify whether a protected interest exists — property interests
arise from a legitimate claim of entitlement under state or federal law; liberty interests
include freedom from physical restraint and certain reputational/status interests; and
(2) determine what process is due using the *Mathews v. Eldridge* balancing test:
(a) the private interest affected; (b) the risk of erroneous deprivation and the probable
value of additional safeguards; and (c) the government's interest, including fiscal and
administrative costs. At minimum, due process requires **notice** and an **opportunity
to be heard** at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner.

employment under a statutory tenure system], and [the pretermination hearing provided /

### Substantive Due Process — Fundamental Rights
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Equal Protection (fundamental-rights EP)*

**Rule.** Under substantive due process, the government may not infringe a **fundamental

the concept of ordered liberty — without satisfying strict scrutiny. Recognized
fundamental rights include: marriage (*Loving*, *Obergefell*); procreation; contraception;
family relations and child-rearing; interstate travel; and voting. Rights not recognized
as fundamental receive only rational basis review. A right must be described with
specificity and careful attention to historical tradition.

### Equal Protection — Overview and Discriminatory Purpose
🟢

### Takings — Overview and Physical Takings
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Contracts Clause, Property (1L)*

**Rule.** The Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment (incorporated against the states via
the Fourteenth Amendment) provides that private property shall not be taken for public
use without just compensation. A **physical taking** occurs when the government authorizes
a permanent physical occupation of property — this is **per se** a taking requiring
compensation regardless of the public benefit or economic impact (*Loretto*). A
temporary physical invasion may be a taking depending on the circumstances.

**Here.** Here, [the city required landlords to permit cable TV installation], which
authorized a permanent physical occupation of the landlord's property.

**Thus.** Thus, a per se taking occurred and just compensation is required.

### Takings — Per Se Regulatory Taking (Lucas)
🟡

**Rule.** A **regulatory** taking occurs when government regulation goes too far in
restricting property use without actually appropriating it. A regulation that denies the
owner **all economically beneficial use** of the property is a **per se** regulatory
taking — the government must compensate unless the restricted use would have been
prohibited under background principles of state property or nuisance law (*Lucas v. South
Carolina Coastal Council*).

**Here.** Here, [the coastal development ban rendered plaintiff's beachfront lots
economically valueless], and [no background state-law principle would have barred the
intended use], leaving no basis to deny compensation.

**Thus.** Thus, a per se regulatory taking has occurred.

### Takings — Penn Central Balancing
🟡

**Rule.** When a regulation does not deprive the owner of all economic value, courts
apply the *Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City* balancing test, weighing:
(1) the **economic impact** of the regulation on the claimant; (2) the extent of
**interference with reasonable investment-backed expectations**; and (3) the **character
of the government action** — physical appropriation is more likely a taking than a broad
health or safety regulation that incidentally reduces value.

**Here.** Here, [the landmark preservation ordinance prevented the landowner from
constructing a higher-value building], and [the economic impact is severe / moderate],
[investment-backed expectations were significantly impaired / reasonable given existing
regulation], and [the character is regulatory, not physical].

**Thus.** Thus, the regulation [does/does not] constitute a taking under Penn Central.

### Takings — Public Use and Just Compensation
🟡

**Rule.** A taking is constitutional only if it is for **public use**. Courts interpret
public use broadly — any conceivable public benefit satisfies the requirement, including
transfer to a private party as part of an economic development plan, provided there is a
public benefit (*Kelo v. City of New London*). **Just compensation** is the **fair market
value** of the property at the time of the taking, not the value to the owner or the
government's gain.

[economic revitalization], which serves a public benefit under *Kelo*; compensation is

### First Amendment — Content-Based vs. Content-Neutral
🟢  ·  *foundation of the entire speech analysis*

**Rule.** The threshold question in any speech case is whether the regulation is
**content-based** (targeting speech because of its message, viewpoint, or subject matter)
or **content-neutral** (targeting speech regardless of message, for reasons unrelated to
communicative impact). Content-based restrictions are presumptively unconstitutional and
survive only if the government proves they are necessary to serve a compelling interest
and narrowly tailored — strict scrutiny. Content-neutral restrictions are subject to
intermediate scrutiny: the government must show the law is narrowly tailored to serve a
significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of
communication. **Viewpoint discrimination** — favoring one perspective over another —
is per se unconstitutional even in a nonpublic forum.

**Here.** Here, [the ordinance prohibited only anti-abortion protest signs in front of
clinics], targeting speech based on its message and viewpoint.

**Thus.** Thus, the regulation is content-based and subject to strict scrutiny.

### Prior Restraints
🟢

**Rule.** A **prior restraint** is a judicial or administrative order that prevents speech
from occurring before it happens. Prior restraints carry a **heavy presumption of
unconstitutionality** — the most serious free-speech burden. To be upheld, a prior
restraint must satisfy strict scrutiny, and any prior-restraint regime must provide
**procedural safeguards**: prompt notice and hearing, narrow scope, definite time limits,
and prompt judicial review. The government bears the burden to justify the restraint.

**Here.** Here, [the court enjoined publication of the story before it was printed],

### Vagueness and Overbreadth
🟡

**Rule.** A law is **void for vagueness** if it fails to give persons of ordinary
intelligence fair notice of what conduct is prohibited, or if it delegates so much
discretion to enforcers that it permits arbitrary enforcement. A law is **overbroad** if
it prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech, even though it might be validly
applied to some unprotected speech. An overbroad law is facially invalid, and a litigant
— even one whose own conduct could be regulated — has standing to challenge overbreadth
because the law may deter third parties from engaging in protected expression.

**Here.** Here, [the ordinance prohibited "offensive" language in public], which fails to
define what qualifies and sweeps in a substantial range of protected expression.

**Thus.** Thus, the ordinance is [vague/overbroad] and facially unconstitutional.

### Unprotected Speech — Incitement
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: True Threats, Fighting Words*

**Rule.** Speech that incites imminent lawless action is outside First Amendment
protection. Under *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, incitement requires: (1) the speech was
**directed to producing** imminent lawless action; and (2) the speech is **likely to
produce** such action. Mere advocacy of illegal action in the abstract — even in
inflammatory terms — is protected; only speech directed to and likely to produce
*imminent* unlawfulness loses protection.

**Here.** Here, [the speaker told an angry crowd to attack the building immediately],
directing imminent unlawful action to a crowd positioned and prepared to act.

**Thus.** Thus, the speech falls outside First Amendment protection as incitement.

### Unprotected Speech — Obscenity
🟡

**Rule.** Obscene material is not protected by the First Amendment. Under the **Miller
test**, material is obscene if: (1) the **average person**, applying **contemporary
community standards**, would find the work appeals to the prurient interest in sex;
(2) the work depicts or describes sexual conduct in a **patently offensive** way as
defined by applicable law; and (3) the work lacks **serious literary, artistic, political,
or scientific value (SLAPS)** — measured by a national, not community, standard. Child
pornography is categorically unprotected and need not meet the Miller test.

**Here.** Here, [the video depicting graphic sexual acts] appears to meet prongs (1) and
(2) under local community standards, but [plaintiff argues it has serious artistic value]
that would defeat prong (3).

**Thus.** Thus, the material is [obscene/protected] depending on the SLAPS value finding.

### Unprotected Speech — True Threats and Fighting Words
🟡

**Rule.** A **true threat** is a serious statement expressing intent to commit unlawful
violence against a reasonably identifiable person or group; the speaker need not intend
to carry it out. The Supreme Court has clarified that true threats require at least
**subjective awareness** that the statement could be perceived as a threat (*Counterman
v. Colorado*, 2023). **Fighting words** are words that, by their very utterance, inflict
injury or tend to provoke an immediate breach of the peace from the person directly
addressed; the category is narrow and rarely successfully invoked.

**Here.** Here, [defendant repeatedly sent messages describing violent acts toward the

🟢

**Rule.** The government may regulate the **time, place, and manner** of speech even in

### Public Forum Doctrine
🟡

**Rule.** The permissibility of speech regulations depends on the forum. In a
**traditional public forum** (streets, parks, sidewalks historically open to speech),
the government may impose only content-neutral time/place/manner restrictions; any
content-based restriction must survive strict scrutiny. In a **designated public forum**
(a space the government has opened for public expression), the same rules apply while
the forum is open. In a **nonpublic forum** (government property not opened for general
expression — military bases, prisons, airports), restrictions are valid if **reasonable
and viewpoint-neutral**. Limited public forums (opened only for certain speakers or
topics) may restrict consistent with the limitation so long as restrictions are viewpoint-
neutral.

**Here.** Here, [the school limited the meeting room to civic organizations but denied
access to a religious club], and the denial [was viewpoint-based], violating even
nonpublic-forum standards.

**Thus.** Thus, the exclusion is unconstitutional as viewpoint discrimination.

🟡

**Rule.** Commercial speech — expression that proposes a commercial transaction — receives

Public Service Commission*, commercial speech regulations are upheld if: (1) the speech
concerns a **lawful activity** and is **not misleading**; (2) the government interest is
**substantial**; (3) the regulation **directly advances** that interest; and (4) the
regulation is **not more extensive than necessary**. Fully false or misleading commercial
speech is unprotected.

prongs].

### Symbolic Speech / Expressive Conduct (O'Brien)
🟡

**Rule.** Conduct with a **communicative intent** and a likelihood that the audience will
understand the message as expressive receives First Amendment protection. A
content-neutral regulation incidentally restricting expressive conduct is upheld under
the *United States v. O'Brien* test if: (1) the regulation is **within the government's
constitutional power**; (2) it furthers an **important or substantial government
interest**; (3) the government interest is **unrelated to the suppression of free
expression**; and (4) the incidental restriction on expression is **no greater than
essential** to furtherance of that interest.

**Here.** Here, [the law criminalizing flag burning was aimed at protecting the flag as

**Thus.** Thus, the flag-burning law fails *O'Brien* and is unconstitutional.

### Free Exercise Clause
🟢

**Rule.** The Free Exercise Clause protects the right to hold any religious beliefs and,
to a significant extent, religious practices. A **neutral law of general applicability**
— one not targeting religious practice — need only satisfy rational basis, even if it
incidentally burdens religious practice (*Employment Division v. Smith*). A law that is
not neutral or not generally applicable — such as one that is gerrymandered to target
religious conduct while exempting comparable secular conduct — triggers **strict
scrutiny** (*Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah*; *Fulton v. City of
Philadelphia*). A law that grants secular exemptions must extend comparable exemptions
to religious conduct.

**Here.** Here, [the city ordinance prohibited animal slaughter but exempted licensed
slaughterhouses while targeting religious ritual sacrifice], which is neither neutral nor
generally applicable.

**Thus.** Thus, strict scrutiny applies, and the ordinance must be justified by a
compelling interest and narrowly tailored — [which it cannot be given the
secular exemptions].

### Establishment Clause
🟢

**Rule.** The Establishment Clause prohibits the government from establishing a religion,
coercing participation in religious activity, or excessively entangling itself with
religious institutions. The traditional *Lemon* test (secular purpose, neither advancing
nor inhibiting religion, no excessive entanglement) has been substantially displaced.
The Supreme Court now emphasizes a **history and tradition** analysis and the
**coercion** of religious participation as the core prohibitions (*Kennedy v. Bremerton
School District*, 2022). Government acknowledgment of religion with deep historical
roots is less likely to violate the Clause than targeted coercion of religious practice.

**Here.** Here, [the public school teacher led organized prayer in class], which involves
government actors promoting religious exercise in a context where students face coercive
pressure to participate.

**Thus.** Thus, the practice violates the Establishment Clause under both coercion

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:** Con Law is the most internally cross-referential subject on the
  exam. Standing + political question often appear together. Commerce Clause power
  crosses with 10th Amendment anti-commandeering and preemption in the same fact
  pattern. Equal protection and substantive due process are almost always analyzed
  together when a law burdens a fundamental right ("same-sex marriage" questions invoke
  both). Takings crosses with procedural due process (property deprivation without
  hearing). First Amendment free speech and free exercise cross on religious speech
  issues. Con Law also crosses with Torts (constitutional torts, §1983) and
  Administrative Law (non-delegation, Chevron/major questions).

- **Sleeper issues:** (1) Forgetting to screen for justiciability before reaching the
  merits — examiners penalize this heavily. (2) Failing to distinguish 11th Amendment
  sovereign immunity from general standing. (3) Missing the taxpayer-standing *Flast*
  exception in Establishment Clause questions. (4) Confusing the Privileges and
  Immunities Clause of Article IV (anti-discrimination, applies to individuals) with the
  14th Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause (rarely litigated). (5) Applying the
  wrong level of scrutiny — especially using rational basis for sex discrimination or
  intermediate scrutiny for race. (6) Missing state action entirely in a fact pattern
  involving nominally private actors. (7) Conflating preemption with Contracts Clause
  challenges. (8) Forgetting *Ex parte Young* when sovereign immunity otherwise bars
  the suit.

- **CA-divergence cheat list:**
  1. **California Constitution has its own free speech clause** (Art. I, §2) that is
     *more protective* than the First Amendment and applies to some private actors —
     notably *Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center* (shopping center as forum); federal
     con law does not reach private actors.
  2. **California's equal protection clause** (Art. I, §7) applies on its face to state
     action; CA courts may apply heightened scrutiny under the CA Constitution
     independently of federal doctrine.
  3. **California Prop 47, Prop 8, and ballot initiative processes** have generated
     unique state constitutional questions; distinguish federal Equal Protection analysis
     from state process.
  4. **California Privacy Right** — Art. I, §1 of the CA Constitution explicitly
     protects a right to privacy; no equivalent express federal constitutional provision.
  5. **Establishment Clause — CA schools:** California's no-aid clause (Art. XVI, §5)
     is stricter than the First Amendment on public funding to religious schools; may
     come up if a question bridges school funding and religion.
  6. **State sovereign immunity in CA state courts:** California has its own sovereign
     immunity doctrine under the Government Claims Act; different from 11th Amendment
     analysis in federal court — note both if the question asks about suing California.

- **Call-of-the-question patterns:**
  - "Does the court have power to hear this case?" → Run the full justiciability checklist:
    standing (all three prongs), ripeness, mootness, political question, advisory
    opinions, sovereign immunity.
  - "Is the federal [or state] law constitutional?" → Identify the governmental actor →
    identify the power claimed → identify the individual right burdened → select the
    correct scrutiny level.
  - "Does [state law] violate the Constitution?" → Think: preemption/Supremacy first,
    then Dormant Commerce Clause, then individual rights if the law also burdens persons.
  - "What are [plaintiff]'s constitutional claims?" → Map each potential constitutional
    violation to an IRAC block; do not combine distinct doctrines into a single analysis.
  - "Discuss the constitutional issues" → Broad call; prioritize justiciability → power
    → scrutiny; watch for state vs. federal actor.
