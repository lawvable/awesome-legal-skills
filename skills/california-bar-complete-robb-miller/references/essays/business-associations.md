# Business Associations — Essay Bank

> All rule language is original. CA divergences are flagged throughout. Flag uncertain
> or recently-changed law with `> ⚠️ VERIFY`.

**High-frequency note:** Business Associations essays almost always anchor on a
**corporations** question — duty of care (business judgment rule), duty of loyalty
(self-dealing or usurped opportunity), shareholder derivative suits, or piercing the
corporate veil. Agency is the perennial companion issue because corporate officers and
partners act as agents. The CA Corporations Code diverges from the MBCA/Delaware model
in several high-yield ways: security-for-expenses in derivative suits, the approach to
fundamental corporate changes, and close-corporation formalities. Expect at least one
CA-specific wrinkle to be the hidden key to the essay.

## Part 2 — IRAC Rule Blocks (full, memorizable)

---

### Agency — Formation

🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Contracts (third-party liability), Torts (respondeat superior)*

**Rule.** An agency relationship arises when (1) the principal manifests consent that
the agent shall act on the principal's behalf, (2) the agent consents to so act, and (3)
the agent is subject to the principal's control. No consideration or formal agreement is
required; the relationship may arise from conduct alone. The agent's actual authority
flows directly from the principal's manifestation to the agent, while the agent's
apparent authority flows from the principal's manifestation to third parties.

**Here.** Here, [principal] directed [agent] to [act], and [agent] agreed, creating an
agency relationship such that [agent]'s subsequent acts may bind [principal].

**Thus.** Thus, an agency relationship [was/was not] formed.

---

### Actual Authority — Express

🟢

**Rule.** An agent has express actual authority when the principal explicitly grants it,
either orally or in writing. The scope of express authority is determined by the
reasonable meaning of the principal's words to the agent. Express authority is
interpreted narrowly when in doubt — ambiguities run against expansion.

**Here.** Here, [principal]'s [instruction/letter/email] expressly authorized [agent] to
[take the specific act], so [agent] acted within express actual authority.

**Thus.** Thus, [principal] is bound by [agent]'s act under express actual authority.

---

### Actual Authority — Implied

🟢

**Rule.** Implied actual authority exists for acts reasonably necessary to carry out the
agent's express authority, or that are customary in similar transactions, or that the
agent reasonably believes the principal would want based on prior dealings and the
circumstances. It is inferred from the express grant, the nature of the task, or the
parties' course of conduct.

**Here.** Here, although [principal] did not expressly authorize [specific act], it was
reasonably necessary to accomplish [authorized task], giving [agent] implied authority.

**Thus.** Thus, [principal] is bound because the act fell within [agent]'s implied actual
authority.

---

### Apparent Authority

🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Contracts (unauthorized agent contracts)*

**Rule.** Apparent authority arises when (1) the principal's words or conduct, reasonably
interpreted, lead a third party to believe the agent has authority, and (2) the third
party justifiably relies on that belief. Apparent authority must be traced to the
principal — it cannot be created solely by the agent's own representations. The
principal is bound to the third party even if the agent exceeded actual authority.

**Here.** Here, [principal] [appointed agent to a role / sent agent to negotiate / allowed
agent to act openly], creating a reasonable belief in [third party] that [agent] was
authorized, and [third party] relied on that belief.

**Thus.** Thus, [principal] is bound by [agent]'s contract/act under apparent authority.

---

### Ratification

🟡

**Rule.** A principal may ratify an agent's unauthorized act by (1) affirming the act
with full knowledge of the material facts, either expressly or by conduct (such as
accepting the benefits of the transaction), and (2) having capacity to authorize the act
at the time of both the original act and the ratification. Ratification relates back to
the original transaction and binds the principal as though authority had existed from
the start.

**Here.** Here, [principal] [accepted payment / retained the goods / failed to repudiate
with knowledge], which constitutes ratification by conduct.

**Thus.** Thus, [principal] is bound as if [agent] had been authorized from the outset.

---

### Agent's Fiduciary Duties

🟡

**Rule.** An agent owes the principal a duty of loyalty (acting solely for the principal's
benefit in matters within the agency, not competing, not secretly profiting), a duty of
obedience (following reasonable instructions), a duty of care (acting with reasonable
diligence), and a duty to account (maintaining and remitting money or property received).
An agent who secretly profits, self-deals, or acts for a competitor breaches the duty of
loyalty and must disgorge profits.

**Here.** Here, [agent] [solicited kickbacks / diverted an opportunity / secretly worked
for a competitor] while acting as [principal]'s agent, breaching the duty of loyalty.

**Thus.** Thus, [principal] may demand an accounting and disgorgement of any secret profits.

---

### Respondeat Superior / Principal Liability for Torts

🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Torts (vicarious liability)*

**Rule.** An employer (principal) is vicariously liable for torts committed by an
employee (agent) acting within the scope of employment. An act is within scope if it
is the kind the employee is hired to perform, occurs substantially within authorized
time and space, and is motivated at least in part by serving the employer. A substantial
departure from the authorized route or purpose — a "frolic" — removes the act from scope;
a minor deviation — a "detour" — does not. An employer is generally not liable for the
torts of an independent contractor, but remains liable if the work is inherently dangerous
or the employer exercises substantial control over the method of work.

**Here.** Here, [employee], while [performing job duty / using company vehicle / on
employer's errand], [committed tort], which occurred within the scope of employment
because [connection to job].

**Thus.** Thus, [employer] is vicariously liable to [injured party].

> **CA divergence:** California applies the same respondeat superior rule but has
> expansive "scope of employment" caselaw and recognizes the **going-and-coming rule**
> (commuting generally not within scope) with a broad **special errand exception** that
> courts apply liberally. CA also imposes liability on employers for sexual harassment
> by supervisors under FEHA regardless of the scope analysis.

---

### Partnership — Formation

🟢

**Rule.** A general partnership is formed when two or more persons — including individuals,
corporations, and other entities — associate to carry on a business as co-owners for
profit. Under the Revised Uniform Partnership Act (RUPA), no written agreement, filing,
or formalities are required; the partnership arises by agreement, which may be inferred
from conduct. Sharing profits is prima facie evidence of a partnership; sharing gross
revenues alone or receiving wages is not.

**Here.** Here, [A] and [B] [agreed to split profits / jointly managed the business /
held themselves out as co-owners], which is prima facie evidence of a partnership
regardless of the absence of a written agreement.

**Thus.** Thus, a general partnership [was/was not] formed.

> **CA divergence:** California has enacted the California Revised Uniform Partnership
> Act (Corp. Code §§16100 et seq.), which tracks RUPA with minor differences. Partnership
> formation analysis is functionally the same.

---

### Partnership by Estoppel

🟡

**Rule.** A person who is not a partner but who represents themselves as one — or who
consents to being so represented — to a third party who then extends credit in reliance
is liable to that third party as though they were a partner. The actual partnership is
not bound unless all partners consented to the representation.

**Here.** Here, [non-partner] allowed [firm name] to list them as a partner or did not
correct the misrepresentation, and [creditor] extended credit in reliance.

**Thus.** Thus, [non-partner] is liable to [creditor] by estoppel.

---

### Partnership Property

🟡

**Rule.** Property belongs to the partnership (not to individual partners) if it was
acquired in the partnership's name, with partnership funds, or for partnership purposes,
or if the circumstances indicate partnership ownership. A partner's interest in the
partnership is personal property — it is the right to receive profits and distributions,
not an ownership stake in specific partnership assets.

**Here.** Here, the [real estate/equipment] was purchased with partnership funds and
titled in the partnership's name, making it partnership property, not [partner A]'s
individual asset.

**Thus.** Thus, the property belongs to the partnership, and individual partners' creditors
cannot attach it directly.

---

### Partner Fiduciary Duties — Loyalty & Care

🟢

**Rule.** Each general partner owes the partnership a duty of loyalty, which requires
the partner to (1) account for and hold as trustee any property, profit, or benefit
derived from the partnership business or from use of partnership property, (2) refrain
from dealing with the partnership on behalf of an adverse party, and (3) refrain from
competing with the partnership in the same or related business before dissolution. Each
partner also owes a duty of care, which RUPA defines as refraining from engaging in
grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct, or knowing violation of
law. Mere negligence does not breach the duty of care.

**Here.** Here, [partner] [diverted a business opportunity / entered a competing venture /
used partnership funds for personal gain], breaching the duty of [loyalty/care] owed to
the partnership and co-partners.

**Thus.** Thus, [partner] must account to the partnership for any profits earned and may
be liable for damages.

---

### Partner Liability to Third Parties

🟢

**Rule.** Under RUPA, partners are jointly and severally liable for all obligations of
the partnership — both contract debts and tort judgments — regardless of fault or
whether the partner was involved in the transaction. A creditor must generally exhaust
partnership assets before pursuing individual partners in most RUPA jurisdictions, but
the partners' personal assets are ultimately reachable.

**Here.** Here, [partner A]'s [contract/tort] created a partnership obligation, and all
general partners — including [partner B], who had no involvement — are personally liable
for the entire amount.

**Thus.** Thus, [third party] may recover the full judgment from any partner.

> **CA divergence:** Under the California Revised Uniform Partnership Act, a judgment
> creditor may proceed against a partner's personal assets only after first obtaining a
> judgment against the partnership itself and the partnership's assets are exhausted or
> enforcement would be inequitable (Corp. Code §16307). This "exhaustion first" rule is
> more protective of individual partners than many other RUPA jurisdictions.

---

### Partnership Profit and Loss Sharing

🟡

**Rule.** In the absence of a contrary agreement, RUPA provides that each partner shares
equally in the profits and each partner is charged equally with the losses, regardless
of the relative size of capital contributions. The default rule is equal sharing, not
pro-rata by investment. Partners may vary this rule by agreement, including in a written
partnership agreement.

**Here.** Here, [partner A] contributed $100,000 and [partner B] contributed $10,000,
but absent a contrary agreement, each receives an equal share of profits and bears equal
losses.

**Thus.** Thus, the default is equal sharing, not sharing proportionate to capital.

---

### Partnership Dissociation & Dissolution

🟡

**Rule.** Under RUPA, a partner may **dissociate** from the partnership by rightful
withdrawal, expulsion, bankruptcy, death, or incapacity. Dissociation does not
automatically dissolve the partnership; if the remaining partners vote to continue the
business, the dissociating partner has a right to be **bought out** at the value of her
interest at the date of dissociation. **Dissolution** — winding up the partnership
business — occurs only upon specified events (e.g., unanimous agreement, expiration of
term, wrongful dissociation triggering dissolution vote, or judicial order). On
dissolution, partnership assets are liquidated and distributed: first to creditors
(including partner-creditors), then to partners for capital contributions, then equally
as profits.

**Here.** Here, [partner]'s [resignation/death] caused dissociation; the remaining
partners [continued / voted to wind up], triggering [buyout rights / dissolution and
liquidation].

**Thus.** Thus, [partner/estate] is entitled to [buyout value / share of liquidation
proceeds].

---

### Limited Partnership — Basics

🟡

**Rule.** A limited partnership (LP) is formed by filing a certificate of limited
partnership and must have at least one general partner and one limited partner. General
partners manage the partnership and bear unlimited personal liability. Limited partners
contribute capital but, **if they do not participate in control**, their liability is
limited to their capital contribution. A limited partner who takes part in the control
of the business loses limited liability protection to third parties who reasonably
believed they were a general partner.

**Here.** Here, [LP partner] [made management decisions / executed contracts on behalf of
the LP], which constitutes participating in control, exposing [LP partner] to personal
liability to those [third parties who dealt with them as a manager].

**Thus.** Thus, [LP partner]'s limited liability [is preserved / is lost] as to [relevant
parties].

> **CA divergence:** California's limited partnership statute (Corp. Code §15900 et seq.,
> the CA Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act) provides a safe harbor list of permitted
> activities for limited partners that do not constitute "participating in control."
> Serving on an advisory board, voting on fundamental changes, or guaranteeing a debt
> generally do not strip limited-liability protection.

---

### Limited Liability Partnership — Basics

🟡

**Rule.** A limited liability partnership (LLP) is a general partnership that files an
LLP registration with the state. In an LLP, partners are not personally liable for the
wrongful acts, omissions, or negligence of other partners or employees they did not
supervise. Each partner remains liable for their own conduct and for debts arising from
their own activities. LLPs are commonly used by professional service firms.

**Here.** Here, [firm] is registered as an LLP, so [partner A] is not personally liable
for [partner B]'s malpractice in a separate matter, though [partner A] remains liable for
their own acts.

**Thus.** Thus, the LLP shield protects [partner A] from vicarious liability for
[partner B]'s conduct.

> **CA divergence:** California requires LLP registration (Corp. Code §16951) and
> restricts LLP status to licensed professionals such as lawyers, accountants, and
> architects. The shield is a "full shield" — partners are not liable for any partnership
> obligations solely by reason of being a partner.

### Corporate Formation — De Jure, De Facto, Corporation by Estoppel

🟢

**Rule.** A **de jure** corporation is validly formed when incorporators file articles of
incorporation with the state and comply with all statutory requirements; it then exists
as a separate legal entity. A **de facto** corporation exists when (1) a valid
incorporation statute exists, (2) the incorporators made a good-faith, colorable attempt
to comply with it, and (3) the business was exercised or carried on as a corporation.
**Corporation by estoppel** prevents a party who dealt with an entity as though it were
a corporation from later denying its corporate status to avoid obligations.

**Here.** Here, [organizers] [filed articles that were defective / failed to file at all],
and [conducted business under a corporate name], so the question is whether a de facto
corporation or estoppel applies.

**Thus.** Thus, the entity [is/is not] a de jure corporation but [may/may not] qualify as
de facto or be estopped from denying corporate status.

> **CA divergence:** California Corporations Code §200 requires filing articles with the
> Secretary of State for formation. California courts recognize corporation by estoppel
> but the de facto doctrine is rarely applied post-RMBCA; the CA trend is that only
> de jure formation provides full protection.

---

### Promoter Liability and Pre-Incorporation Contracts

🟡

**Rule.** A **promoter** is a person who organizes a corporation and acts on its behalf
before it is formed. A promoter is personally liable on contracts signed before
incorporation unless the contract expressly relieves the promoter of liability, or the
corporation, once formed, adopts the contract and a **novation** occurs — i.e., the third
party agrees to substitute the corporation for the promoter. Mere adoption of a contract
by the corporation does not automatically release the promoter. Until novation, both the
promoter and the corporation may be liable.

**Here.** Here, [promoter] signed a lease before the corporation was formed; the
corporation later adopted the lease but the third party [never expressly released promoter],
so no novation occurred and [promoter] remains liable.

**Thus.** Thus, [promoter] is personally liable on the pre-incorporation contract unless
a true novation is shown.

---

### Piercing the Corporate Veil — Alter Ego

🟢

**Rule.** Although shareholders generally are not liable for corporate debts, a court
may pierce the corporate veil and hold shareholders personally liable under the **alter
ego** doctrine when (1) there is such a unity of interest and ownership that the separate
personalities of the corporation and the shareholder no longer exist — shown by commingling
funds, failing to observe corporate formalities, undercapitalization, or using the
corporation as a personal instrumentality — and (2) adherence to the separate entity
fiction would sanction fraud, promote injustice, or cause an inequitable result.

**Here.** Here, [shareholder] [paid personal bills from corporate accounts / never held
board meetings / treated corporate assets as personal funds], demonstrating unity of
interest, and [creditor] would be left with an uncollectible judgment — an inequitable
result.

**Thus.** Thus, the court should pierce the corporate veil and hold [shareholder]
personally liable.

> **CA divergence:** California applies the two-pronged alter ego test (Corp. Code §§17703.04,
> 17703.05 for LLCs; case law for corps) and is willing to pierce in both the traditional
> (individual vs. corp) and **reverse pierce** (corp liable for owner's debts) directions.
> Courts look at: commingling of funds, failure to segregate funds of separate entities,
> failure to observe corporate formalities, failure to maintain arm's-length transactions,
> and use of the corporate form to avoid existing obligations.

---

### Piercing the Corporate Veil — Undercapitalization

🟡

**Rule.** A corporation that is so inadequately capitalized at the time of formation or
at the time it incurred an obligation that it had no reasonable prospect of meeting its
liabilities may have its veil pierced if enforcing the corporate form would work a
fraud or injustice on creditors. Undercapitalization alone may be insufficient; courts
generally require the additional element of inequity or an intent to defraud.

**Here.** Here, [corporation] was formed with minimal capital to conduct a high-risk
[construction/lending] business, with no reasonably proportionate assets to cover
foreseeable liabilities, and [creditor] was left with an uncollectible judgment.

**Thus.** Thus, undercapitalization combined with [fraud/injustice to creditor] may
support piercing.

---

### Directors' Duty of Care and the Business Judgment Rule

🟢  ·  *primary issue in most BA essays*

**Rule.** Directors owe the corporation a duty of care — to act in good faith with the
care that an ordinarily prudent person in a like position would exercise under similar
circumstances, and in a manner the director reasonably believes to be in the best
interests of the corporation. The **business judgment rule** creates a presumption that
directors who (1) act in good faith, (2) are reasonably informed, and (3) have no
personal financial interest in the transaction made a valid business decision. Courts
defer to that decision and will not substitute their judgment for the board's. The
presumption is rebutted by showing gross negligence, bad faith, fraud, or a disqualifying
personal interest.

**Here.** Here, the board [approved the acquisition / issued the stock] after [reviewing
reports / relying on expert advice / meeting and deliberating], so the business judgment
rule [protects / does not protect because gross negligence is shown by failure to
investigate at all].

**Thus.** Thus, the directors are [protected by the BJR / liable for breach of the duty
of care].

> **CA divergence:** California Corporations Code §309 codifies the duty of care and the
> BJR. A director may rely on information from officers, legal counsel, accountants, and
> board committees. CA allows corporations to include exculpation clauses in the articles
> limiting monetary liability for duty-of-care violations (but not for intentional
> misconduct, knowing violation of law, or an improper personal benefit — Corp. Code
> §204(a)(10)).

---

### Directors' Duty of Loyalty — Self-Dealing Transactions

🟢

**Rule.** A director has a conflict of interest when the director or a related party has
a material financial interest in a transaction with the corporation. Such a self-interested
transaction is voidable unless the director (1) fully discloses the material facts of
the interest, and (2) either the transaction is approved by a **majority of disinterested
directors** (after disclosure and without counting the interested director's vote), or
it is approved by a **majority of disinterested shareholders**, or a court finds the
transaction was **fair to the corporation** at the time of approval. All three pathways
lead to the same result: the transaction is cleaned up.

**Here.** Here, [director D] had a personal financial interest in the [sale / contract]
between [D's other company] and the corporation; the question is whether disclosure was
made and disinterested approval obtained.

**Thus.** Thus, the transaction is [voidable / validated by the safe harbor].

> **CA divergence:** California Corporations Code §310 provides the framework for
> interested director transactions. The transaction is not void or voidable if: (1) the
> material facts are disclosed and a majority of disinterested directors approved; or
> (2) the material facts are disclosed and a majority of disinterested shareholders approved;
> or (3) the transaction was just and reasonable to the corporation at the time it was
> authorized. The "just and reasonable" standard in (3) is sometimes said to be slightly
> more flexible than the "entire fairness" standard used in Delaware.

---

### Directors' Duty of Loyalty — Usurping a Corporate Opportunity

🟢

**Rule.** A director or officer who personally takes advantage of a business opportunity
that belongs to the corporation breaches the duty of loyalty. An opportunity belongs to
the corporation if (1) it is in the corporation's **line of business**, (2) the
corporation has an **interest or expectancy** in it, or (3) the opportunity was presented
to the director **in their corporate capacity**. Before pursuing the opportunity
personally, the director must first offer it to the corporation; usurpation occurs only
if the corporation rejects or is unable to pursue the opportunity.

**Here.** Here, [director] learned of the [acquisition target / land parcel / contract
opportunity] while serving as a corporate officer, and the corporation was in the business
of [same line]; [director] invested personally without disclosing the opportunity to the
board.

**Thus.** Thus, [director] usurped a corporate opportunity, breaching the duty of loyalty,
and must hold profits as constructive trustee for the corporation.

### Shareholder Derivative Suit

🟢  ·  *CA-specific security rule is a high-yield trap*

**Rule.** A shareholder derivative suit is brought by a shareholder on behalf of the
corporation when the board refuses or fails to pursue a valid corporate claim. To
maintain a derivative suit, a plaintiff shareholder must (1) have been a **contemporaneous
owner** — owning stock at the time of the wrong complained of, (2) make a **demand on
the board** to take action (or allege with particularity why demand would have been
futile, such as when the majority of directors are interested or not independent), and
(3) fairly and adequately represent the interests of shareholders. Any recovery belongs
to the **corporation**, not to the suing shareholder (except reasonable attorney's fees).

**Here.** Here, [shareholder] owned shares at the time of [breach]; [shareholder] made a
written demand on the board, which [refused within 90 days / was wrongfully ignored],
and the suit proceeds derivatively on behalf of the corporation.

**Thus.** Thus, the derivative suit is [properly/improperly] maintained.

> **CA divergence:** Under California Corporations Code §800, a plaintiff in a derivative
> suit must (1) be a shareholder at the time of the transaction (contemporaneous ownership),
> (2) make demand unless demand is excused by futility, and (3) in a **corporation with
> 500 or more shareholders**, post **security for expenses** (a bond covering defendants'
> litigation costs) unless the plaintiff holds at least 5% of the outstanding shares or
> shares with a value over $25,000. This **security-for-expenses** requirement is a
> distinctly CA mechanism designed to deter strike suits and is a high-yield exam point.
> Delaware and MBCA jurisdictions do not have this requirement.

### Shareholder Direct Suit

🟡

**Rule.** A shareholder may sue directly — in her own name, for her own recovery — when
the harm is to the shareholder personally or to a class of shareholders qua shareholders,
distinct from any harm to the corporation. Direct claims include: deprivation of voting
rights, improper exclusion from the corporation, denial of inspection rights, and a
controlling shareholder's wrongful oppression of minority shareholders. The key test is:
who suffered the injury, and who would receive the remedy?

**Here.** Here, [majority shareholders] blocked [minority shareholders'] voting rights,
which is a direct harm to the shareholders' membership rights, not to the corporation.

**Thus.** Thus, [shareholder] may sue directly rather than derivatively.

---

### Controlling Shareholder Duties

🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Duty of Loyalty*

**Rule.** A shareholder who controls the corporation — typically owning more than 50%
of voting shares or exercising actual control — owes a fiduciary duty to minority
shareholders. Transactions between the controlling shareholder and the corporation
are subject to **entire fairness** review: the controlling party must show both fair
dealing (process) and fair price (economic fairness to the minority). In a freeze-out
or cash-out merger, the burden shifts to the controlling shareholder to prove fairness.

**Here.** Here, [controlling shareholder] caused the corporation to [buy back shares at
below-market price / enter a related-party contract], and the minority received no
independent protection.

**Thus.** Thus, the transaction must satisfy entire fairness, and [controlling shareholder]
bears the burden of proving fair price and fair dealing.

### Dividends

🟡

**Rule.** The board of directors has broad discretion to declare or withhold dividends —
shareholders have no absolute right to dividends. Preferred shareholders have a
preferential right to dividends at the stated rate before common shareholders receive
anything, and cumulative preferred dividends accumulate if unpaid. A dividend may not
be paid if the corporation is insolvent or if paying the dividend would render it
insolvent. Directors who authorize an illegal dividend may be personally liable to
creditors for the amount paid out.

**Here.** Here, the board [declared a dividend while the corporation was insolvent /
paid common dividends without satisfying cumulative preferred arrears], which [was
impermissible / breaches the preferential rights of preferred shareholders].

**Thus.** Thus, the dividend was [lawful under the BJR / improper and recoverable].

> **CA divergence:** Under California Corporations Code §500, dividends may be paid
> only if the corporation's retained earnings equal or exceed the amount of the
> distribution, OR after the distribution the corporation's assets would be at least
> 1.25 times its liabilities and current assets at least equal current liabilities.
> This solvency test differs somewhat from the MBCA approach.

### Shareholder Inspection Rights

🟡

**Rule.** Shareholders have a right to inspect corporate books and records — including
the articles, bylaws, accounting books, and shareholder list — upon making a written
demand stating a **proper purpose**: one reasonably related to the person's interest as
a shareholder (e.g., investigating misconduct, valuing shares, communicating with fellow
shareholders). The burden is on the shareholder to establish a proper purpose; the
corporation may then deny inspection only if the purpose is improper or the demand is
not in proper form.

**Here.** Here, [shareholder]'s demand stated a purpose of [investigating suspected
self-dealing / determining the value of shares], which is a proper purpose, and the
corporation's refusal was wrongful.

**Thus.** Thus, [shareholder] is entitled to the requested inspection.

> **CA divergence:** California Corporations Code §1600 et seq. provides inspection
> rights somewhat more broadly than many other states. Any holder of at least 5% of
> the outstanding shares, or any holder of 1% who was denied statutory inspection rights,
> may demand an inspection of the **accounting books and records** and the **shareholder
> list**. CA courts have held the proper-purpose requirement applies but interpret it
> liberally. Shareholders holding any amount may inspect articles and bylaws.

### Fundamental Corporate Changes and Appraisal Rights

🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Contracts (consideration for merger)*

**Rule.** Certain fundamental corporate changes — mergers, sale of substantially all
assets outside the ordinary course of business, dissolution, and amendments to articles
affecting fundamental rights — require (1) **board of directors approval** followed by
(2) **shareholder approval** by the requisite vote (usually a majority of outstanding
shares, or higher if the articles require). **Dissenting shareholders** who perfect their
appraisal rights are entitled to receive the **fair value** of their shares as determined
by the court, in lieu of the merger consideration — they may not seek both appraisal and
other remedies simultaneously (election of remedies).

**Here.** Here, [corporation] entered a merger after board approval and a [majority /
supermajority] shareholder vote; [shareholder] who voted against the merger may perfect
appraisal rights by following the statutory procedures.

**Thus.** Thus, [dissenting shareholder] may demand appraisal and receive fair value rather
than the merger consideration.

> **CA divergence:** California Corporations Code §§1200–1312 govern mergers and
> reorganizations. CA's appraisal rights apply to shareholders of constituent corporations
> in a merger, but the **market-out exception** applies — if shares are listed on a
> national securities exchange or the company has 2,000+ shareholders and meets assets/
> revenue tests, shareholders generally cannot demand appraisal (on the theory that a
> liquid public market provides an adequate exit). This is broadly similar to the MBCA
> and Delaware approaches but check the specific statutory thresholds.
> ⚠️ VERIFY current Corp. Code §1300 thresholds.

### LLC — Formation

🟢

**Rule.** A limited liability company (LLC) is formed by filing articles of organization
(or a certificate of organization) with the state. The LLC then has a separate legal
existence. Governance and economic arrangements are typically set out in an **operating
agreement**, which may be written or oral (though written is strongly advised). The LLC
provides limited liability to all members — no member is personally liable for the debts
or obligations of the LLC solely by reason of being a member.

**Here.** Here, [organizers] filed articles of organization with the state, forming the
LLC; members' personal assets are therefore shielded from LLC debts unless veil piercing
applies.

**Thus.** Thus, the LLC was duly formed and its members enjoy limited liability.

> **CA divergence:** California LLCs are governed by the California Revised Uniform
> Limited Liability Company Act (Corp. Code §17701 et seq.), effective 2014. Formation
> requires filing Articles of Organization with the Secretary of State and paying the
> applicable filing fee. **All LLCs doing business in California must pay an annual
> minimum franchise tax of $800** (Rev. & Tax. Code §17942) — a trap for out-of-state
> LLCs operating in CA.

### LLC — Member-Managed vs. Manager-Managed

🟡

**Rule.** An LLC is **member-managed** by default — each member has actual authority to
act as an agent of the LLC and bind it in the ordinary course of business. The members
may instead elect **manager-managed** in the articles of organization or operating
agreement; in that case, only designated managers (who may or may not be members) have
actual authority to bind the LLC, while non-managing members have no such authority.
Third parties dealing with a member-managed LLC may rely on each member's apparent
authority to bind the LLC in the ordinary course.

**Here.** Here, the LLC [was silent / designated manager-managed]; therefore [member /
non-managing member]'s contract [did / did not] bind the LLC.

**Thus.** Thus, [the LLC is bound / [party] acted without authority and the LLC is not
bound].

---

### LLC — Fiduciary Duties

🟡

**Rule.** In a member-managed LLC, each member owes the LLC duties of loyalty and care
analogous to those owed by partners in a general partnership — no self-dealing without
disclosure, no usurping LLC opportunities, and no grossly negligent management. In a
manager-managed LLC, the manager owes those duties and non-managing members owe only a
duty to act in good faith. Operating agreements may **modify or eliminate** some fiduciary
duties, but generally may not eliminate the duty to act in good faith and deal fairly.

**Here.** Here, [manager/member] [diverted an LLC contract to a personal entity / voted
to self-deal without disclosure], which [breaches / may breach, subject to operating
agreement waiver] the duty of loyalty.

**Thus.** Thus, [manager/member]'s conduct is a breach of fiduciary duty unless the
operating agreement validly waived it.

> **CA divergence:** California Revised Uniform LLC Act §17704.09 codifies the duties of
> loyalty and care for managers and managing members. The operating agreement may modify
> but cannot eliminate these duties entirely — it cannot relieve a manager of the duty to
> deal in good faith, and cannot authorize conduct that is illegal or constitutes willful
> misconduct (Corp. Code §17701.10(c)).

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:** Business Associations is most commonly paired with **Contracts**
  (pre-incorporation contracts, partnership and LLC agreements, third-party contracts
  through agency), **Torts** (respondeat superior, officer/director liability), and
  **Remedies** (injunctive relief against a disloyal director, disgorgement, constructive
  trust). A corporation essay may include a securities-law or tax overlay as a distractor
  — focus on Corp. Code analysis and ignore the distractor unless the call of the question
  specifically raises it.

- **Sleeper issues:**
  - Forgetting to analyze **apparent authority** separately from actual authority when a
    third party was involved — the third party's reasonable belief is the key.
  - Missing **promoter liability** when facts describe someone organizing a business before
    articles are filed.
  - Skipping **demand futility** analysis on derivative suits when the board is conflicted.
  - Omitting CA's **security-for-expenses** requirement in derivative suits (the single
    highest-yield CA-specific point in BA).
  - Failing to recognize that a **duty of loyalty** breach (self-dealing or usurped
    opportunity) requires entire fairness review, not just business judgment review.
  - Conflating a **direct** shareholder suit with a **derivative** suit — identify who
    suffered the harm and who receives the remedy.
  - In dissolution problems, missing the **priority waterfall**: creditors first (including
    partner-creditors), then capital contributions, then equal profits.

- **CA-divergence cheat list:**
  1. **Security for expenses** in derivative suits — required for corps with 500+ shareholders
     unless plaintiff holds 5% or $25,000 in shares (Corp. Code §800). Not in MBCA/Delaware.
  2. **Interested director transactions** — CA §310 uses "just and reasonable" as a fairness
     standard (not "entire fairness" as in Delaware), and the three clean-up pathways are
     codified.
  3. **Dividends solvency test** — CA Corp. Code §500's retained-earnings / 1.25x assets-to-
     liabilities test differs from the simple MBCA solvency test.
  4. **Inspection rights** — CA gives broader inspection rights; 5%/1% thresholds for accounting
     books; any amount for articles and bylaws.
  5. **LP control** — CA's safe-harbor list of permitted limited-partner activities is broader
     than the traditional rule.
  6. **LLC franchise tax** — CA imposes $800/year minimum on all LLCs doing business in CA,
     including foreign LLCs; a fact-pattern involving an out-of-state LLC operating in CA
     should trigger this.
  7. **Partnership exhaustion rule** — CA generally requires creditors to exhaust partnership
     assets before pursuing individual partners personally (Corp. Code §16307).

- **Call-of-the-question patterns:**
  - "What liability, if any, does [shareholder / officer] have?" → run veil-piercing and/or
    duty-of-care/loyalty analysis; if an officer, add agency.
  - "May [shareholder] maintain this suit?" → derivative vs. direct; demand requirement;
    contemporaneous ownership; CA security-for-expenses.
  - "Did [director] breach any duty?" → always run both duty of care (BJR) and duty of
    loyalty (self-dealing + corporate opportunity) — both may be present.
  - "What are the rights of [third party] against [defendant]?" → agency + respondeat
    superior; partnership joint-and-several liability; corporate veil.
  - "Is [entity] liable?" → start with entity formation (de jure / de facto), then authority
    of the agent who acted, then any ratification.
