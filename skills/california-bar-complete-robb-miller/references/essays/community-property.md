# Community Property — Essay Bank

> California is a community-property state. Every rule below is CA law (Family Code
> unless otherwise noted). "CA divergence" callouts flag precise CA rules, traps, and
> verify points rather than CA-vs-majority splits. All rule language is original prose.
> Flag uncertain law `> ⚠️ VERIFY`.

**High-frequency note:** CP essays almost always require characterization first, then
pivot to a remedy context (divorce/dissolution division, death/probate, or debt
liability). The classic chain is: characterize → apply presumption → rebut (tracing or
transmutation) → divide. Crossed most often with **Wills & Trusts** (testamentary power
over CP at death) and **Professional Responsibility** (attorney managing marital assets).
The examiner rewards precision on **date of separation**, **transmutation writing
requirements**, and the **Pereira/Van Camp business-accounting split**.

## Part 2 — IRAC Rule Blocks (full, memorizable)

### Characterization — General CP Presumption
🟢  ·  *anchors every CP essay; do this first*

**Rule.** California is a community-property state. All property acquired by either spouse during the marriage while domiciled in California is presumed to be community property. (Fam. Code §760.) This presumption applies unless rebutted by clear and convincing evidence that the property is separate property or was transmuted. The marital community begins at the date of marriage and ends at the date of separation (not the date a dissolution petition is filed).

**Here.** Here, [asset] was acquired [by/during] the marriage, triggering the CP presumption; [spouse] must rebut it to establish a separate-property claim.

**Thus.** Thus, absent rebuttal, [asset] is community property subject to equal division.

---

### Separate Property — Definition and Categories
🟢

**Rule.** Separate property is: (1) all property owned before marriage; (2) all property acquired after the date of separation; (3) all property received by gift, bequest, devise, or descent during the marriage; and (4) the rents, issues, and profits of any separate property. (Fam. Code §770.) The last point — **SP income/appreciation is also SP** — is the key CA rule distinguishing it from common-law equitable-distribution states where passive appreciation may be marital.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] received [asset] as [inheritance/gift] from [donor] during the marriage, placing it in category (3); [or it was owned before marriage, placing it in category (1)].

**Thus.** Thus, [asset] is [spouse]'s separate property.

> **CA rule to know:** In CA, the income and appreciation of SP remains SP. A spouse who inherits stock during marriage and the stock doubles in value retains both the original stock and the appreciation as SP.

---

### Date of Separation
🟢  ·  *critical trigger issue — can change characterization of everything after it*

**Rule.** The date of separation is the date on which a complete and final break in the marital relationship occurs, meaning the spouse (1) subjectively formed an intent to permanently end the marriage **and** (2) objectively expressed that intent through conduct inconsistent with an ongoing marriage. (Fam. Code §70, codifying *In re Marriage of Davis* (2015).) Property acquired by either spouse's labor **after** the date of separation is that spouse's separate property.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] [moved out / told the other spouse the marriage was over / retained a divorce attorney] on [date], which may establish both the subjective intent and its objective expression; if so, [asset acquired after that date] is SP, not CP.

**Thus.** Thus, if the court finds separation occurred on [date], [later-acquired asset] is [spouse]'s separate property.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: FC §70 was enacted in 2017 after *Davis* and codified the two-part test; confirm no subsequent statutory amendment.

---

### Commingling — Tracing Generally
🟢

**Rule.** When separate-property funds are commingled with community-property funds so that the two cannot be distinguished, the entire fund is presumed community property. The owner of the SP may overcome this presumption by **tracing** the SP contribution using either the exhaustion method or the direct-tracing method. The burden of tracing rests on the spouse asserting the SP claim.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] deposited SP funds of $[X] into a joint account also containing CP funds; [spouse] must trace to reclaim SP character.

**Thus.** Thus, [spouse] must establish tracing or the entire account is treated as CP.

---

### Commingling — Exhaustion (Lowest-Intermediate-Balance) Tracing
🟡

**Rule.** Under the exhaustion method, a spouse establishes that SP survives in a commingled account by showing that the account balance **never fell below** the amount of the claimed SP contribution. If the balance dipped below that floor, the SP is deemed exhausted by community expenditures and is lost.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] deposited $[X] SP into the account; if the balance never dropped below $[X], $[X] survives as SP regardless of subsequent deposits and withdrawals.

**Thus.** Thus, [spouse] [can/cannot] trace $[X] as SP under the exhaustion method because the balance [never fell / fell] below $[X].

---

### Commingling — Direct Tracing
🟡

**Rule.** Under the direct-tracing method, a spouse must show (1) that SP funds were on deposit in the account at the time of the purchase **and** (2) that the spouse intended to use the SP funds (not CP funds) for that specific purchase. Both elements are required; intent is often shown by records or testimony demonstrating the spouse earmarked the SP funds.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] claims the [asset] purchased from the commingled account was paid with SP funds; [spouse] must show SP was on deposit and intended for that purchase.

**Thus.** Thus, direct tracing [succeeds/fails] because [the SP funds were/were not on deposit and the requisite intent is/is not supported].

---

### Transmutation — Post-1984 Writing Requirement
🟢  ·  *high-frequency; examiner loves fact patterns where parties "agreed" orally*

**Rule.** A transmutation — an interspousal agreement that changes the character of property from CP to SP, SP to CP, or the SP of one spouse to the SP of the other — executed **on or after January 1, 1985** is invalid unless it is made in writing and contains an **express declaration** by the spouse whose interest is adversely affected that the character of the property is being changed. (Fam. Code §852(a).) Oral agreements, conduct, and oral promises do not transmute post-1984 property.

**Here.** Here, the parties [orally agreed / [spouse] simply began titling CP as SP]; because no written express declaration exists from the adversely affected spouse, no transmutation occurred post-1985.

**Thus.** Thus, the property retains its original character.

> **CA rule:** The writing must contain the word "transmute" or equivalent language expressly acknowledging a change of character — a deed granting property to one spouse "as separate property" alone may be sufficient, but love notes and informal emails are not. Courts read the requirement strictly.

---

### Transmutation — Pre-1985 (Oral / Conduct)
🟡

**Rule.** For transactions occurring **before January 1, 1985**, a transmutation could be effected orally, by conduct, or by written agreement, without the express-declaration requirement. The party claiming transmutation must still show mutual agreement to change the character of the property.

**Here.** Here, if the agreement occurred in [year pre-1985], the oral agreement [may / may not] have transmuted the property depending on evidence of mutual assent.

**Thus.** Thus, the pre-1985 transmutation [succeeded/failed] based on the evidence of agreement.

---

### Transmutation — Personal-Use Gift Exception
🟡

**Rule.** A written express declaration is **not required** for a transmutation effected by an interspousal gift of clothing, wearing apparel, jewelry, or other personal-use items unless the item is of substantial value relative to the community estate. (Fam. Code §852(c).)

**Here.** Here, [spouse A] gave [spouse B] a [wedding ring / piece of jewelry] from CP funds; if the item is of modest value and for personal use, no writing is needed and it is transmuted to [spouse B]'s SP.

**Thus.** Thus, the personal-use gift is [spouse B]'s separate property without a writing.

---

### Business Valuation — Pereira vs. Van Camp
🔴  ·  *high-frequency, analytically rich; always present both methods and choose*

**Rule.** When a spouse owned a business as separate property before marriage (or the business has a SP component) and community labor contributed to its growth during marriage, courts use one of two apportionment formulas to separate the CP and SP shares:

- **Pereira approach:** Assign the SP a fair rate of return on the original SP investment (typically the legal rate of interest or a reasonable market return); all appreciation **above** that return is CP, credited to community labor.
- **Van Camp approach:** Assign the CP the reasonable value of the community labor contributed (what the spouse should have been paid); the **residual** appreciation beyond that reasonable salary remains SP, credited to the inherent nature or capital of the business.

A court **selects the method** that most fairly apportions the respective contributions. Pereira favors the community when community labor drove growth; Van Camp favors the SP owner when the business's nature, capital, or market forces drove growth.

**Here.** Here, [spouse]'s pre-marital business was worth $[X]; during the marriage, [spouse]'s labor [drove growth / maintained a capital-intensive enterprise]; under Pereira, the CP share = total value minus [$X × (fair return × years)]; under Van Camp, the CP share = reasonable salary × years.

**Thus.** Thus, [Pereira/Van Camp] better reflects the contributions, and the CP share is approximately $[amount].

> ⚠️ VERIFY: The "legal rate" Pereira historically uses is 7% (former legal rate); some courts use a reasonable market return. Confirm current judicial preference.

### Business Goodwill
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Business assets above*

**Rule.** Professional or enterprise goodwill that exists in a business or professional practice as of the date of separation is a community asset to the extent it was built during the marriage. In California, **both enterprise goodwill** (the business's going-concern value) and **professional goodwill** (the personal reputation and client base of a professional spouse) are divisible CP. This is a CA-specific rule — many other states exclude personal goodwill.

**Here.** Here, [spouse]'s [law practice / dental practice] has an established client base and reputation built during the marriage; both the enterprise and personal goodwill components are CP subject to valuation and division.

**Thus.** Thus, [spouse]'s professional goodwill is a CP asset subject to equal division.

> **CA rule to know:** California is in the minority in treating professional/personal goodwill as divisible CP. On the exam, flag this as a CA-specific rule.

---

### Pensions and Retirement Benefits — Time Rule
🟢

**Rule.** A pension or retirement benefit earned in part during marriage and in part outside marriage is apportioned under the **time rule**: the CP fraction equals the number of years (or months) of plan participation **during the marriage** (from marriage to date of separation) divided by the total years of participation at retirement. The resulting CP fraction is applied to the total benefit.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] participated in the pension plan for [total N] years, [M] of which fell between the date of marriage and the date of separation; the CP fraction is M/N; the other spouse is entitled to one-half of that fraction.

**Thus.** Thus, the non-employee spouse's share = ½ × (M/N) × [total benefit].

---

### Pensions — Vested vs. Unvested
🟡

**Rule.** An unvested pension is still a community asset if it was accrued during the marriage, because it represents a contingent future right earned by the employee spouse's labor. Courts divide it either at the time of dissolution (present-value method) or when benefits are actually received (reserved jurisdiction / QDRO method). Under the QDRO method, the non-employee spouse receives a proportionate share when payments commence.

**Here.** Here, [spouse]'s pension is unvested at the time of dissolution; the court may defer division through a QDRO requiring the plan administrator to pay the non-employee spouse when benefits vest and are paid.

**Thus.** Thus, the unvested pension is still CP subject to division, typically by QDRO.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: Federal ERISA governs most private-sector pension QDROs; CA court orders must comply with the plan's terms. Government plans use DROsunder state law.

---

### Education / Professional Degrees — Reimbursement
🟡

**Rule.** A professional degree or license earned during marriage is **not divisible community property** in California — it belongs solely to the earning spouse. However, if community funds contributed to the education costs (tuition, fees, books, living expenses attributable to education), the community estate is entitled to **reimbursement** of those direct contributions. (Fam. Code §2641.) Reimbursement is not available if: (1) the community substantially benefited from the education for **10 or more years** before dissolution, or (2) the other spouse also received community-funded education of equal or greater value.

**Here.** Here, the community paid $[X] in tuition/fees for [spouse]'s [degree]; if fewer than 10 years have passed and the other spouse received no equivalent education benefit, the community is entitled to reimbursement of $[X].

**Thus.** Thus, the community estate is reimbursed $[X], not a share of the enhanced earning capacity.

> **CA rule:** CA expressly rejects treating enhanced earning capacity from a degree as a divisible asset (contrast with some equitable-distribution states). The remedy is reimbursement only.

---

### Personal-Injury Recoveries
🟡

**Rule.** Money or property received by a spouse during marriage as a personal-injury recovery (damages for bodily injury including pain and suffering, lost wages, and medical expenses) is **community property** while the community exists, because it replaced community income and was paid with community funds (insurance premiums). At **dissolution**, however, the recovery is **assigned** to the injured spouse — not divided equally — unless the interests of justice require otherwise. (Fam. Code §2603.) If the recovery has been commingled with other CP so it can no longer be identified, the entire commingled fund is assigned to the injured spouse.

**Here.** Here, [injured spouse] received $[X] in tort damages during the marriage; at dissolution, those funds — even if CP during marriage — are assigned to [injured spouse] rather than split.

**Thus.** Thus, the personal-injury recovery is assigned to the injured spouse at dissolution.

---

### Stock Options
🟡

**Rule.** Stock options granted during marriage are CP to the extent they were earned by community labor. When an option straddles the marriage and separation, courts use a **time-rule apportionment**, typically: CP fraction = (period from grant to vesting that overlaps with the marriage) / (total period from grant to vesting). Some courts use the date-of-grant method if the grant was compensation for past services; others use a vesting-period method. California courts have not adopted a single uniform formula — the method depends on the facts.

**Here.** Here, [spouse]'s options were granted on [date] and vest on [date]; the marriage lasted from [date] to [separation date]; applying the time rule, the CP fraction = [overlap period / total period].

**Thus.** Thus, [fraction] of the options is CP, entitling the non-employee spouse to [fraction/2] of the options or their value.

> ⚠️ VERIFY: The correct formula (*In re Marriage of Hug* grant-date method vs. *In re Marriage of Nelson* vesting method) continues to be litigated; know both and flag.

---

### Life Insurance — Term vs. Whole
🟡

**Rule.** A **term life insurance policy** is characterized based on the premiums paid: if all premiums were paid from CP funds, the policy proceeds are CP. A **whole life (permanent) policy** has both a death-benefit component and an investment/cash-value component. The entire policy is CP if all premiums were paid from CP. If premiums were paid partly from SP and partly from CP, courts **prorate** the CP and SP interests based on the proportionate premium contributions (the *Biltoft/Grimm* formula).

**Here.** Here, the [whole life] policy was purchased before marriage with SP funds but premiums were continued with CP funds during marriage; the CP share of the death benefit = (CP premiums paid / total premiums paid) × total proceeds.

**Thus.** Thus, the CP and SP interests in the policy are prorated by premium contribution.

---

### Disability and Severance Pay
🟡

**Rule.** The character of disability pay and severance pay is determined by **what the payment replaces**. Disability pay replacing wages the spouse would have earned **during marriage** is CP; disability pay replacing post-separation wages is SP. Severance pay replacing compensation for past services rendered during the marriage is CP to that extent; severance replacing future lost wages that would have been earned post-separation is SP. Courts apportion if both periods are implicated.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] received a lump-sum disability payment covering [time period]; to the extent that period falls within the marriage (before separation), it is CP; to the extent it covers post-separation time, it is SP.

**Thus.** Thus, the disability/severance payment is apportioned between CP and SP based on the covered time period.

---

### Credit Acquisitions — Intent-of-Lender Test
🟡

**Rule.** Property acquired during marriage on credit is characterized by the **intent of the lender**: if the lender looked primarily to **community credit** (the earnings and community assets of the spouses), the property is CP. If the lender relied solely on the borrowing spouse's **separate credit or separate assets**, the property is SP. Intent is determined by objective indicia at the time of the loan — the loan application, credit history used, and collateral pledged.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] purchased [asset] during marriage using a loan; if the lender approved the loan based on [combined marital income / separate credit history], the asset is [CP / SP].

**Thus.** Thus, [asset] is [CP/SP] based on the lender's reliance on [community/separate] credit.

---

### Improvements — SP Funds Improving CP Property
🟡

**Rule.** When a spouse uses separate-property funds to improve community property (e.g., renovating the marital home), the SP contribution does not create a lien or an ownership interest in the CP. At dissolution, the contributing spouse is entitled to **reimbursement** of the SP contribution without interest, but only to the extent the CP benefited (the lesser of the amount contributed or the increase in market value). (Fam. Code §2640.) The reimbursement right may be waived by a written agreement.

**Here.** Here, [spouse] spent $[X] in SP funds on [marital home]; at dissolution, [spouse] is entitled to reimbursement of $[X] (or the increase in value if less), not a separate ownership interest.

**Thus.** Thus, [spouse] receives a reimbursement credit of up to $[X] at dissolution.

---

### Improvements — CP Funds Improving SP Property
🟡

**Rule.** When community funds improve a spouse's separate property (e.g., community earnings used to pay down the mortgage on pre-marital real estate or to add a room), the community acquires a **pro-tanto interest** in the SP: the community's interest is proportionate to its contributions relative to the total value. Alternatively, the community may simply be entitled to reimbursement without acquiring an ownership interest. Courts choose the approach that most fairly reflects the parties' equitable position.

**Here.** Here, CP funds of $[X] were used to pay down the mortgage on [spouse]'s SP real property; if the property appreciated, the community may have acquired a pro-rata share based on the ratio of CP contributions to total equity.

**Thus.** Thus, the community has a cognizable interest in [spouse]'s SP realty to the extent of its contributions.

---

### Management and Control / Fiduciary Duty Between Spouses
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Professional Responsibility (attorney representing both spouses)*

**Rule.** Each spouse has equal rights to manage and control the community property during marriage. (Fam. Code §1100.) However, **both spouses must join** in: (1) any sale, conveyance, or encumbrance of community real property; (2) leases of CP real property for more than one year; and (3) gifts of CP. Each spouse owes the other the **highest good-faith fiduciary duty** — the same duty a trustee owes a beneficiary — including duties of full disclosure of all CP assets, fair dealing, and no secret profits. (Fam. Code §721.) A spouse who breaches this fiduciary duty may be liable for 50% to 100% of the improperly concealed or transferred asset.

**Here.** Here, [spouse A] conveyed the community residence without [spouse B]'s joinder, or [spouse A] concealed a CP asset during dissolution; either act constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty.

**Thus.** Thus, [spouse B] may recover up to 100% of the transferred or concealed asset and attorney's fees.

---

### Division at Dissolution — Equal Division Rule
🟢

**Rule.** Upon dissolution of marriage, California courts are required to divide all community property **equally** — each spouse receives one half of the community estate. (Fam. Code §2550.) The court must also **confirm** separate property to the owning spouse. Equal division may be in kind (each spouse gets equal-value assets) or by awarding one asset and equalizing with a cash payment. The equal-division mandate is subject to narrow exceptions.

**Here.** Here, the parties' CP estate consists of [assets with aggregate value]; the court must divide them such that each spouse walks away with approximately $[X].

**Thus.** Thus, each spouse receives one-half of the CP estate, and each spouse's SP is confirmed to them.

---

### Division at Dissolution — Exceptions: Misappropriation and Breach of Fiduciary Duty
🟡

**Rule.** A court may **deviate from equal division** and award an **unequal share** of the community estate — up to the entirety of a specific asset — when a spouse has: (1) deliberately misappropriated CP; (2) breached the fiduciary duty of disclosure; or (3) wrongfully dissipated or wasted CP. (Fam. Code §1101; §2602.) The court may also award attorney's fees to the aggrieved spouse as a remedy for the breach.

**Here.** Here, [spouse A] secretly transferred $[X] in CP to a third party or to a separate account; the court may award the entirety of that amount (or an equivalent asset) to [spouse B] as remedy for the breach.

**Thus.** Thus, [spouse B] is entitled to more than an equal share to offset [spouse A]'s misappropriation.

---

### Debt Division at Dissolution
🟡

**Rule.** Community debts — obligations incurred by either spouse during marriage for community purposes — are divided equally at dissolution. The non-contracting spouse's SP is **not** liable for the other spouse's premarital debts or debts incurred for non-community purposes. However, a creditor's rights are not diminished by the dissolution decree; creditors may still reach whichever assets are available, and the burdened spouse has a right of contribution from the other if required to pay more than his/her share.

**Here.** Here, [debt] was incurred during the marriage for [household expenses]; it is a community debt divided equally; if [spouse A] is required to pay the full debt, [spouse A] has a contribution claim against [spouse B] for half.

**Thus.** Thus, the debt is allocated equally, though third-party creditor rights are unaffected by the allocation.

---

### Division at Death — Testamentary Power Over CP
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Wills & Trusts*

**Rule.** Each spouse owns an **undivided one-half interest** in all community property. At death, a spouse may **dispose by will of only his or her one-half of the CP**; the surviving spouse's half passes automatically to the survivor and is beyond the decedent's testamentary control. (Prob. Code §100.) The decedent's one-half of CP may be devised to any beneficiary — including a third party — or placed in a trust. If a decedent dies intestate, the decedent's one-half of CP passes to the surviving spouse by intestacy. (Prob. Code §101.)

**Here.** Here, [decedent spouse] purported to devise the entirety of [CP asset] by will; the devise is valid only as to the decedent's one-half; the surviving spouse retains the other half regardless of the will.

**Thus.** Thus, the surviving spouse takes one-half outright, and only the other half passes under the will.

> **CA rule:** In CA, if both halves of the CP are devised to the surviving spouse (or pass by intestacy to the surviving spouse), the survivor may elect to **confirm the entire asset** as SP (stepped-up basis planning) or treat it as CP — the surviving-spouse election under Prob. Code §101.

### Quasi-Community Property
🟡  ·  *often a sleeper issue in essays involving couples who moved to CA*

**Rule.** Quasi-community property is property that: (1) was acquired by either spouse while **domiciled outside California** and (2) would have been community property **if** the acquiring spouse had been domiciled in California at the time of acquisition. (Fam. Code §125.) QCP is treated as CP for purposes of **dissolution** and at the **death of the acquiring spouse**, but not during an ongoing marriage — neither spouse has a present interest in QCP while the marriage continues; the acquiring spouse may manage and even dispose of it during life.

**Here.** Here, the parties moved to California after [spouse] acquired [asset] while domiciled in [other state]; that asset is QCP, subject to equal division at dissolution as though it were CP.

**Thus.** Thus, [asset] is QCP and is equally divisible at dissolution.

> **CA rule:** QCP does not apply at the death of the **non-acquiring** spouse — the non-acquiring spouse cannot reach QCP by will or intestacy unless it has already become subject to CA law at dissolution.

### Quasi-Marital Property / Putative Spouse
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Family Law (void/voidable marriage); Wills & Trusts*

**Rule.** A **putative spouse** is a person who participated in an invalid marriage ceremony (void or voidable marriage) in **good faith**, believing the marriage to be valid. Good faith is judged subjectively — did this person honestly and reasonably believe the marriage was legal? A putative spouse acquires **quasi-marital property** rights: property accumulated during the putative marriage that would have been CP in a valid marriage is treated as quasi-marital property and divided as CP at the end of the relationship. Good faith is lost once the party learns of the impediment; property acquired after that date is not quasi-marital.

**Here.** Here, [party A] married [party B] not knowing [party B] had a prior undissolved marriage; if [party A]'s belief in the validity was genuine and reasonable, [party A] is a putative spouse entitled to an equal share of quasi-marital property.

**Thus.** Thus, [party A] has quasi-marital property rights in the assets accumulated during the putative marriage.

### Premarital Agreements — Validity Under CA UPAA
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Professional Responsibility (independent counsel); Contracts*

**Rule.** Under California's Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (Fam. Code §§1610–1617), a premarital agreement is enforceable if: (1) it is in **writing** and **signed by both parties**; (2) the party challenging the agreement had **independent legal counsel** at signing, OR was advised to seek independent counsel and voluntarily and expressly waived that right in a separate writing; (3) the agreement was presented at least **7 calendar days** before execution (the seven-day rule); and (4) the agreement was **not the product of fraud, duress, coercion, or undue influence**. The party seeking to invalidate bears the burden of proof.

**Here.** Here, the agreement was signed [X] days before the wedding; [challenging spouse] [had/did not have] independent counsel; [the 7-day rule was/was not met]; if fewer than 7 days elapsed and no independent counsel represented the challenging party, the agreement is **presumed involuntary**.

**Thus.** Thus, the agreement is [enforceable / presumptively involuntary and may be set aside].

### Premarital Agreements — Scope and Spousal Support Waiver
🟡

**Rule.** Parties to a premarital agreement may contract with respect to: the rights and obligations of each in property of either or both; the disposition of property at dissolution or death; the modification or elimination of spousal support; and any other matter not in violation of public policy or criminal statutes. (Fam. Code §1612.) A waiver of **spousal support** is enforceable unless it was **unconscionable** at the time of enforcement — the court may refuse to enforce it if doing so would leave a party eligible for public assistance or if enforcement is unjust given changed circumstances.

**Here.** Here, the agreement waived all spousal support; [challenging spouse] now argues the waiver is unconscionable because [significant change in economic circumstances / eligibility for public assistance].

**Thus.** Thus, the spousal support waiver [is/is not] enforceable depending on whether circumstances at the time of enforcement render it unconscionable.

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:**
  - **Wills & Trusts:** The CP/death split is the most common crossover — a will question must first characterize property as CP or SP before identifying the devisable share. Watch for decedents devising more than their half of CP.
  - **Professional Responsibility:** Attorneys who represent both spouses in dissolution face conflicts; attorney who holds CP or manages CP assets owes fiduciary duties. Issues of dual representation and disclosure arise.
  - **Contracts:** Premarital agreement validity is tested as a contract-formation problem — offer/acceptance, consideration (marriage is sufficient), writing, capacity — on top of the UPAA specific rules.
  - **Business Associations / Corporations:** If a spouse's closely-held business is involved, Pereira/Van Camp analysis intersects with valuation of ownership interests.

- **Sleeper issues:**
  - **Date of separation precision** — examinees forget that property earned by labor after separation is SP, not CP; this can completely reverse the characterization of a business income stream or salary.
  - **Rents/issues/profits of SP are SP** — examinees treat rental income from a pre-marital property as CP; it is not.
  - **Personal goodwill** — examinees either omit it entirely or argue it is not divisible (minority rule); in CA it IS divisible CP.
  - **QCP** — examinees miss the "couple moved from another state" trigger entirely.
  - **FC §2640 reimbursement cap** — the reimbursement for SP funds improving CP is the **lesser** of the amount contributed or the increase in value; examinees give full credit without applying the cap.
  - **Putative spouse / quasi-marital property** — only raised when the marriage is void or voidable; examinees sometimes skip directly to division without flagging the invalidity.
  - **Severance and disability character** — examinees automatically call all employer-paid benefits CP; the "what does it replace" test is required.

- **CA-divergence cheat list:**
  1. **Rents/issues/profits of SP = SP** — passive income from SP stays SP; not so in most common-law equitable-distribution states.
  2. **Personal/professional goodwill is divisible CP** — a minority CA-specific rule.
  3. **No enhanced earning capacity from degree** — degree itself is not CP; reimbursement only under FC §2641.
  4. **Personal-injury recovery assigned to injured spouse at dissolution** (FC §2603) — not split 50/50 even though CP during marriage.
  5. **Transmutation requires express written declaration** — post-1984, no oral agreement suffices; the writing must expressly declare the change of character.
  6. **7-day rule for premarital agreements** — shorter than some other states; agreement signed the day before the wedding is presumptively involuntary.
  7. **Quasi-community property** — CA extends CP treatment to property acquired in other states; not all community-property states have this doctrine.

- **Call-of-the-question patterns:**
  - "Discuss the rights of the parties to [asset]" → full characterization chain: acquisition date → presumption → SP claim → tracing or transmutation → value at separation → division method.
  - "How should the court divide the estate at dissolution?" → characterize all assets first, then apply equal-division rule, then check for exceptions (waste, breach of fiduciary duty, FC §2640 credits).
  - "What are [spouse]'s rights upon [decedent spouse]'s death?" → identify CP (decedent can devise only ½), SP (decedent can devise all), QCP (treated as CP at death of acquiring spouse); apply intestacy if no will.
  - "Is the premarital agreement enforceable?" → UPAA checklist: writing + signatures → 7-day rule → independent counsel/waiver → voluntariness → unconscionability at enforcement (spousal support only).
  - "How much of the business / pension / stock options is community property?" → pick the correct formula (time rule, Pereira/Van Camp), apply it explicitly with the numbers given.
