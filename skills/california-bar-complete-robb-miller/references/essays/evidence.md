# Evidence — Essay Bank

**High-frequency note:** Evidence almost always appears as a standalone full essay or
as a major component of a Criminal Law or Civil Procedure crossover. The hidden issue
is almost always the FRE vs. CEC split — hearsay exceptions, privilege, and character
evidence rules diverge materially; graders reward explicit comparison.

---

## Part 1 — Attack Outline (condensed, one-screen)

```
RELEVANCE  — trigger: any offered item of evidence
   rule: logically tends to make a material fact more or less probable; CEC 210 / FRE 401
   ├─ Logical relevance (401/CEC 210) — trigger: opponent says "irrelevant" · rule: any tendency to make fact of consequence more/less probable
   ├─ Legal relevance / prejudice balancing (403/CEC 352) — trigger: gruesome photos, prior bad acts, cumulative proof · rule: exclude if probative value substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion, or waste of time
   ├─ Conditional relevance (FRE 104(b)/CEC 403) — trigger: exhibit depends on authentication or other fact · rule: court admits if sufficient evidence for jury to find condition met

JUDICIAL NOTICE  — trigger: fact described as "undisputed," "public record," geography, statutes
   rule: court may accept adjudicative fact that is generally known or accurately/readily determined from reliable sources
   ├─ Mandatory vs. discretionary — trigger: party requests notice · rule: court must take notice if requested and supplied with information; criminal jury need not accept as conclusive (FRE 201(f)); CA jury must accept in civil, may but need not in criminal

PRELIMINARY QUESTIONS  — trigger: admissibility of confession, competency, privilege
   rule: judge decides under FRE 104(a)/CEC 400–406; judge not bound by rules of evidence except privilege

CHARACTER EVIDENCE  — trigger: defendant's good character, victim's character, MIMIC facts
   rule: inadmissible to prove conduct in conformity therewith on a specific occasion; exceptions below
   ├─ Criminal defendant — opens door · rule: D may offer own pertinent trait (reputation/opinion); prosecution may rebut; CA broader rebuttal
   ├─ Victim character — self-defense · rule: D may offer V's violent character (reputation/opinion); prosecution may rebut; CA sex-offense shield
   ├─ MIMIC / prior bad acts (404(b)) — trigger: words "prior incident," "did same thing before" · rule: prior acts admissible for non-propensity purpose: motive, intent, knowledge, absence of mistake, identity, common plan
   ├─ CEC 1109 domestic violence propensity — trigger: DV or sex offense charged · rule: prior DV/sex acts admissible for propensity in CA (exception to CEC 1101); no FRE analog
   ├─ Civil cases — trigger: negligent entrustment, defamation truth · rule: FRE bars propensity; CEC 1101 bars propensity but allows same non-propensity exceptions
   ├─ Methods of proving character — trigger: "how do you prove it" · rule: in-issue: specific acts OK; reputation/opinion in criminal; cross on specific acts allowed

HABIT / ROUTINE PRACTICE  — trigger: "always," "every time," "invariably," "office routine"
   rule: evidence of a person's regular response to a specific situation admissible to infer conduct on a specific occasion; no FRE/CEC divergence on basic rule

SUBSEQUENT REMEDIAL MEASURES  — trigger: repair, policy change, firing after accident
   rule: not admissible to prove negligence or culpable conduct; admissible for feasibility (if controverted), ownership/control, or impeachment

COMPROMISE / SETTLEMENT  — trigger: settlement negotiations, offers to pay
   rule: offers to pay, acceptances, and related statements made in compromise negotiations inadmissible to prove liability; admissible for bias, negating contention of delay
   ├─ Payment of medical expenses — trigger: D pays victim's bills · rule: not admissible to prove liability; statements accompanying payment ARE admissible under FRE (unlike CA, which excludes accompanying statements)

PLEA BARGAINING  — trigger: withdrawn guilty plea, nolo plea, statements in plea talks
   rule: inadmissible against D; does not apply to civil parties

LIABILITY INSURANCE  — trigger: "do you have insurance?" "D was insured"
   rule: inadmissible to prove negligence or wrongdoing; admissible for agency, ownership, or impeachment

AUTHENTICATION  — trigger: document, photograph, recording, email, social media post offered
   rule: proponent must offer sufficient evidence that item is what it is claimed to be; self-authenticating documents (certified official records, acknowledged instruments, newspapers) need no extrinsic evidence
   ├─ Distinctive characteristics — trigger: email, text, social media · rule: content, context, and circumstances can authenticate; reply doctrine

BEST EVIDENCE RULE (Original Writing Rule)  — trigger: party proves contents of a writing, recording, or photo
   rule: to prove contents of a writing/recording/photo, the original must be produced; duplicates admissible unless genuine question of authenticity or unfair; oral testimony OK if original lost/destroyed without bad faith, unobtainable, or not closely related to controlling issue
   > **CA divergence:** CEC uses "secondary evidence rule" — secondary evidence generally admissible unless opponent shows (1) genuine dispute about authenticity, or (2) admission would be unfair; slightly more permissive than FRE

HEARSAY — trigger: out-of-court statement offered for truth
   rule: an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted is hearsay and inadmissible unless an exception applies
   ├─ Non-hearsay: verbal act / operative legal words — trigger: words that create legal effect · rule: words of offer, defamation, gift — not offered for truth
   ├─ Non-hearsay: effect on listener/reader — trigger: notice, knowledge, state of mind · rule: statement shows listener's knowledge or belief, not truth of statement
   ├─ Non-hearsay: Prior Inconsistent Statements (FRE 801(d)(1)(A)) — trigger: witness said something different before · rule: FRE: only non-hearsay if given under oath at prior proceeding; CA (CEC 1235): any prior inconsistent statement is substantive non-hearsay
   ├─ Non-hearsay: Prior Consistent Statements (FRE 801(d)(1)(B)) — trigger: rehabilitation after charge of recent fabrication · rule: admissible if made before alleged motive to fabricate; also admissible to rehabilitate
   ├─ Non-hearsay: Prior Identification (FRE 801(d)(1)(C)/CEC 1238) — trigger: lineup ID, show-up ID · rule: admissible as substantive evidence
   ├─ Non-hearsay: Party Admissions / Opposing Party Statements (FRE 801(d)(2)) — trigger: party's own out-of-court statement · rule: any statement by party-opponent, adopted admission, authorized admission, agent within scope, co-conspirator in furtherance; CA treats as hearsay exception (CEC 1220-1228) but same result
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Present Sense Impression — trigger: statement made while or immediately after perceiving event · rule: describing or explaining an event made contemporaneously or immediately thereafter; excitement not required
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Excited Utterance — trigger: shocking or startling event, statement made while under stress · rule: statement relating to a startling event made while declarant under stress of excitement
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — State of Mind (Existing) — trigger: "I intend to," "I feel," "I plan to" · rule: then-existing mental, emotional, or physical condition; not statements of memory or belief to prove past fact (Hillmon doctrine)
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Medical Diagnosis/Treatment — trigger: statement to doctor, nurse, EMT · rule: statements of present condition, past condition, cause, or history made for purpose of diagnosis or treatment; CA: statements to treating physician only; FRE extends to consulting physicians
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Recorded Recollection — trigger: witness testifies memory exhausted, once had knowledge · rule: memorandum made/adopted when matter was fresh and accurately reflected knowledge; read into record, not received as exhibit unless adverse party offers
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Business Records — trigger: hospital record, corporate log, police report (check) · rule: record of regularly conducted activity, kept in course of regular business, by person with knowledge, made at or near time of event; lack of trustworthiness defeats; opponent can show circumstances indicate untrustworthiness
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Public Records — trigger: government agency document, official report · rule: records of agency activities, matters observed under legal duty, or factual findings from authorized investigation; criminal cases: police observations NOT admissible against D under FRE; CA: CEC 1280 allows if made in course of duty and near time of act
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Former Testimony — trigger: witness now unavailable, same testimony given at earlier proceeding · rule: unavailable declarant; given under oath at prior proceeding; opportunity and similar motive to develop testimony by the party against whom now offered (or predecessor in interest in civil)
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Dying Declaration — trigger: declarant believed death imminent, statement about cause or circumstances of death · rule: FRE: unavailability required, homicide and civil cases only; CA (CEC 1242): any action; declarant need not actually die under CA
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Statement Against Interest — trigger: unavailable declarant made statement that hurt their own interest when made · rule: unavailable declarant; against pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest when made; reasonable person would not have made it unless true; if offered to exculpate D in criminal case, must be corroborated
   ├─ HEARSAY EXCEPTION — Forfeiture by Wrongdoing — trigger: D killed or threatened witness to prevent testimony · rule: party who wrongfully caused declarant's unavailability with intent to prevent testimony forfeits hearsay objection; Giles v. California: intent required even in DV context

CONFRONTATION CLAUSE  — trigger: criminal case, testimonial hearsay offered against D, declarant unavailable
   rule: Crawford v. Washington: testimonial hearsay admitted against criminal defendant requires prior opportunity for cross-examination; non-testimonial hearsay not barred; Davis v. Washington: 911 calls to address ongoing emergency = non-testimonial; formal police interrogation after emergency = testimonial
   > **CA divergence:** CEC has no direct Confrontation Clause analog (it is a federal constitutional guarantee applied through 14th Amendment); CA courts apply Crawford. However, Prop 8 (1982) provides that relevant evidence is generally admissible in CA criminal cases, but does not override constitutional exclusions.

IMPEACHMENT  — trigger: witness testifies; adversary wants to attack credibility
   rule: any party may attack any witness's credibility
   ├─ Prior Inconsistent Statements — trigger: witness said something different before · rule: extrinsic evidence allowed if witness given opportunity to explain or deny (FRE 613); CA: same; statement need not be under oath for impeachment (though FRE 801(d)(1)(A) limits substantive use)
   ├─ Bias / Interest / Motive — trigger: witness paid, relationship to party, plea deal · rule: always admissible; no collateral issue bar; extrinsic evidence of bias allowed
   ├─ Prior Conviction — trigger: witness or party has criminal record · rule: FRE 609: crime involving dishonesty or false statement — automatic; other felony — balancing (prejudice to D outweighs probative); CA (Prop 8/CEC 788): prior felony conviction admissible for impeachment of any witness including D in criminal case; judge has discretion under CEC 352
   ├─ Character for Untruthfulness — trigger: witness's general reputation for lying · rule: reputation or opinion evidence about truthfulness admissible; specific acts of untruthfulness may be asked on cross (no extrinsic evidence); FRE 608; CEC 786-790
   ├─ Contradiction — trigger: witness says X, other evidence says not-X · rule: may impeach by contradiction; extrinsic evidence barred on collateral matters; allowed on non-collateral matters
   ├─ Sensory/Mental Defect — trigger: witness had impaired perception · rule: evidence of incapacity to observe, recall, or communicate
   ├─ Rehabilitation — trigger: after attack on credibility · rule: prior consistent statement (before motive to fabricate); good character for truthfulness only after character attacked; cannot bolster before attack

PRIVILEGES  — trigger: attorney-client communication, marital communication, doctor-patient, therapist
   rule: privilege holder may refuse to disclose and prevent others from disclosing protected communication
   ├─ Attorney-Client — trigger: confidential communication between attorney and client for purpose of legal advice · rule: protects confidential communications (not underlying facts); holder is client; survives death; crime-fraud exception; inadvertent waiver analysis
   ├─ Spousal — Testimonial (anti-marital facts) — trigger: spouse called to testify against other spouse · rule: FRE/federal: witness-spouse holds privilege (can choose to testify or not); CA (CEC 970): either spouse may refuse to testify against the other in criminal proceedings; both must consent in civil
   ├─ Spousal — Confidential Communications — trigger: private conversation between spouses · rule: protects private communications during valid marriage; survives divorce; either spouse holds; exceptions for crimes against children, crimes against each other
   > **CA divergence:** CA has two separate spousal privileges: CEC 970 (anti-testimony, criminal only — witness-spouse holds) and CEC 980 (confidential marital communications — either spouse holds). FRE: federal courts recognize both but the anti-marital facts privilege is held by the witness-spouse only.
   ├─ Physician-Patient — trigger: patient's statement to treating physician · rule: CA (CEC 994): patient holds; protects confidential communications made for purposes of diagnosis or treatment; NO analogous FRE federal privilege (FRE defers to state law in diversity; no federal physician-patient privilege in federal question cases)
   > **CA divergence:** Federal courts do NOT recognize a physician-patient privilege in federal question cases. CA does. In a CA criminal case, the privilege is subject to CEC 996-1007 exceptions (patient-litigant exception, crime/fraud, etc.).
   ├─ Psychotherapist-Patient — trigger: statement to psychiatrist, psychologist, licensed therapist · rule: Jaffee v. Redmond: federal courts recognize psychotherapist privilege; CA CEC 1010-1026: similar; Tarasoff duty to warn does not itself destroy privilege

OPINION EVIDENCE  — trigger: witness gives opinion, expert called
   rule: lay witness may testify to opinion rationally based on personal perception and helpful to understanding testimony (not requiring expertise); expert may testify if specialized knowledge will help trier of fact
   ├─ Lay Opinion — trigger: speed estimate, sobriety, emotional state · rule: FRE 701: rationally based on perception, helpful, not based on scientific knowledge requiring expert
   ├─ Expert Opinion — trigger: DNA, accident reconstruction, medical causation, financial analysis · rule: FRE 702 (Daubert): (1) sufficient facts/data, (2) reliable principles/methods, (3) reliably applied to facts; judge acts as gatekeeper; Daubert factors: tested, peer reviewed, known error rate, generally accepted
   > **CA divergence:** CA uses Kelly/Frye standard (People v. Kelly; Frye v. United States): novel scientific evidence is admissible only if based on a method that is generally accepted in the relevant scientific community. Less flexible than Daubert; CA courts do not conduct Daubert's multi-factor gatekeeping for established science, but Kelly applies to any new scientific technique. Expert basis (CEC 801): may rely on matter perceived, personally known, or made known at or before hearing — including inadmissible matter if reasonably relied on by experts in the field.
   ├─ Ultimate Issue — trigger: expert says "defendant was negligent" · rule: FRE: experts may testify to ultimate issues (except mental state of criminal defendant re: element of crime); CEC 805: same result, ultimate issue testimony allowed
   ├─ Basis of Opinion — trigger: expert relied on hearsay, lab reports · rule: FRE 703: expert may rely on inadmissible evidence if type reasonably relied on by experts; court may allow disclosure to jury if probative value outweighs prejudicial effect; CEC 802: expert may state reasons including hearsay if of type reasonably relied on
```

## Part 2 — IRAC Rule Blocks (full, memorizable)

### Relevance — Logical (FRE 401 / CEC 210)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence, Hearsay (any admission must first be relevant)*

**Rule.** Evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a fact of consequence to the determination of the action more or less probable than it would be without the evidence. The standard is minimal — "any tendency" is enough. Irrelevant evidence is inadmissible.

**Here.** Here, [the item of evidence] tends to make [the disputed fact] [more/less] probable because [logical connection]. Because that fact is of consequence to [issue in case], the threshold is met.

**Thus.** Thus, the evidence clears the logical relevance threshold and must be assessed for legal relevance.

---

### Relevance — Legal (FRE 403 / CEC 352)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence (propensity), Prior Convictions*

**Rule.** Even if logically relevant, evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, misleading the jury, or considerations of undue delay or waste of time. The rule favors admissibility — exclusion is the exception.

**Here.** Here, [the evidence] has [high/moderate/low] probative value because [reason]. The risk of unfair prejudice is [high/low] because [reason — e.g., gruesome, inflammatory, likely to cause emotional decision]. [The balance tips toward / against] admission.

**Thus.** Thus, the court [should/should not] exclude under 403/CEC 352.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 352 language is nearly identical to FRE 403. Functionally the same. CA courts occasionally apply it more liberally in criminal cases because Prop 8 (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28) requires that relevant evidence be admitted in criminal proceedings, but CEC 352 discretion survives Prop 8.

---

### Judicial Notice (FRE 201 / CEC 451-453)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Public Records hearsay exception*

**Rule.** A court may take judicial notice of an adjudicative fact that is (1) generally known within the trial court's territorial jurisdiction, or (2) capable of accurate and ready determination by resort to sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned. In a civil case the jury must accept a noticed fact as conclusive. In a criminal case the jury may, but is not required to, accept the noticed fact.

**Here.** Here, [the fact — e.g., state capital, business day, geography] is [generally known / accurately determinable from official source], so the court may notice it. [In civil/criminal context, the effect on the jury is….]

**Thus.** Thus, judicial notice [is/is not] proper; in a [civil/criminal] case the jury [must/may] accept it.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 457 — in criminal cases, the jury is instructed that it may but is not required to accept a noticed fact as conclusive, same as FRE. CEC 451 lists mandatory notice (CA statutes, federal law, rules of professional conduct); CEC 452 permissive notice. CA courts may take judicial notice of official government websites and published government reports — a practical distinction from narrow federal practice.

---

### Preliminary Questions of Admissibility (FRE 104 / CEC 400-406)
🟢

**Rule.** The trial judge, not the jury, decides preliminary questions of admissibility (e.g., is a confession voluntary, is a privilege waived, is a witness qualified as an expert). The judge is not bound by rules of evidence except privilege when making these determinations. The jury decides conditional relevance questions (FRE 104(b)) — whether a condition of fact has been satisfied — by a preponderance standard.

**Here.** Here, [the admissibility question — e.g., whether the confession was voluntary, whether the expert is qualified] is a question for the judge under FRE 104(a) / CEC 400. The judge may consider hearsay in that determination.

**Thus.** Thus, the court conducts a 104 hearing outside the jury's presence and rules on admissibility.

---

### Character Evidence — General Bar (FRE 404(a) / CEC 1101)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: MIMIC, Habit, Impeachment*

**Rule.** Evidence of a person's character trait is inadmissible when offered to prove that the person acted in conformity with that trait on the specific occasion at issue. Exceptions apply for: (1) criminal defendant's pertinent character trait (opened by D, rebutted by prosecution); (2) character of alleged victim in criminal cases; (3) homicide — prosecution may offer victim's peaceful character if D claims self-defense; (4) sex offense cases (FRE 412-415).

**Here.** Here, [the proponent] offers [the character evidence] to show that [the party] [acted in conformity with the trait]. This is [the prohibited propensity inference / falls within an exception because…].

**Thus.** Thus, the evidence is [inadmissible as propensity / admissible under exception].

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1101(a) similarly bars propensity evidence. CEC 1101(b) (same as FRE 404(b)) allows non-propensity use. Key CA-specific rules: (1) CEC 1102: criminal defendant may offer own pertinent character trait by reputation or opinion; prosecution may rebut with reputation or opinion — CA does NOT allow specific instances on rebuttal (narrower than some federal circuits). (2) CEC 1103: defendant may offer victim's character for violence in assault cases; prosecution may rebut. (3) CEC 1109: in DV or elder abuse criminal cases, prior acts of domestic violence/elder abuse ARE admissible for propensity — this is a major CA-only exception with no FRE analog.

---

### MIMIC / Prior Bad Acts (FRE 404(b) / CEC 1101(b))
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence, Relevance 403*

**Rule.** Evidence of prior crimes, wrongs, or acts is inadmissible to show propensity but is admissible for another purpose, including: Motive, Intent, knowledge (Mistake/absence of), Identity (modus operandi), and Common plan or scheme. The court must also find probative value not substantially outweighed by prejudice (Rule 403/CEC 352), and on request must give a limiting instruction.

**Here.** Here, [prior act] is offered to prove [the non-propensity purpose — e.g., that D knew the substance was cocaine, not as a mistake; or identity via distinctive MO]. The act [is/is not] sufficiently similar to establish [the purpose]. The 403 balance [tips toward admission/exclusion because…].

**Thus.** Thus, the prior act evidence [is/is not] admissible under 404(b)/CEC 1101(b) for the limited purpose of [proving X], and the jury must be instructed accordingly.

> **CA divergence:** Functionally identical to FRE under CEC 1101(b). However, CA Prop 115 (1990) and CEC 1109 create propensity exceptions for DV/sex cases that have no FRE 404(b) analog — those are separate analyses.

---

### CEC 1109 — Domestic Violence / Sex Offense Propensity (CA ONLY)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence, FRE 413-415*

**Rule.** In a CA criminal case charging domestic violence, elder abuse, or child abuse, evidence of the defendant's prior acts of such abuse is admissible to prove propensity, subject to CEC 352 balancing. Prior acts within 10 years are presumptively admissible; acts older than 10 years require heightened scrutiny. FRE 413-415 provide a similar (but not identical) federal analog for sex offense cases only.

**Here.** Here, D is charged with [DV/elder abuse/child abuse]. The prior act of [description] is offered to show D's propensity for [abuse]. Under CEC 1109, this is permissible if the court finds probative value not substantially outweighed by prejudice. [The act occurred X years ago, so….]

**Thus.** Thus, under CEC 1109 (not available under general FRE 404(a)), the prior act [is/is not] admissible for propensity.

---

### Habit / Routine Practice (FRE 406 / CEC 1105)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence (distinguish)*

**Rule.** Evidence of a person's habit or an organization's routine practice is admissible to prove that conduct on a particular occasion conformed to the habit or routine. Habit is a regular response to a specific situation — more specific and automatic than character. Corroboration and eyewitness are not required.

**Here.** Here, [the evidence] describes [the person's/organization's] [regular, specific conduct] in response to [the specific triggering situation]. This [is/is not] habitual because [it is/is not] specific, regular, and semi-automatic versus a general character trait.

**Thus.** Thus, the habit evidence is admissible to show [party] acted consistently on the occasion in question.

---

### Subsequent Remedial Measures (FRE 407 / CEC 1151)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Relevance 403*

**Rule.** Evidence that a party took measures after an event that, if taken before the event, would have made injury less likely is inadmissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a product defect, or a need for warning. It may be admitted for other purposes: ownership or control (if disputed), feasibility of precautionary measures (if controverted), or impeachment.

**Here.** Here, [D] [repaired/changed policy/fired employee] after the [accident/incident]. [P] offers this to prove [negligence/defect]. This is [barred / permitted for the limited purpose of X because D has controverted feasibility / control].

**Thus.** Thus, the subsequent remedial measure [is/is not] admissible, and if admitted, a limiting instruction is appropriate.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1151 is the California analog; essentially identical. Note that strict products liability is NOT subject to CEC 1151 under CA case law (Ault v. International Harvester Co.) — subsequent remedial measures ARE admissible in strict liability cases in CA. This is a significant CA divergence from FRE 407.

---

### Compromise / Settlement Offers (FRE 408 / CEC 1152)
🟡

**Rule.** Offers to compromise a disputed claim, acceptances of such offers, and statements made during compromise negotiations are inadmissible to prove or disprove validity or amount of a claim or to impeach through inconsistency. Admissible for other purposes: showing bias, proving obstruction of a criminal investigation, or for purposes unrelated to liability.

**Here.** Here, [D's/P's] statement [during settlement talks / as part of an offer] is offered to prove [liability/damages]. This falls squarely within the bar. [Alternatively, it is offered to show [D's] bias/relationship, which is a permitted use.]

**Thus.** Thus, the settlement-related evidence is [inadmissible / admissible for the limited purpose of showing X].

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1152 bars the offer and surrounding conduct/statements. CEC 1154 separately bars evidence of offer to pay medical or similar expenses. Unlike FRE 408, which explicitly protects statements made during compromise negotiations, CA practice is similar but CEC 1152(a) focuses on the offer itself; associated statements are also protected.

---

### Payment of Medical Expenses (FRE 409 / CEC 1152)
🟢

**Rule.** FRE 409: Evidence that a party offered or paid medical, hospital, or similar expenses following an injury is inadmissible to prove liability. Unlike FRE 408, statements accompanying the payment are NOT protected — only the payment itself.

**Here.** Here, [D] [paid / offered to pay] [P's] hospital bills. This offer of payment [is inadmissible to prove negligence]. However, any accompanying statement by D (e.g., "I was going too fast — let me pay your bills") [remains admissible under FRE, though not necessarily under CEC].

**Thus.** Thus, the payment itself [is excluded], but accompanying admissions [may/may not] come in depending on jurisdiction.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1152 protects both the offer to pay and any conduct or statements made in connection with it — broader protection than FRE 409 for accompanying statements.

---

### Plea Bargaining (FRE 410)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Party Admissions*

**Rule.** Evidence of a withdrawn guilty plea, a plea of nolo contendere, any statement made during plea proceedings, or any statement made during plea discussions with a prosecuting attorney is inadmissible against the defendant who made it. Applies only in proceedings against that defendant.

**Here.** Here, [D's] statement during plea negotiations [is offered by the prosecution] to prove [X]. This is excluded under FRE 410 because it was made in the course of plea discussions and [D] did not enter a final plea.

**Thus.** Thus, the plea-related statement is inadmissible.

---

### Authentication (FRE 901-902 / CEC 1400-1454)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Best Evidence Rule*

**Rule.** The proponent of evidence must authenticate it — offer evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims. Methods include: testimony of a witness with knowledge, distinctive characteristics (content, context, reply doctrine), chain of custody, expert comparison, and self-authentication (certified official records, acknowledged instruments, official publications, certified business records under FRE 902(11)).

**Here.** Here, [the item — e.g., email, contract, photograph, recording] must be authenticated. [The proponent offers testimony from X, who [received the email from D's account / recognizes the signature / identifies the photograph].] This [is/is not] sufficient to support a jury finding that the item is what it is claimed to be.

**Thus.** Thus, authentication [is/is not] satisfied, and the item [may/may not] be admitted.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1400-1454 mirror the FRE framework. CEC 1421-1422 permit authentication by content and circumstance (distinctive characteristics). CEC 1550 provides for self-authentication of certified copies of official records. No material divergence on the basic standard.

---

### Best Evidence Rule / Original Writing Rule (FRE 1001-1008 / CEC 1500-1510)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Authentication, Hearsay (recorded recollection)*

**Rule.** To prove the contents of a writing, recording, or photograph, the original must be produced. A duplicate (exact copy) is admissible to the same extent as the original unless (1) there is a genuine question about the original's authenticity, or (2) admission of the duplicate would be unfair. Original not required when: lost/destroyed without bad faith, unobtainable by judicial process, the original is in the opponent's possession and the opponent has not produced it despite notice, or the writing is not closely related to a controlling issue.

**Here.** Here, [party] seeks to prove [the contents of X document] through [oral testimony / a photocopy]. [The original is [unavailable because / available but not produced].] [Duplicate admissibility is [proper/improper] because [no question of authenticity / no unfairness].

**Thus.** Thus, the [original / duplicate / secondary evidence] [is/is not] properly admitted.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1500 adopts the "secondary evidence rule" — any secondary evidence of writing contents is admissible unless (1) there is genuine dispute about material terms of the writing and it would be unfair to admit secondary evidence, or (2) admission of secondary evidence would be unfair. This is more permissive than FRE — the opponent bears a heavier burden to exclude. CA eliminated the "best evidence" terminology.

---

### Hearsay — Definition and Non-Hearsay Uses (FRE 801 / CEC 1200)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Party Admissions, Prior Statements, Confrontation Clause*

**Rule.** Hearsay is an out-of-court statement made by a declarant, offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Not offered for truth (non-hearsay): verbal act / operative words (offer, defamation), statement offered to show effect on listener (notice, knowledge), and legally operative words. Prior statements and party admissions are separately treated.

**Here.** Here, [declarant] stated [X] out of court. [P/D] offers this to prove [X is true / not for truth, but to show that recipient was on notice / that the words themselves had legal effect]. [This is / is not] hearsay because [it is / is not] offered for the truth of the matter asserted.

**Thus.** Thus, the statement [is hearsay and must qualify under an exception / is not hearsay and is admissible].

---

### Prior Inconsistent Statements — Substantive Use (FRE 801(d)(1)(A) / CEC 1235)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Impeachment (PIS), Confrontation Clause*

**Rule.** FRE 801(d)(1)(A): a prior inconsistent statement is excluded from hearsay (treated as non-hearsay) only if made under oath at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition, and the declarant now testifies and is subject to cross-examination. If not under oath, it may still be used for impeachment but not as substantive evidence.

**Here.** Here, [witness] made a prior statement [at deposition / in a letter / in an interview] that contradicts current trial testimony. Under FRE, this is [substantive / impeachment only] because it was [made / not made] under oath at a prior proceeding.

**Thus.** Thus, the prior statement is [non-hearsay admissible as substantive evidence / admissible for impeachment only].

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1235 is broader: any prior inconsistent statement of a witness who testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination is admissible as substantive evidence, regardless of whether made under oath. This is one of the most-tested CA/FRE splits.

---

### Party Admissions / Opposing Party Statements (FRE 801(d)(2) / CEC 1220)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Hearsay, Vicarious Admissions*

**Rule.** FRE 801(d)(2): a statement is not hearsay if offered against the opposing party and it is (1) the party's own statement; (2) a statement the party adopted; (3) made by a person the party authorized to speak; (4) made by the party's agent/employee on a matter within the scope of the agency/employment during the relationship; or (5) made by a co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy. No personal knowledge requirement; no trustworthiness requirement.

**Here.** Here, [party's] out-of-court statement [X] is offered against that party. It [is the party's own statement / was made by D's employee about a matter within the scope of employment]. No hearsay exception needed.

**Thus.** Thus, the statement is excluded from hearsay as an opposing party statement and is admissible.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1220-1228 treats party admissions as hearsay exceptions (not exclusions) but the substantive result is identical. CEC 1222 covers authorized admissions; CEC 1223 covers co-conspirator statements. The label "exception" vs. "exclusion" has no practical effect in CA essay answers, but use CEC numbering to show CA knowledge.

---

### Present Sense Impression (FRE 803(1) / CEC 1241)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Excited Utterance (distinguish)*

**Rule.** A statement describing or explaining an event or condition, made while the declarant was perceiving the event or condition or immediately thereafter, is admissible regardless of the declarant's availability. No requirement of excitement or stress.

**Here.** Here, [declarant] stated [X] [while watching / immediately after witnessing] [the event]. The statement describes that event and was made [contemporaneously / within moments], satisfying the immediacy requirement. No showing of excitement is needed.

**Thus.** Thus, the statement qualifies as a present sense impression and is not excluded by the hearsay rule.

---

### Excited Utterance (FRE 803(2) / CEC 1240)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Present Sense Impression, State of Mind*

**Rule.** A statement relating to a startling event or condition, made while the declarant was still under the stress of excitement caused by the event, is admissible regardless of declarant's availability. The key inquiry is whether the declarant was still under the stress of the startling event when the statement was made; time elapsed is relevant but not dispositive.

**Here.** Here, [declarant] made the statement [time frame] after [the startling event]. [The statement relates to the event.] The question is whether [declarant] remained under the stress of excitement — [factors: still shaking / crying / at the scene / or, conversely, had time to reflect and fabricate].

**Thus.** Thus, the statement [qualifies / does not qualify] as an excited utterance.

---

### State of Mind (FRE 803(3) / CEC 1250-1252)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Hearsay, Hillmon doctrine*

**Rule.** A statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind (intent, plan, motive, emotion, mental feeling), or physical sensation (pain, bodily health) is admissible. A statement of memory or belief is not admissible to prove the fact remembered or believed — except in a will contest. The Hillmon doctrine: a statement of intent to do something in the future is admissible to show the declarant acted in conformity with the intent (and, in most courts, that a third person did so as well).

**Here.** Here, [declarant's] statement "I plan to [meet X]" [or "I am afraid of D"] is [a statement of then-existing intent / emotion] admissible under 803(3). [Alternatively, the statement "I believe X caused the accident" is a statement of belief about a past fact — not admissible under this exception.]

**Thus.** Thus, the state-of-mind exception [applies / does not apply].

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1250 admits statements of mental or physical state (same as FRE 803(3)). CEC 1252 bars statements of memory/belief to prove past fact, same as FRE. No material divergence.

---

### Medical Diagnosis or Treatment (FRE 803(4) / CEC 1253)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Hearsay, Physician-Patient Privilege*

**Rule.** Statements made for the purpose of medical diagnosis or treatment describing medical history, symptoms, pain, sensations, or the inception or general cause of the condition are admissible. The statement must be reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment. Under FRE, this extends to statements made to a physician consulted solely for purposes of testifying at trial (consulting expert). Statements attributing fault are generally inadmissible.

**Here.** Here, [the patient's] statement to [the doctor/nurse/EMT] — "[I have chest pain / I was hit by a car]" — was made for purposes of treatment. The cause (being hit) is pertinent because it directs the type of treatment. However, "the red car hit me, not the blue one" [goes to fault and is not pertinent to treatment].

**Thus.** Thus, the statement [qualifies / does not qualify] under the medical diagnosis exception.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1253 is narrower than FRE 803(4): the statement must be for purposes of diagnosis or treatment by a treating physician or health care provider. CA courts generally exclude statements made solely to a consulting expert for litigation purposes under CEC 1253 (though such statements may still be relied on under CEC 802 as a basis for expert opinion).

---

### Recorded Recollection (FRE 803(5) / CEC 1237)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Refreshing Recollection (FRE 612 — not a hearsay exception)*

**Rule.** A memorandum or record concerning a matter about which a witness once had knowledge but now has insufficient memory to testify fully is admissible if: (1) the record was made or adopted by the witness; (2) when the matter was fresh in the witness's memory; and (3) the record accurately reflects the witness's knowledge. Under FRE, the record is read into evidence but not received as an exhibit unless offered by the adverse party.

**Here.** Here, [witness] cannot recall [the details]. [The witness] [created / reviewed and verified] the [document] shortly after [the event], when memory was fresh. The [document] accurately reflects [witness's] then-existing knowledge. It [is read aloud / if offered as exhibit, only adverse party may do so].

**Thus.** Thus, the recorded recollection exception [applies / does not apply].

---

### Business Records (FRE 803(6) / CEC 1271)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Hearsay, Authentication*

**Rule.** A record of a regularly conducted activity is admissible if: (1) made at or near the time of the act, event, or condition; (2) by a person with knowledge or from information transmitted by such a person; (3) kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity; (4) making such records is a regular practice of the business; (5) established by a qualified custodian or other qualified witness or by certification under FRE 902(11). Excluded if source of information or circumstances of preparation indicate lack of trustworthiness.

**Here.** Here, [the record — hospital chart, business log, email chain] was [created/maintained] by [the business]. [The custodian testifies it was made at or near the time of [the event] by someone with personal knowledge, in the regular course of business.] [The opponent argues the record lacks trustworthiness because [it was created in anticipation of litigation / contains double hearsay from a non-business source].]

**Thus.** Thus, the business record [is / is not] admissible; any embedded hearsay from an outside source must independently qualify.

---

### Public Records (FRE 803(8) / CEC 1280)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Business Records, Confrontation Clause*

**Rule.** Records, reports, and statements of public agencies are admissible if setting out: (A) activities of the office; (B) matters observed under legal duty to report (excluding, in criminal cases, matters observed by law enforcement); or (C) factual findings from a legally authorized investigation in civil cases or against the government in criminal cases. Excluded if circumstances indicate lack of trustworthiness.

**Here.** Here, [the government report] contains [factual findings / law enforcement observations]. In [a civil case / against D in criminal case], the [factual findings] portion [is admissible]. The [police officer's field observations] in a criminal prosecution against D [are excluded under FRE 803(8)(B)].

**Thus.** Thus, [the relevant portion of the public record is / is not] admissible.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1280 admits public records if made by a public employee acting in the scope of duty, near the time of the act, and conditions of preparation indicate trustworthiness. CEC 1280 does not contain the same law enforcement restriction as FRE 803(8)(B) — CA courts may admit law enforcement records more readily, subject to CEC 352 and Confrontation Clause analysis.

---

### Former Testimony (FRE 804(b)(1) / CEC 1291)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Confrontation Clause, Unavailability*

**Rule.** Former testimony is admissible if: (1) the declarant is unavailable (dead, privileged, refuses to testify, memory failure, absent despite reasonable process); (2) the testimony was given at a trial, hearing, or deposition; (3) the party against whom it is now offered had an opportunity and similar motive to develop the testimony by direct, cross, or redirect examination. In civil cases, a predecessor in interest satisfies the similar-motive requirement.

**Here.** Here, [witness] is unavailable because [dead/invokes privilege/cannot be found despite subpoena]. [Witness] testified at [prior trial/deposition]. [The party opposing admission] had [the opportunity and similar motive] to cross-examine at the prior proceeding because [the issues and stakes were substantially similar].

**Thus.** Thus, the former testimony is [admissible as a hearsay exception / inadmissible because the motive or opportunity was lacking].

---

### Dying Declaration (FRE 804(b)(2) / CEC 1242)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Confrontation Clause (not testimonial under Giles)*

**Rule.** FRE 804(b)(2): A statement made by a declarant who believed death was imminent, concerning the cause or circumstances of the believed impending death, is admissible in homicide prosecutions and civil cases. Declarant must be unavailable (but need not have actually died). CA (CEC 1242): admissible in any action (not limited to homicide); declarant need not actually die.

**Here.** Here, [declarant] stated "[X]" while [in hospital / on the ground] with [wounds] believing death was imminent. The statement describes [the cause/circumstances of injury]. Declarant [has/has not] died. [In CA, admissible in any action; under FRE, admissible only if it is a homicide prosecution or civil case.]

**Thus.** Thus, the dying declaration [qualifies / does not qualify] under this exception.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 1242 is broader than FRE 804(b)(2): (1) applies in any action, not just homicide and civil; (2) declarant need not have actually died. Both require the declarant's belief that death was imminent.

---

### Statement Against Interest (FRE 804(b)(3) / CEC 1230)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Dying Declaration, Unavailability*

**Rule.** A statement made by an unavailable declarant that was, at the time of making, so contrary to the declarant's pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest that a reasonable person would not have made it unless believing it to be true. If offered in a criminal case to exculpate the defendant, the statement must be supported by corroborating circumstances that clearly indicate trustworthiness. Collateral self-serving statements packaged with the against-interest statement are not covered.

**Here.** Here, [the declarant] stated [X], which would subject declarant to [criminal liability / civil liability / financial loss]. The declarant is unavailable because [reason]. [The statement is offered to exculpate D, so corroboration of trustworthiness must be shown — [corroborating facts].]

**Thus.** Thus, the statement against interest [qualifies / does not qualify] as a hearsay exception.

---

### Forfeiture by Wrongdoing (FRE 804(b)(6) / CEC 1390)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Confrontation Clause, DV cases*

**Rule.** A party who intentionally caused the unavailability of a declarant to prevent the declarant from testifying forfeits the right to object to the declarant's out-of-court statements on hearsay grounds. Under Giles v. California (2008), intent to procure the absence to prevent testimony is required — mere killing of the declarant does not automatically forfeit the objection absent evidence of that specific intent. CEC 1390 codifies similar doctrine.

**Here.** Here, [D is alleged to have threatened/killed witness] to prevent [witness from testifying]. [The prosecution must show D acted with intent to prevent testimony — not merely intent to harm.] [Evidence of prior DV and threats, even if testimonial, may be admissible to show this pattern.]

**Thus.** Thus, forfeiture by wrongdoing [applies / does not apply]; if it applies, both hearsay and Confrontation objections are forfeited.

---

### Confrontation Clause (Crawford v. Washington; Davis v. Washington)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Hearsay Exceptions, Forfeiture, Prior Testimony*

**Rule.** The Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause bars admission of testimonial hearsay against a criminal defendant unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. Testimonial: formal police interrogation, prior trial testimony, affidavits, certificates (Melendez-Diaz), and statements made with primary purpose of creating evidence for use at trial. Non-testimonial: statements made to address an ongoing emergency (911 calls, statements to first responders while emergency ongoing). Non-testimonial hearsay is governed only by hearsay rules, not the Confrontation Clause.

**Here.** Here, [the out-of-court statement] was made [during police interrogation / to 911 / in a lab report]. [Was the primary purpose creating a record for prosecution or addressing an ongoing emergency?] [If testimonial, D had / did not have a prior opportunity to cross-examine.]

**Thus.** Thus, the Confrontation Clause [bars / does not bar] admission of this testimonial/non-testimonial statement.

> **CA divergence:** The Confrontation Clause applies in CA criminal cases through the 14th Amendment. CEC has no independent Confrontation analog, but CA courts are bound by Crawford. Prop 8 does not override constitutional exclusions. Lab reports and certificates of forensic analysis are testimonial under Melendez-Diaz; the analyst must testify or a Crawford-compliant substitute must be provided.

---

### Impeachment — Prior Inconsistent Statements (FRE 613 / CEC 770)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Hearsay 801(d)(1)(A)/CEC 1235 (substantive use)*

**Rule.** A witness may be impeached by showing a prior statement inconsistent with current testimony. Under FRE 613, extrinsic evidence of the prior statement is allowed only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny (at any time during the proceeding). Under CEC 770, extrinsic evidence requires that the witness have been given an opportunity to explain, unless the court finds in the interests of justice to excuse the requirement.

**Here.** Here, [witness] testified [X] at trial but [stated Y] in [a prior statement]. [The prior statement is inconsistent.] [Extrinsic evidence — the written statement / deposition — may be offered after the witness has been given a chance to explain.]

**Thus.** Thus, impeachment by prior inconsistent statement [is/is not] proper; the statement [is also / is not also] admissible substantively under [FRE 801(d)(1)(A) if under oath / CEC 1235 regardless].

---

### Impeachment — Bias, Interest, Motive
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: FRE 607-608*

**Rule.** A witness may always be impeached by showing bias, interest, or motive to lie — e.g., financial interest in the outcome, prior relationship, plea deal, employment by a party. No bar on extrinsic evidence of bias; the collateral matter rule does not apply. This is universally considered the most powerful impeachment.

**Here.** Here, [witness] [received a plea deal / is employed by D / has a financial interest in the outcome]. This establishes a motive to [favor / lie for / against] [party]. Extrinsic evidence (e.g., the plea agreement) is admissible.

**Thus.** Thus, bias impeachment is permissible, and extrinsic evidence may be offered.

---

### Impeachment — Prior Conviction (FRE 609 / CEC 788 / Prop 8)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence, Confrontation Clause*

**Rule.** FRE 609(a)(1): a prior felony conviction of any witness is admissible to attack credibility if (for a non-defendant witness) the court determines probative value is not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice; (for the defendant in a criminal case) if probative value outweighs prejudicial effect to defendant. FRE 609(a)(2): a conviction for any crime involving dishonesty or false statement (crimen falsi) is automatically admissible regardless of grade. Convictions older than 10 years have heightened scrutiny.

**Here.** Here, [witness/D] has a prior conviction for [felony/crimen falsi crime — perjury/fraud]. [For a crimen falsi conviction, admission is automatic.] [For a general felony, the court balances — for D, the defense prejudice weighs heavily.]

**Thus.** Thus, the prior conviction [is automatically / subject to balancing / inadmissible] for impeachment.

> **CA divergence:** Prop 8 (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)): in CA criminal cases, any prior felony conviction is admissible to impeach any witness, including the defendant, subject only to CEC 352 balancing. CA does NOT use FRE 609's automatic crimen falsi rule — all prior felony convictions go through CEC 352. This is a high-frequency tested issue: in CA, it is harder to exclude prior felony convictions for impeachment than under FRE because Prop 8 eliminates many of FRE 609's categorical bars.

---

### Impeachment — Character for Untruthfulness (FRE 608 / CEC 786-790)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Character Evidence*

**Rule.** Opinion or reputation evidence about a witness's character for untruthfulness is admissible to attack credibility. A character witness may also be cross-examined on specific instances of the witness's untruthful conduct. Extrinsic evidence of specific untruthful acts is not permitted — the questioner is bound by the answer. A witness's character for truthfulness may be supported only after the character for truthfulness has been attacked.

**Here.** Here, [the impeaching party] calls [character witness] to testify to [W's] reputation for untruthfulness in the community. [Alternatively, on cross of W, counsel asks about a specific dishonest act — this is permissible but W's denial ends the inquiry; no extrinsic proof of the specific act is allowed.]

**Thus.** Thus, [the reputation/opinion testimony is admissible / the specific-act question may be asked but extrinsic evidence is barred].

---

### Rehabilitation
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Impeachment*

**Rule.** After impeachment, a witness's credibility may be rehabilitated by: (1) a prior consistent statement to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper influence (FRE 801(d)(1)(B)) — the statement must predate the motive to fabricate; (2) character evidence of truthfulness, but only after character for truthfulness has been attacked; (3) explanation of inconsistency. Bolstering (prior consistent statement before any attack) is improper.

**Here.** Here, [D's counsel] offers [W's prior consistent statement] after [opposing counsel] charged that [W fabricated the story for a specific reason]. The prior statement [was / was not] made before [the alleged motive to fabricate arose]. [If before, it is admissible as rehabilitation and as substantive non-hearsay under FRE 801(d)(1)(B).]

**Thus.** Thus, rehabilitation [is/is not] proper here.

### Attorney-Client Privilege
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Work Product Doctrine (not an evidence rule), Privileges*

**Rule.** Confidential communications between an attorney and client, made for the purpose of seeking or providing legal advice, are privileged. The client holds the privilege and may prevent disclosure. The privilege protects the communication, not the underlying facts. Elements: (1) attorney-client relationship; (2) communication; (3) confidential (not intended to be disclosed to third parties); (4) for purpose of legal advice. Exceptions: crime-fraud (communication made to facilitate a crime or fraud); breach of duty between attorney and client; communication at issue in a dispute between them.

**Here.** Here, [client] communicated [X] to [attorney] in [a meeting / email]. The communication was [confidential — no third parties present / made in confidence]. It was for the purpose of [seeking legal advice about Y]. [The crime-fraud exception [does/does not] apply because [client sought advice to further an ongoing crime].]

**Thus.** Thus, the communication [is/is not] privileged.

> **CA divergence:** CA Evidence Code §§ 950-962 codify attorney-client privilege. Substantively similar to FRE and common law. CA defines "confidential communication" to include advice given by the attorney as well as the client's disclosure — broader than some federal formulations. The crime-fraud exception (CEC 956) requires that the communication was sought to enable a crime or fraud.

---

### Spousal Privileges — Testimonial / Anti-Marital Facts
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Spousal Confidential Communications (distinguish)*

**Rule.** The testimonial (anti-marital facts) privilege allows one spouse to refuse to testify adversely against the other. Under the federal rule (Trammel v. United States): the witness-spouse alone holds the privilege and can choose to testify or refuse; the defendant-spouse cannot block the witness-spouse's voluntary testimony. CA (CEC 970): either spouse may refuse to testify against the other in a criminal proceeding; the witness-spouse's consent alone is insufficient if the other spouse invokes the privilege. In civil cases, both spouses must agree.

**Here.** Here, [W, D's spouse] is called to testify against [D] in a [criminal / civil] case. Under federal law, [W] can choose to testify; [D] cannot prevent it. Under CA law in a criminal case, [D] may invoke CEC 970 to prevent [W] from testifying adverse to [D] even if [W] is willing.

**Thus.** Thus, [under federal law / under CA law], the testimonial privilege [is/is not] available to block [W's] testimony.

> **CA divergence:** This is the biggest spousal privilege divergence. Under Trammel (federal), only the witness-spouse decides. Under CEC 970, either spouse can refuse (criminal) — defendant-spouse retains a veto right that federal law eliminated in Trammel.

---

### Spousal Privileges — Confidential Marital Communications
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Testimonial Spousal Privilege (distinguish)*

**Rule.** Communications made in confidence between spouses during a valid marriage are privileged. Either spouse holds the privilege and may refuse to disclose, and may prevent the other from disclosing. The privilege survives divorce. Exceptions: communications in furtherance of a joint crime or fraud; crimes against children; crimes against the other spouse. Requires a valid marriage at the time of the communication.

**Here.** Here, [spouse A] made [a private statement] to [spouse B] during their marriage. [No third parties were present, and the communication was made in confidence.] [The crime against children / joint crime exception [does/does not] apply.]

**Thus.** Thus, the confidential marital communication [is / is not] privileged, and [the spouse / neither spouse] may be compelled to disclose it.

> **CA divergence:** CEC 980-987 codify the confidential marital communications privilege in CA. Functionally the same as federal common law but explicitly codified. CA has separate statutes for the two spousal privileges (CEC 970 and CEC 980), making them easier to distinguish on a CA exam.

---

### Physician-Patient Privilege (CA Only)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Psychotherapist-Patient Privilege, Medical Diagnosis Hearsay Exception*

**Rule.** CA CEC 994: a patient may refuse to disclose, and may prevent a physician from disclosing, any confidential communication between patient and physician made in the course of a physician-patient relationship. The patient holds the privilege. Exceptions: patient-litigant exception (CEC 996 — patient puts condition in issue, privilege is waived as to that condition); criminal proceeding exception for certain offenses; public interest exception (communicable diseases, gunshot wounds).

**Here.** Here, [D/P] communicated [medical information] to [Dr. X] in the course of treatment. [D/P] holds the physician-patient privilege. [The patient-litigant exception [does/does not] apply because [the patient/plaintiff has placed their physical condition at issue in this lawsuit].]

**Thus.** Thus, the communication [is privileged / the privilege is waived under the patient-litigant exception].

> **CA divergence:** There is NO federal physician-patient privilege in federal question cases. The FRE explicitly defers to state law in diversity actions (FRE 501), so CA privilege applies in diversity cases in federal court. In federal question cases, no physician-patient privilege exists. This is a major and highly tested CA-specific rule.

---

### Psychotherapist-Patient Privilege (FRE/Federal + CEC 1010-1026)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Physician-Patient Privilege (distinguish)*

**Rule.** Jaffee v. Redmond (1996): federal courts recognize a psychotherapist-patient privilege covering confidential communications between a patient and a licensed psychiatrist, psychologist, or licensed clinical social worker. CA CEC 1010-1026: parallel privilege. Patient holds the privilege. Exceptions: patient-litigant; dangerous patient (when patient presents serious threat of harm to a reasonably identifiable victim — Tarasoff reporting obligation does not itself destroy the privilege, but disclosure in compliance with Tarasoff duty is not a privilege violation).

**Here.** Here, [the patient] made disclosures to [the therapist] in the course of treatment. [The patient-litigant exception [does/does not] apply.] [Tarasoff duty to warn [does/does not] convert the therapist's disclosure into an evidentiary privilege waiver — the compelled reporting and the testimonial privilege are distinct.]

**Thus.** Thus, the psychotherapist-patient privilege [applies / does not apply].

### Lay Opinion (FRE 701 / CEC 800)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Expert Opinion*

**Rule.** A lay witness may give opinion testimony if: (1) rationally based on the witness's personal perception; (2) helpful to clearly understanding the testimony or determining a fact in issue; and (3) not based on scientific, technical, or specialized knowledge that would require expert qualification. Common lay opinions: speed of a vehicle, intoxication, emotional state, identity (voice, appearance), handwriting (if familiar), and value of one's own property.

**Here.** Here, [lay witness] testifies that [the car was going fast / D seemed drunk / the voice on the recording sounded like D]. This opinion is [rationally based on personal observation] and [does not require specialized expertise].

**Thus.** Thus, the lay opinion [is / is not] admissible.

---

### Expert Opinion — Daubert (FRE 702) vs. Kelly/Frye (CA)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Lay Opinion, Basis of Opinion*

**Rule.** FRE 702 (Daubert standard): expert testimony is admissible if (1) the expert has sufficient qualifications (knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education); (2) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data; (3) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods; and (4) the expert has reliably applied the methods to the facts of the case. The trial judge acts as gatekeeper. Daubert factors (non-exhaustive): testing and peer review, known error rate, general acceptance in the field.

**Here.** Here, [expert] offers an opinion on [DNA match / accident reconstruction / psychiatric diagnosis]. [Under Daubert, the court examines whether the methodology is reliable — tested, peer-reviewed, with a known error rate, generally accepted.] [Under Kelly/Frye (CA), the sole inquiry for novel science is whether the method is generally accepted in the relevant scientific community.]

**Thus.** Thus, [under Daubert / under CA Kelly/Frye], the expert opinion [is / is not] admissible.

> **CA divergence:** CA uses the Kelly/Frye standard (People v. Kelly (1976)), which requires only that a novel scientific technique be generally accepted in the relevant scientific community. CA courts do NOT apply Daubert's multi-factor reliability test. For established science, neither Kelly nor Daubert requires a gatekeeping hearing — the standard only applies to "new" or "novel" scientific techniques. This is one of the highest-frequency CA divergences in evidence essays; always flag it when expert testimony involves forensic science, DNA, polygraph, bite marks, or any emerging methodology.

---

### Expert Opinion — Basis and Ultimate Issue (FRE 703-704 / CEC 801-805)
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Expert Opinion (Daubert/Kelly)*

**Rule.** An expert may base an opinion on facts or data perceived, personally known, or made known at or before the hearing — including inadmissible evidence if it is of the type reasonably relied on by experts in the field (FRE 703). Under FRE 705, an expert may state an opinion without first disclosing the underlying basis, subject to cross-examination. Experts may testify to ultimate issues (FRE 704), except that in criminal cases no expert may state an opinion about whether a defendant did or did not have a mental state that constitutes an element of the crime charged or a defense.

**Here.** Here, [expert] relied on [lab reports / medical records / hearsay statements from other experts] in forming an opinion. These [are / are not] of the type reasonably relied on by experts in this field. [If not admissible for the truth, the court may exclude disclosure to the jury if prejudice outweighs probative value.]

**Thus.** Thus, the expert's opinion [is / is not] properly based, and [the underlying basis may / may not] be disclosed to the jury.

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:** Evidence almost always appears with Criminal Law (hearsay, Confrontation, impeachment of D), Torts (subsequent remedial measures, habit, expert opinion on causation), and Family Law (spousal privilege, physician-patient privilege in custody/support disputes). Constitutional Law crossovers arise with the Confrontation Clause and Fourth Amendment (suppression — a separate doctrine, not the same as evidence exclusion).

- **Sleeper issues:** (1) CEC 1235 broader prior inconsistent statement rule — examinees default to FRE 801(d)(1)(A)'s oath requirement and miss that CA treats any prior inconsistent statement as substantive. (2) CEC 1109 domestic violence propensity — frequently tested in DV criminal essays; examinees miss that CA has a propensity exception FRE lacks. (3) No federal physician-patient privilege — examinees assume the privilege exists everywhere. (4) Ault doctrine — subsequent remedial measures ARE admissible in CA strict products liability cases; examinees apply FRE 407 blindly. (5) Giles v. California intent requirement for forfeiture — examinees assume killing the witness always forfeits the objection.

- **CA-divergence cheat list:**
  - CEC 1235: any prior inconsistent statement is substantive (no oath requirement; FRE requires oath at prior proceeding)
  - CEC 970 spousal testimonial privilege: either spouse holds (Trammel: witness-spouse only, federally)
  - CEC 994 physician-patient privilege: exists in CA; does not exist in federal question cases
  - CEC 1109: prior DV/sex/elder abuse acts admissible for propensity (no FRE 404(a) analog)
  - CA Kelly/Frye: general acceptance standard for novel science; Daubert multi-factor test does not apply
  - Ault: subsequent remedial measures admissible in CA strict products liability (FRE 407 bars them)
  - Prop 8 / CEC 788: prior felony convictions broadly admissible to impeach in CA criminal cases; less restrictive than FRE 609
  - CEC secondary evidence rule: more permissive than FRE best evidence (FRE 1002/1003)
  - Dying declaration CEC 1242: applies in any action; declarant need not have died (FRE: homicide + civil only)

- **Call-of-the-question patterns:**
  - "What objections might be raised, and how should the court rule?" — Analyze hearsay, relevance, authentication, privilege, Confrontation in order for each piece of evidence.
  - "Discuss the admissibility of…" — IRAC each item; always note the CA/FRE split.
  - "What evidence, if any, could the prosecution introduce to rebut D's character evidence?" — Identify what D opened the door to; apply CEC 1102/FRE 404(a)(1) rebuttal.
  - "Can [witness] be compelled to testify?" — Privilege analysis (spousal, attorney-client, physician-patient).
  - "The defense moved to suppress…" — This signals Fourth/Fifth Amendment, not an evidence rule issue; address suppression separately but note that Prop 8 limits exclusionary rule in CA (non-constitutional violations may not require exclusion).
