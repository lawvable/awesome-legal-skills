# Real Property — Essay Bank

> Format spec: original prose throughout. Flag splits with `> **CA divergence:**` and
> uncertain law with `> ⚠️ VERIFY`.

**High-frequency note:** Real Property appears as a full standalone or paired with
Contracts (land sale K) or Trusts/Wills (future interests, co-tenancy). The hidden
issue is almost always a CA divergence — adverse possession tax-payment requirement,
lien-theory mortgage, race-notice recording, or trustee-sale foreclosure.

### Present Estates — Fee Simple Absolute
🟢 · *cross-ref: Wills/Trusts (testamentary transfers)*

**Rule.** A fee simple absolute is the largest estate in land, conveyed by words like "to A" or "to A and her heirs." It has unlimited duration, no future interest, and is freely devisable, descendible, and alienable. No conditions can automatically divest it.

**Here.** Here, the conveyance read "[to A]," which [contains/lacks] durational or conditional language; [therefore the estate is/is not] a fee simple absolute because [no condition follows].

**Thus.** Thus, A holds a fee simple absolute with no reversionary interest in the grantor.

---

### Present Estates — Fee Simple Determinable
🟡 · *cross-ref: Future Interests (possibility of reverter)*

**Rule.** A fee simple determinable is created by durational language — "so long as," "while," "until," or "during." The estate automatically terminates and reverts to the grantor the instant the stated condition ceases to be satisfied, without any act by the grantor. The grantor retains a possibility of reverter.

**Here.** Here, the deed conveyed land "so long as [condition]," which [is/is not] durational language; if [condition] ceased, the estate [would/would not] automatically revert to [grantor] without any action required.

**Thus.** Thus, [grantee] holds a fee simple determinable, and [grantor] holds a possibility of reverter.

---

### Present Estates — Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent
🟡 · *cross-ref: Future Interests (right of entry)*

**Rule.** A fee simple subject to a condition subsequent arises from language like "but if," "on condition that," or "provided however, that." Unlike the determinable fee, the estate does NOT automatically terminate; it continues until the grantor exercises the right of entry (power of termination). Without affirmative exercise, the grantee's estate persists.

**Here.** Here, the conveyance said "but if [event occurs]," which is conditional rather than durational; the estate did not automatically end when [event] occurred — [grantor] must [re-enter/act] to reclaim title.

**Thus.** Thus, [grantee]'s estate continues until [grantor] exercises the right of entry; failure to act in a timely manner may extinguish that right.

### Present Estates — Fee Simple Subject to Executory Limitation
🟡 · *cross-ref: Future Interests (executory interest); RAP*

**Rule.** A fee simple subject to an executory limitation is defeasible in favor of a third party (not the grantor). Language like "to A, but if X occurs, then to B" creates this estate. Upon the triggering event, A's interest is automatically cut short and title shifts to B, who holds a shifting executory interest. RAP applies to B's interest.

**Here.** Here, the grant provided "to A, but if [condition], then to B," creating a defeasible fee in A and [a shifting executory interest/springing executory interest] in B; RAP [voids/saves] B's interest because [analysis].

**Thus.** Thus, [A holds the fee subject to divestment; B's executory interest is/is not valid under RAP].

### Present Estates — Life Estate & Waste
🟡 · *cross-ref: Future Interests (reversion/remainder)*

**Rule.** A life estate endures for the measuring life, either the life tenant's own life ("to A for life") or another's ("to A for the life of B" — estate pur autre vie). The life tenant may use the property but must not commit waste. Affirmative waste = voluntary destruction or exploitation beyond normal use. Permissive waste = failure to maintain (taxes, interest, ordinary repairs). Ameliorative waste = changes that increase value — permitted only if all future interest holders consent or changed conditions make the original use no longer suitable.

**Here.** Here, [A] held a life estate and [extracted minerals/allowed roof to collapse/demolished a building], which constitutes [affirmative/permissive/ameliorative] waste because [the act went beyond normal use/failed maintenance/altered the property's character].

**Thus.** Thus, the remainderman [may/may not] seek damages or an injunction for [type] waste.

> **CA divergence:** California codifies the waste doctrine but courts give ameliorative waste more flexibility when the change reflects the property's highest and best use and the remainderman's interest is adequately protected.

### Future Interests — Reversion
🟢 · *cross-ref: Life Estate; Landlord-Tenant*

**Rule.** A reversion is a future interest retained by the grantor whenever the grantor conveys an estate of lesser quantum than the grantor held. It arises automatically — no express reservation required. A reversion is always vested and not subject to RAP.

**Here.** Here, O conveyed a life estate (or a term of years) to A, retaining no express future interest; by operation of law, O holds a reversion because O conveyed less than a fee simple.

**Thus.** Thus, upon termination of the life estate (or term), title reverts to O automatically.

---

### Future Interests — Possibility of Reverter
🟢 · *cross-ref: Fee Simple Determinable*

**Rule.** A possibility of reverter is the future interest retained by the grantor following a fee simple determinable. It vests automatically when the durational condition fails, with no action required. It is descendible and devisable but traditionally not alienable inter vivos (many modern courts and statutes allow transfer).

**Here.** Here, O conveyed to A "so long as [condition]"; O's possibility of reverter [vested/did not vest] because [condition] [ceased/did not cease].

**Thus.** Thus, [title reverted to O automatically / O's possibility of reverter has not yet triggered].

---

### Future Interests — Right of Entry (Power of Termination)
🟢 · *cross-ref: Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent*

**Rule.** A right of entry (also called power of termination) is the future interest retained by the grantor following a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent. The estate does not end automatically; the grantor must affirmatively exercise the right of re-entry. Failure to act promptly may result in loss of the right by waiver, estoppel, or the statute of limitations.

**Here.** Here, O held a right of entry after the "but if" condition was [met/broken]; O [did/did not] re-enter or provide notice, which means the estate [continues/has been terminated].

**Thus.** Thus, [grantee's estate persists / O successfully terminated the estate].

### Future Interests — Vested Remainder
🟢 · *cross-ref: Contingent Remainder; RAP*

**Rule.** A remainder is a future interest in a third party that takes effect in possession upon the natural termination of the preceding estate. A remainder is vested if: (1) the holder is ascertained, and (2) no condition precedent stands between the holder and possession (other than the natural end of the prior estate). Vested remainders are not subject to RAP (unless subject to open). A vested remainder subject to open (class gift) is subject to RAP as to unborn class members.

**Here.** Here, "to A for life, then to B" gives B a vested remainder because B [is a living, identified person] and [no condition precedes B's interest other than A's death].

**Thus.** Thus, B's vested remainder [is/is not] subject to RAP and [will/will not] pass to B's estate if B predeceases A.

### Future Interests — Contingent Remainder
🟡 · *cross-ref: Executory Interests; RAP*

**Rule.** A contingent remainder exists when either (1) the taker is unascertained at the time of creation, or (2) a condition precedent (other than the natural end of the prior estate) must be satisfied before the remainder can become possessory. Contingent remainders ARE subject to RAP. Under common law, a contingent remainder was destroyed if it failed to vest before or at the termination of the supporting life estate (destructibility rule — abolished in most states).

**Here.** Here, the conveyance "to A for life, then to B if B has graduated" creates a contingent remainder in B because [graduation is a condition precedent / B is unascertained]; RAP [voids/validates] it because [a life in being could determine vesting within 21 years / there is a possibility the condition does not resolve within the perpetuities period].

**Thus.** Thus, B's interest [is/is not] valid under RAP and [will/will not] vest upon [condition].

> **CA divergence:** California has abolished the destructibility of contingent remainders (Prob. Code § 21103 and related provisions). CA also applies the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities (wait-and-see, 90-year period).

### Future Interests — Executory Interest
🟡 · *cross-ref: Defeasible Fees; RAP*

**Rule.** An executory interest is a future interest in a third party that divests either a prior grantee's estate (shifting executory interest) or springs from the grantor at a future time (springing executory interest). Executory interests are always subject to RAP because they are always contingent at creation.

**Here.** Here, "to A, but if [event], then to B" gives B a [shifting] executory interest; RAP [voids/validates] it because [there is/is no] possibility that the interest vests beyond a life in being plus 21 years.

**Thus.** Thus, B's executory interest [survives/is void under RAP]; if void, [A's estate becomes a fee simple absolute / the grantor retains a reversion].

### Rule Against Perpetuities
🔴 · *cross-ref: Contingent Remainder; Executory Interest; Class Gifts*

**Rule.** The common-law RAP voids any future interest that might vest — or fail to vest — later than 21 years after the death of some life in being at the time of the instrument's creation. Apply the "what-if" test: hypothesize any scenario, however unlikely, in which vesting could occur beyond the period. If such a scenario is possible, the interest is void ab initio. RAP applies to: contingent remainders, executory interests, vested remainders subject to open (class gifts), options to purchase not incident to a lease, and rights of first refusal.

**Here.** Here, [interest X] might vest [beyond all measuring lives + 21 years] because [hypothetical scenario]; therefore it [is/is not] valid at common law.

**Thus.** Thus, [interest X is void at common law and the preceding estate becomes/remains a fee simple absolute / the interest is saved by the applicable reform].

> **CA divergence:** California has adopted the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities (Prob. Code §§ 21200–21231). Under USRAP, an interest that violates the common-law RAP is not automatically void; instead there is a 90-year wait-and-see period. After 90 years, if the interest has not vested, the court reforms it using cy pres to approximate the transferor's intent. This is a major testable split.

### Concurrent Estates — Joint Tenancy
🔴 · *cross-ref: Severance; Recording Acts; Community Property*

**Rule.** A joint tenancy requires the four unities: Time (interests created simultaneously), Title (same instrument), Interest (equal shares), and Possession (equal right to possess). Modern courts require express language of survivorship ("as joint tenants with right of survivorship"). The hallmark is the right of survivorship — a deceased joint tenant's share passes automatically to surviving joint tenants, not through the decedent's estate. Any joint tenant may unilaterally sever by conveying their interest inter vivos to a third party or to themselves, converting the severed share into a tenancy in common. Secret severance (to a straw man and back) is recognized in some states.

**Here.** Here, O conveyed "to A and B as joint tenants with right of survivorship," satisfying [all four unities / three of four unities]; when A [conveyed/mortgaged/entered a judgment lien against] A's share, [severance did/did not] occur.

**Thus.** Thus, [A and B hold as joint tenants with right of survivorship / the joint tenancy was severed, and A and B now hold as tenants in common].

> **CA divergence:** (1) CA requires the deed to expressly state "joint tenancy" or "joint tenants" — mere co-grantee language does not create a JT. (2) A mortgage in CA (lien theory) does NOT sever a joint tenancy because the mortgagor does not convey title; a deed of trust similarly may not sever under CA case law. (3) CA allows severance by written agreement between joint tenants without a conveyance. Cross-ref: Community Property — property acquired during marriage is presumptively community property in CA; to hold as JT, spouses must expressly choose JT.

### Concurrent Estates — Tenancy in Common
🟢 · *cross-ref: Partition*

**Rule.** A tenancy in common is a concurrent estate in which each co-tenant holds a separate, undivided fractional share with no right of survivorship. Shares may be unequal. Each share is independently alienable, devisable, and descendible. There is a presumption in favor of tenancy in common when co-ownership language is ambiguous.

**Here.** Here, the deed conveyed to A and B without survivorship language, raising the presumption of tenancy in common; each holds an undivided [fraction] interest alienable without the other's consent.

**Thus.** Thus, [A/B] may transfer, encumber, or devise their individual share, and [the survivor does not automatically take the other's share].

### Concurrent Estates — Tenancy by the Entirety
🟡 · *cross-ref: Joint Tenancy; Community Property*

**Rule.** A tenancy by the entirety is a joint tenancy between spouses that neither spouse can unilaterally sever, encumber, or convey without the other's consent. It carries right of survivorship. A creditor of one spouse alone cannot reach the property. Recognized in roughly half the states; not recognized in CA.

**Here.** Here, [spouses] hold as tenants by the entirety in a state that recognizes TE; [one spouse]'s unilateral attempt to mortgage/convey is [ineffective] because both spouses must act together.

**Thus.** Thus, the creditor of one spouse [cannot/can] reach the property held in tenancy by the entirety.

> **CA divergence:** California does not recognize tenancy by the entirety. Married couples hold co-owned property either as community property, joint tenancy, or tenancy in common. See Community Property topic.

### Co-Tenant Rights and Duties
🟡 · *cross-ref: Partition; Accounting*

**Rule.** Each co-tenant has an equal right to possess the whole; one co-tenant cannot exclude another (that is ouster). A co-tenant who exclusively occupies is not liable for rent to others absent ouster. A co-tenant who collects rents from third parties must account to co-tenants for their proportionate share. Each co-tenant must pay their proportionate share of carrying costs (taxes, mortgage); a co-tenant who pays more than their share has a contribution right, typically enforced at partition. Improvements do not require consent; improver gets credit at partition but no action lies during co-tenancy.

**Here.** Here, A occupied the entire property and [did/did not] oust B; A collected $X in rent from a third-party tenant and [must/need not] account to B for B's [fraction] share.

**Thus.** Thus, [B may recover B's fractional share of third-party rents / A owes B no rent for personal occupation absent ouster].

### Partition
🟢 · *cross-ref: Co-Tenant Rights; Tenancy in Common*

**Rule.** Any co-tenant may seek partition at any time as a matter of right (TIC or unsevered JT). Courts prefer partition in kind (physical division). Partition by sale (forced sale and division of proceeds) is ordered when physical division is impractical or would cause disproportionate harm. Voluntary partition by agreement is also available. A cotenant cannot waive the right to partition permanently, but a reasonable time restriction may be enforced.

**Here.** Here, A sought partition of [the property]; the court will [divide in kind if feasible / order a sale if physical division would harm the parties' interests disproportionately].

**Thus.** Thus, partition [in kind / by sale] is the likely remedy, with [adjustments for carrying costs, improvements, and rents].

> **CA divergence:** CA Partition of Real Property Act (2022, based on the Uniform Act) requires courts in certain co-owned residential properties to give preference to partition in kind and to allow co-tenants to buy out others at appraised value before ordering a sale.

### Landlord-Tenant — Types of Tenancies
🟢 · *cross-ref: Holdover; Notice*

**Rule.** Four types: (1) Tenancy for years — fixed definite term, ends automatically, no notice required. (2) Periodic tenancy — successive periods (week/month/year), auto-renews unless proper notice given (notice = one period, max 6 months for year-to-year at common law). (3) Tenancy at will — no fixed term, either party terminates with reasonable notice; by statute many states require minimum notice. (4) Tenancy at sufferance — tenant wrongfully holds over after lease ends; landlord may elect to treat as trespasser or as periodic tenant.

**Here.** Here, the lease [ran from Jan 1 to Dec 31 — tenancy for years / ran month to month — periodic tenancy]; proper notice to terminate [was/was not] given [one month/period] in advance.

**Thus.** Thus, the tenancy [terminated on its own terms / requires [notice period] notice to end / continues as a periodic tenancy].

> **CA divergence:** CA requires 30-day notice to terminate a month-to-month tenancy of less than 1 year; 60-day notice if tenant has resided for 1+ year. CA's "just cause" eviction protections (AB 1482) apply to many residential tenancies — landlord must have a qualifying reason to evict after the first year.

### Implied Warranty of Habitability
🔴 · *cross-ref: Constructive Eviction; Quiet Enjoyment*

**Rule.** The implied warranty of habitability (IWH) applies to residential leases and requires the landlord to maintain the premises in a condition fit for human habitation throughout the tenancy. The standard is measured by local housing codes and basic livability (heat, water, structural safety). A material breach by the landlord gives the tenant remedies: (1) repair and deduct (up to one month's rent in many states); (2) rent withholding (must notify and may need to pay into escrow); (3) rent abatement/reduction; (4) termination; (5) damages. The warranty cannot be waived by the parties.

**Here.** Here, the landlord [failed to repair a broken heating system / allowed vermin infestation], which [is/is not] a material breach of the IWH because [it affects basic livability]; tenant [gave notice and] [withheld rent / repaired and deducted].

**Thus.** Thus, the landlord [is in breach of the IWH / has not materially breached], and tenant's remedy is [appropriate remedy].

> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code §§ 1941–1942 codify the IWH. Tenant may repair and deduct up to one month's rent twice in any 12-month period. CA also recognizes "habitability" broadly to include conditions that make the unit unfit or unsafe per health and safety codes.

### Quiet Enjoyment and Constructive Eviction
🟡 · *cross-ref: IWH; Landlord-Tenant duties*

**Rule.** The covenant of quiet enjoyment (CQE) is implied in every lease and protects the tenant's right to peaceable possession against interference by the landlord or anyone claiming superior title. Constructive eviction (CE) occurs when the landlord's acts or omissions substantially interfere with the tenant's use and enjoyment to such a degree that the tenant is effectively forced to vacate. Elements of CE: (1) landlord's act or omission; (2) substantial interference with use/enjoyment; (3) tenant gives notice and landlord fails to cure; (4) tenant vacates within a reasonable time. A tenant who does not vacate cannot claim CE.

**Here.** Here, the landlord [failed to supply heat / permitted a noisy co-tenant to interfere], which [substantially interfered with] T's use; T [did/did not] notify LL and [vacated/remained]; because T [vacated / failed to vacate], CE [is/is not] established.

**Thus.** Thus, [T may terminate the lease and sue for damages / T's CE claim fails because T did not vacate].

### Assignment vs. Sublease
🟡 · *cross-ref: Privity of Contract/Estate; Landlord consent*

**Rule.** An assignment transfers the tenant's entire remaining lease term to the assignee; a sublease transfers less than the full remaining term (or retains a reversion in the original tenant). Privity of estate exists between the assignee and landlord — assignee is directly liable to LL for covenants that run with the lease (rent, repair). Original tenant remains liable on privity of contract unless released. In a sublease, the sublessee is not in privity with the landlord; the original tenant remains fully liable. A lease clause requiring landlord consent to assignment: majority rule — landlord may withhold for any reason; CA rule — landlord may not unreasonably withhold consent to a residential assignment.

**Here.** Here, T transferred [all/part] of the remaining term to X, creating [an assignment / a sublease]; [LL may / may not] look to X for rent because [privity of estate does/does not exist].

**Thus.** Thus, [X is/is not] directly liable to LL, and T [remains/is released from] liability on the original lease.

> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code § 1995.010 et seq.: In commercial leases, a landlord may not unreasonably withhold consent to assignment unless the lease expressly permits arbitrary refusal. For residential leases, a landlord may not unreasonably withhold consent.

### Easements — Creation and Types
🟡 · *cross-ref: Recording Acts; Covenants; Adverse Possession*

**Rule.** An easement is a nonpossessory right to use another's land (servient estate) for a specific purpose. It is appurtenant (benefits a dominant estate — passes automatically with that estate) or in gross (personal benefit — commercial easements in gross are assignable; personal ones generally are not). Creation methods: (1) Express grant or reservation — must satisfy SOF; deeds should be recorded. (2) Implied by prior use — common ownership, apparent and continuous use at time of severance, and reasonable necessity (strict necessity not required). (3) Implied by necessity — common ownership at some point, strict necessity (landlocked parcel), arises at time of severance. (4) Prescriptive easement — similar to adverse possession but without exclusivity requirement: open, notorious, continuous use for the statutory period, without the owner's permission.

**Thus.** Thus, [O/successors] hold [an express/implied/prescriptive] easement over [servient estate].

### Easement Scope and Termination
🟢 · *cross-ref: Easements — Creation*

**Rule.** The scope of an easement is determined by the creating instrument or, for implied easements, by the circumstances at creation. The holder may not expand use beyond the original purpose; the servient owner may not unreasonably interfere with the easement holder's use. Termination methods: (1) merger — dominant and servient estates come under common ownership; (2) express release in writing; (3) abandonment — requires physical act demonstrating intent to permanently abandon (non-use alone insufficient); (4) estoppel — servient owner reasonably relies on holder's representation; (5) end of necessity — for necessity easements only; (6) prescription — servient owner's continuous, hostile interference for the statutory period; (7) condemnation.

**Here.** Here, the easement holder [used the road for residential access and then began using it for commercial trucks], which [exceeds/does not exceed] the original scope because [the creating instrument/circumstances limited use to residential].

**Thus.** Thus, the [expanded use may be enjoined / easement terminated by merger / abandonment established].

### Real Covenants (Running at Law)
🟡 · *cross-ref: Equitable Servitudes; Recording Acts*

**Rule.** A real covenant runs with the land and binds/benefits successors if: (1) writing (in a deed or instrument); (2) parties intended it to run; (3) it touches and concerns the land (affects the parties as landowners); (4) horizontal privity — grantor-grantee relationship at the time the covenant was created; (5) vertical privity — successor holds the same estate as the original covenantor (burden side) or any succeeding estate (benefit side); (6) notice — successor must have had actual, constructive, or inquiry notice. If burden fails to run at law, try equitable servitude.

**Here.** Here, O conveyed Lot 1 to A with a covenant that A would not build above 2 stories; [all elements are/are not met]: [the covenant was in the deed (writing); it touches and concerns (limits use as a landowner); horizontal privity exists because it was in a deed; A's successor B had constructive notice via recording].

**Thus.** Thus, the covenant [does/does not] run with the land to bind B.

### Equitable Servitudes
🟡 · *cross-ref: Real Covenants; Common Scheme; Injunction*

**Rule.** An equitable servitude is enforced in equity (injunction). It requires: (1) writing (or common scheme for implied); (2) intent to bind successors; (3) touch and concern the land; (4) notice. No privity required. Remedy is injunctive relief, not damages. Changed conditions doctrine: if the neighborhood has changed so substantially that enforcement would be inequitable, a court may refuse to enforce the servitude.

### Common Scheme (Implied Reciprocal Servitudes)

**Rule.** When a developer subdivides and sells lots imposing a common restriction — intending to create a uniform development scheme — courts imply a reciprocal servitude on lots sold without the express restriction if: (1) a general scheme existed when the developer began selling; and (2) subsequent buyers had notice of the scheme (actual, constructive, or inquiry — e.g., a recorded plat or widespread visible restrictions).

### Profits and Licenses
🟢

**Rule.** A profit à prendre (profit) is the right to enter another's land and remove resources (timber, minerals, fish). It follows easement rules for creation, scope, and termination. It may be appurtenant or in gross; an exclusive profit in gross is assignable. A license is a revocable permission to use land; it is not an interest in land, need not be in writing, and is generally revocable at will. Exceptions: (1) a license coupled with an interest (e.g., right to enter and remove goods purchased) is irrevocable; (2) an executed license on which the licensee has reasonably relied with expenditures becomes irrevocable by estoppel (easement by estoppel).

**Here.** Here, O orally permitted A to use O's road; A [expended substantial sums improving the road in reliance]; [the license became irrevocable by estoppel / remains revocable].

**Thus.** Thus, O [may/may not] revoke the license because [no estoppel / estoppel applies].

### Land Sale Contracts — Marketable Title
🔴 · *cross-ref: Deeds (merger); Recording Acts*

**Rule.** Every land sale contract contains an implied covenant that the seller will convey marketable title at closing. Marketable title means title reasonably free from doubt — a title a reasonable, prudent buyer would accept without special risk. Title is unmarketable if there is: an adverse possession claim with a reasonable basis; an encumbrance (easement, covenant, mortgage) not disclosed in the contract; a significant chain-of-title defect; or a violation of zoning (split authority — most courts say zoning violations don't automatically make title unmarketable). The seller has until closing to cure defects; the buyer may not rescind prematurely.

**Here.** Here, [seller's title was subject to an unrecorded easement / a neighbor's adverse possession claim / an outstanding mortgage]; this [is/is not] a marketable title defect because [it creates a reasonable doubt about seller's right to convey].

**Thus.** Thus, buyer [may/may not] rescind or seek damages for breach of the implied marketable title covenant.

### Equitable Conversion and Risk of Loss
🟡 · *cross-ref: Land Sale Contracts; Insurance*

**Rule.** Under the equitable conversion doctrine, once a specifically enforceable land sale contract is executed, equity regards the buyer as the owner of the real property and the seller as holding legal title in trust (the seller's interest converts to personalty — the right to receive proceeds). Majority rule: risk of loss passes to the buyer at contract execution; if the property is destroyed through no fault of either party, the buyer still must pay the full purchase price. Minority (Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act): risk of loss stays with the seller until closing or delivery of possession. If the seller has insurance, most courts impose a constructive trust on proceeds for the buyer.

**Here.** Here, the house burned after contract execution but before closing; under majority equitable conversion, the buyer [bears the loss and must proceed / may recover from seller if CA applies UVPRA].

**Thus.** Thus, [buyer/seller] bears the risk under [applicable state rule]; insurance proceeds [are/are not] held for buyer's benefit.

> **CA divergence:** CA follows the minority Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act — risk of loss stays with the seller until either title or possession passes to the buyer. This is a high-yield CA split.

### Land Sale Contracts — Duty to Disclose
🟡 · *cross-ref: Fraud; Misrepresentation*

**Rule.** A seller of real property has a duty to disclose all known material defects that are not reasonably observable by the buyer. Caveat emptor is substantially eroded. The duty extends to latent physical defects, legal defects affecting use, and — in CA — psychological defects under specific statutory limits. Nondisclosure of a known material defect supports rescission, damages, or both.

**Here.** Here, seller [knew of foundation cracks / undisclosed easement / prior death on premises] and [did/did not] disclose; the defect [was/was not] observable by buyer in reasonable inspection.

**Thus.** Thus, seller [breached/did not breach] the disclosure duty; buyer [may/may not] rescind or recover damages.

> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code § 1102 et seq. imposes a comprehensive Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) requirement for most residential (1–4 unit) sales. Sellers must complete the TDS; failure allows rescission within 3 days (in-person delivery) or 5 days (mail). CA also requires disclosure of natural hazard zones, death on the property within 3 years (but not beyond 3 years unless asked), and Megan's Law database information.

### Merger Doctrine
🟢 · *cross-ref: Land Sale Contracts; Deeds*

**Rule.** Upon closing, the land sale contract merges into the deed; only the deed's terms govern thereafter. Exceptions: (1) a collateral promise not intended to be performed by the deed (e.g., seller's promise to repair something post-closing); (2) fraud; (3) mutual mistake. A buyer who discovers a breach of the land sale contract after closing generally cannot sue on the contract — the deed is the controlling instrument.

**Here.** Here, seller [promised in the contract to leave appliances but the deed was silent]; at closing, [the contract merged into the deed]; buyer [may/may not] enforce the appliance promise because it [is/is not] a collateral matter.

**Thus.** Thus, the [contract term merged / collateral promise survives] and buyer [has/has no] post-closing claim.

### Deeds — Requirements and Delivery
🔴 · *cross-ref: Recording Acts; Gifts (Wills/Trusts)*

**Rule.** A valid deed requires: (1) a writing; (2) identification of the grantor; (3) identification of the grantee (must be ascertainable); (4) words of conveyance (granting clause); (5) adequate legal description of the property; (6) grantor's signature; (7) delivery. Delivery requires the grantor to have the present intent to be immediately bound — to pass title now, not in the future. Handing the deed to the grantee raises a strong presumption of delivery. Recording creates a presumption of delivery. A deed delivered in escrow under a condition is effective upon satisfaction of the condition. Acceptance is presumed for a beneficial conveyance.

### Deed Covenants — Types
🟡 · *cross-ref: Marketable Title; Warranties*

**Rule.** A general warranty deed contains six covenants: (1) Seisin — grantor owns the estate conveyed; (2) Right to convey — grantor has power to transfer; (3) Covenant against encumbrances — no undisclosed mortgages, easements, etc.; (4) Quiet enjoyment — grantee will not be disturbed by someone with superior title; (5) Warranty — grantor will defend against lawful claims; (6) Further assurances — grantor will execute additional documents to perfect title. The first three are present covenants (breached, if at all, at delivery — SOL runs immediately). The last three are future covenants (breached when grantee is actually disturbed — SOL runs then). A special warranty deed limits covenants to acts of the grantor only. A quitclaim deed carries no covenants.

**Thus.** Thus, [seller/grantor] [is/is not] liable on the deed covenant because [analysis of present vs future covenant timing].

### Recording Acts — Framework
🔴 · *cross-ref: BFP; Chain of Title; Mortgages*

**Rule.** Recording acts protect subsequent purchasers against prior unrecorded interests. Three types: (1) Race statute — first to record wins, regardless of notice (rare). (2) Notice statute — a subsequent bona fide purchaser (BFP) who takes without notice of a prior unrecorded interest prevails, even if they don't record first. (3) Race-notice statute — a subsequent BFP prevails only if they (a) take without notice AND (b) record before the prior grantee. A BFP pays valuable consideration, takes without actual, constructive (record), or inquiry notice, and in some states must record. One who takes as a gift is not a BFP.

**Here.** Here, O conveyed to A (unrecorded), then to B; under a [race-notice/notice] statute, B prevails if B [lacked notice at time of purchase AND recorded first / merely lacked notice]; B [had/lacked] constructive notice because A's deed [was/was not] in the chain of title.

**Thus.** Thus, [A/B] prevails under the recording act.

> **CA divergence:** California has a race-notice statute (Civil Code § 1214). A subsequent purchaser prevails only if they (1) take for valuable consideration, (2) lack notice (actual, constructive, or inquiry) at the time of conveyance, AND (3) record first.

### Recording Acts — BFP and Shelter Rule
🟡 · *cross-ref: Recording Acts Framework; Chain of Title*

**Rule.** A BFP takes free of prior unrecorded interests. To qualify: (1) pays valuable consideration (nominal consideration insufficient); (2) lacks actual notice (personal knowledge); (3) lacks constructive notice (prior recorded instrument in the chain of title); (4) lacks inquiry notice (visible facts that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate). The shelter rule: a person who takes from a BFP acquires the BFP's protected status, even if that person had personal notice — this allows the BFP to convey good title to others.

**Here.** Here, C purchased from B (a BFP) with knowledge of A's prior claim; under the shelter rule, C [takes free of / is subject to] A's claim because [B was a BFP and can convey that status to C].

**Thus.** Thus, [C / A] prevails because [shelter rule applies / C did not take from a BFP].

### Chain of Title Problems
🟡 · *cross-ref: Recording Acts; BFP*

**Rule.** Chain of title runs from the original grantor to the present owner as revealed by the grantor-grantee index. Common problems: (1) Wild deed — a recorded deed outside the chain (grantor never received from anyone in the chain) — gives no constructive notice. (2) Estoppel by deed — grantor who conveys before acquiring title, then later acquires it; the after-acquired title feeds through to the earlier grantee; a subsequent BFP from the same grantor may take free depending on timing. (3) Late-recorded deed — a deed recorded outside the proper sequence may not provide constructive notice to subsequent purchasers who searched before late-recording occurred.

### Mortgages — Theory, Transfer, and Priority
🔴 · *cross-ref: Recording Acts; Foreclosure; Deed of Trust*

**Rule.** A mortgage is a voluntary lien given to secure a debt; it does not transfer title (lien theory, majority). The mortgagor retains legal title. Priority among competing mortgages follows the recording act — first to record (with proper BFP status under race-notice) prevails. A purchase money mortgage (PMM) — given to the seller or a third-party lender to finance the purchase — has super-priority over judgment liens and other mortgages even if recorded later. Mortgagor may transfer by: (a) "subject to" the mortgage — transferee takes subject to lien, no personal liability, original mortgagor remains liable; (b) assumption — transferee takes on personal liability; original mortgagor remains liable unless lender releases (novation). Mortgagee may transfer the note; the mortgage follows the note automatically.

**Here.** Here, [borrower] executed a mortgage/deed of trust to secure a $X loan; [borrower] later sold "subject to" the mortgage; [new buyer] [is/is not] personally liable to lender because [no assumption occurred / assumption occurred].

**Thus.** Thus, lender [can/cannot] obtain a deficiency judgment against [new buyer] and [can/cannot] against [original borrower].

> **CA divergence:** (1) CA is a lien-theory state. (2) Most CA real estate financing uses a deed of trust (three-party instrument: trustor/borrower, trustee, beneficiary/lender) rather than a traditional two-party mortgage. (3) CA has a one-action rule (CCP § 726): lender must exhaust the security (foreclose first) before seeking a personal judgment. (4) CA antideficiency statutes (CCP §§ 580b, 580d): after nonjudicial foreclosure (trustee's sale), the lender is BARRED from a deficiency judgment — this is a critical CA rule. Also bars deficiency on purchase money mortgages after judicial or nonjudicial foreclosure.

### Foreclosure and Equity of Redemption
🔴 · *cross-ref: Mortgages; Deed of Trust; Antideficiency*

**Rule.** Foreclosure is the process by which the lender enforces the mortgage lien upon default. Judicial foreclosure — court proceeding; extinguishes all junior interests joined as parties; produces a deficiency judgment if permitted. Nonjudicial foreclosure — under a power of sale clause; faster; in many states (including CA) no court involvement. Equity of redemption — the mortgagor's right to redeem the property by paying the full debt at any time before the foreclosure sale; this is an equitable right that cannot be waived in the original mortgage ("clogging" is void). Statutory redemption — some states allow redemption for a period after the sale (state-specific). Foreclosure extinguishes junior liens but does not affect senior interests — a buyer at foreclosure sale takes subject to senior encumbrances.

**Thus.** Thus, [borrower's equity of redemption was extinguished at the trustee's sale / junior lienholders look only to surplus proceeds / deficiency judgment is barred under CA CCP § 580d].

> **CA divergence:** CA primarily uses nonjudicial foreclosure via trustee's sale under a deed of trust. After a trustee's sale, the lender is absolutely barred from a deficiency judgment (CCP § 580d). Judicial foreclosure is available but rarely used because it triggers a 3-month or 1-year statutory right of redemption by the debtor (CCP § 729.030), which discourages buyers. One-action rule applies regardless of method.

### Adverse Possession
🔴 · *cross-ref: Recording Acts; Prescriptive Easement; Tacking*

**Rule.** Adverse possession transfers title to a claimant who satisfies all elements for the statutory period: (1) Actual entry and use — physical occupation consistent with the nature of the land; (2) Open and notorious — visible, such that a reasonable owner would notice; (3) Exclusive — not shared with the true owner or the general public; (4) Continuous — uninterrupted for the full statutory period (seasonal use of seasonal property may suffice); (5) Hostile/adverse — without the owner's permission and under a claim of right; (6) [CA specific] Payment of property taxes for the statutory period. Tacking: a subsequent adverse possessor may add (tack) a predecessor's time if there is privity of possession (voluntary transfer of possession). A disability of the true owner existing at the time AP begins tolls the statute.

**Thus.** Thus, A [has/has not] satisfied all elements and [has/has not] acquired title by adverse possession.

> **CA divergence:** CA requires the adverse possessor to have ACTUALLY PAID PROPERTY TAXES on the disputed parcel for the entire 5-year statutory period (Civil Code § 1007; CCP § 325). This is the single most-tested CA AP divergence. Without tax payment, no AP claim succeeds in CA regardless of how long or obvious the possession. Statutory period is 5 years (CCP § 321 et seq.).

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:**
  - Real Property + Contracts: land sale contract, SOF, equitable conversion, risk of loss (often one Q covers both).
  - Real Property + Wills/Trusts: future interests in testamentary transfers, life estates in trusts, RAP in trust instruments.
  - Real Property + Community Property: co-tenancy classification for married couples, transmutation, joint tenancy vs. CP presumption.
  - Real Property + Remedies: injunction for covenant/easement violations, specific performance for land sale K, ejectment for AP.
  - Real Property + Torts: landlord-tenant negligence, duty to disclose as fraud/misrepresentation.

- **Sleeper issues:**
  - Equitable conversion + CA's UVPRA risk-of-loss rule — most examinees apply the majority rule; CA is the minority.
  - Merger doctrine — students forget K terms merge into deed at closing and try to enforce pre-closing promises.
  - Deed delivery — candidates overlook the "present intent" requirement and miss failed deliveries.
  - Wild deed / chain of title — examinees credit a recorded deed without checking whether it is in the chain.
  - Co-tenant accounting — students miss that co-tenant using property alone owes no rent absent ouster, but must account for third-party rents.
  - TE not recognized in CA — examinees trained on majority rules may reflexively apply TE to married couples.
  - RAP applied to options and rights of first refusal — often missed as candidates focus only on remainders.

- **CA-divergence cheat list:**
  1. Adverse possession requires 5 years + actual payment of property taxes (Civil Code § 1007).
  2. CA is a race-notice recording state (Civil Code § 1214).
  3. CA is a lien-theory state; mortgage/deed of trust does not convey title; a mortgage does NOT sever a joint tenancy.
  4. CA foreclosure: deed of trust + trustee sale (nonjudicial); after trustee sale, deficiency judgment BARRED (CCP § 580d); one-action rule (CCP § 726).
  5. Risk of loss under land sale K: CA follows Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act — risk stays with seller until title or possession passes.
  6. CA does not recognize tenancy by the entirety; married couples use community property, JT, or TIC.
  7. CA AB 1482 just-cause eviction protections apply to most residential tenancies after 12 months.
  8. CA Partition of Real Property Act (2022) favors in-kind partition and gives co-tenants buyout rights before forced sale.
  9. CA implied warranty of habitability: repair-and-deduct up to one month's rent, twice per year.
  10. CA Transfer Disclosure Statement mandatory for most 1–4 unit residential sales.

- **Call-of-the-question patterns:**
  - "Discuss the rights of the parties" → full survey; hit every plausible issue; do IRAC on each.
  - "Who has title?" → recording act analysis; chain of title; BFP test; shelter rule.
  - "May [landlord] evict [tenant]?" → classify tenancy; notice; just cause (CA); IWH defenses.
  - "Does the covenant/restriction bind [subsequent owner]?" → real covenant elements + equitable servitude fallback; common scheme.
  - "What are [party]'s rights against [mortgagee/lender]?" → lien theory; equity of redemption; CA antideficiency; one-action.
  - "Has [claimant] acquired title by adverse possession?" → run through OCEAN + CA tax-payment element.
  - "Identify and discuss all future interests" → classify every interest; test each contingent one under RAP; apply CA USRAP wait-and-see.
