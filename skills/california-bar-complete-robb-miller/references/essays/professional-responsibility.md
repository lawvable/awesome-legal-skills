### Duty of Competence
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Malpractice, Declining Representation*

**Rule.** A lawyer must provide competent representation, meaning the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation. Competence can be achieved by studying the unfamiliar area, associating with competent counsel, or — if neither is feasible without undue delay — declining the matter. ABA Model Rule 1.1; CRPC 1.1 (same standard).

### Duty of Diligence
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Competence, Communication*

**Rule.** A lawyer must pursue a matter with reasonable diligence and promptness. The duty continues through conclusion; the lawyer may not neglect or abandon the matter. ABA MR 1.3; CRPC 1.3 (same).

### Duty of Communication
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Diligence, Fees*

### Confidentiality — General Duty
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Attorney-Client Privilege, Conflicts, Candor*

**Rule.** A lawyer shall not reveal information relating to the representation of a client without the client's informed consent, unless an exception applies. The duty is broader than the attorney-client privilege: it covers all information related to the representation, regardless of source, not just confidential communications. ABA MR 1.6; CRPC 1.6; Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6068(e).

**Here.** Here, [attorney] disclosed [information] that [was/was not] related to [client]'s representation. Unless [attorney] can point to [informed consent / an applicable exception], the disclosure [violates/does not violate] the duty.

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [likely/likely did not] breach confidentiality.

> **CA divergence:** ABA MR 1.6 permits (but does not require) disclosure in six scenarios, including to prevent future crimes causing substantial financial harm. California is narrower: CRPC 1.6(b) and Bus. & Prof. Code §6068(e)(2) permit disclosure *only* to prevent a criminal act the lawyer reasonably believes will result in **death or substantial bodily harm**. CA does not permit disclosure to prevent financial crimes. Additionally, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6068(e)(1) makes the duty of confidentiality *mandatory* absent exceptions — emphasize the mandatory character.

### Attorney-Client Privilege vs. Duty of Confidentiality
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Confidentiality, Candor to Tribunal*

**Rule.** The attorney-client privilege is an *evidentiary* rule that protects confidential communications between attorney and client made for the purpose of obtaining legal advice; it belongs to the client and may be waived only by the client. The ethical duty of confidentiality is broader: it applies to all information relating to the representation, arises from professional responsibility rules, and governs voluntary as well as compelled disclosure. A court order may compel disclosure of privileged material only after the privilege is properly overridden; the ethical duty may require the lawyer to assert the privilege on the client's behalf.

[Attorney] must first determine whether [the A-C privilege / work-product doctrine] applies and assert it if so; separately, [attorney] must determine whether the broader ethical duty of confidentiality bars [voluntary disclosure / production].

**Thus.** Thus, the privilege and the ethical duty [both apply/differ in scope here] and [attorney] must [assert privilege / decline voluntary disclosure] accordingly.

### Concurrent Conflicts of Interest (ABA 1.7 / CRPC 1.7)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Former Client Conflicts, Imputed Disqualification, Business Transactions*

**Rule.** A concurrent conflict exists if (1) the representation of one client is directly adverse to another current client, or (2) there is a significant risk that the representation will be materially limited by the lawyer's responsibilities to another client, former client, third person, or the lawyer's own interests. Such a conflict is consentable if: (a) the lawyer reasonably believes competent, diligent representation is possible for each client; (b) the representation is not prohibited by law; (c) it does not involve asserting a claim by one client against the other in the same proceeding; and (d) each affected client gives informed written consent. ABA MR 1.7; CRPC 1.7 (same structure, "written informed consent" required in CA).

[Attorney] must determine if this is consentable — [facts suggesting reasonable belief / facts suggesting non-consentable conflict].

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [may/may not] continue the dual representation [with/without] informed written consent from both clients.

### Personal-Interest Conflicts
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Concurrent Conflicts, Business Transactions with Clients*

**Rule.** A lawyer's own financial, romantic, family, or reputational interests can create a conflict if there is a significant risk the interest materially limits the lawyer's representation. Analyzed under the MR 1.7(a)(2) / CRPC 1.7(b) material-limitation prong. Some personal-interest conflicts are per se non-consentable (e.g., sexual relations with client in CA — CRPC 1.8.10 prohibits unless pre-existing).

**Here.** Here, [attorney]'s [financial stake in outcome / romantic relationship with client / personal vendetta against opposing party] creates a risk of material limitation because [specific impact on judgment]. [Attorney] must assess whether a reasonable attorney would be able to provide unaffected representation.

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [has/does not have] a consentable personal-interest conflict.

> **CA divergence:** CRPC 1.8.10 prohibits a lawyer from engaging in sexual relations with a current client unless a consensual relationship predated the representation. ABA MR 1.8(j) is functionally similar but CA's express rule is worth citing separately.

### Business Transactions with Clients (ABA 1.8(a) / CRPC 1.8.1)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Personal-Interest Conflicts, Fees*

**Rule.** A lawyer shall not enter a business transaction with a client or knowingly acquire a pecuniary interest adverse to a client unless: (1) the transaction and terms are fair and reasonable to the client and fully disclosed in writing in a manner the client can understand; (2) the client is advised in writing to seek independent legal counsel; and (3) the client gives informed written consent to the essential terms and to the lawyer's role. ABA MR 1.8(a); CRPC 1.8.1 (same elements).

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [violated/complied with] the business-transaction rule.

### Gifts from Clients / Drafting Instruments Benefiting Lawyer
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Business Transactions, Conflicts*

**Rule.** A lawyer shall not solicit a substantial gift from a client, including a testamentary gift, or prepare an instrument for a client that gives the lawyer or a related person a substantial gift, unless the lawyer or recipient is related to the client. ABA MR 1.8(c); CRPC 1.8.3 (same).

### Former Client Conflicts (ABA 1.9 / CRPC 1.9)
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Concurrent Conflicts, Imputed Disqualification*

**Rule.** A lawyer who has previously represented a client shall not thereafter represent another person in the same or a substantially related matter in which that person's interests are materially adverse to the former client's interests, without informed written consent from the former client. Two matters are "substantially related" if they involve the same transaction or legal dispute, or if there is a substantial risk that confidential information from the prior matter would be material to the new one.

The matters [are/are not] substantially related because [connection between matters / confidential information at risk].

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [is/is not] disqualified from the new representation absent [Former Client]'s informed written consent.

### Duties to Prospective Clients (ABA 1.18 / CRPC 1.18)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Confidentiality, Former Client Conflicts*

**Rule.** A person who consults with a lawyer about possible representation is a prospective client. The lawyer owes that person confidentiality as to the information disclosed. The lawyer is disqualified from representing someone with materially adverse interests in the same or substantially related matter if the lawyer received information that could be significantly harmful to the prospective client — unless the prospective client gives informed written consent, or the lawyer is timely screened.

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [owes / does not owe] disqualification obligations to [Prospective Client].

### Fees — Reasonableness / Unconscionability
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Written Fee Agreements, Contingent Fees, Fee Splitting*

**Rule.** Under ABA MR 1.5, a lawyer's fee must be "reasonable," assessed by eight factors including time, difficulty, prevailing rate, experience, and results obtained. California applies a stricter standard: a fee is unenforceable if "unconscionable" — a higher threshold for the client to overcome. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6147–6148; CRPC 1.5.

**Here.** Here, [attorney]'s fee of [$X] for [services] [is/is not] reasonable under ABA because [factors]. Under CA's unconscionable standard, the fee [may/may not] be enforceable because [materiality of departure from norms].

**Thus.** Thus, the fee [is/is not] problematic under ABA; under CA the analysis [differs / reaches the same result].

> **CA divergence:** ABA: "reasonable." CA: "unconscionable." CA sets a higher bar before a fee violates the rule — a fee may be unreasonable under ABA but still not unconscionable under CA. Always apply both standards on the CA exam.

### Written Fee Agreements
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Fees, Contingent Fees*

**Rule.** California law imposes affirmative writing requirements broader than the ABA. Under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6148, a fee agreement must be in writing when the client is an individual and it is reasonably foreseeable that the total expense will exceed $1,000. The writing must state the basis of compensation and the general nature of legal services. Failure to comply makes the fee agreement voidable at the client's election. For contingent fees, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6147 requires a separate written agreement stating the contingency percentage, how costs are deducted, and other specifics; a non-compliant contingent fee agreement is voidable and the attorney may recover only in quantum meruit.

**Here.** Here, [attorney] [did/did not] execute a written fee agreement before [client] retained [attorney] for [matter worth/exceeding $1,000]. The absence of a written agreement means [client] may [void the agreement / limit attorney to quantum meruit recovery].

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney]'s failure to obtain a written fee agreement [violates §6148 / exposes attorney to fee forfeiture].

> **CA divergence:** ABA MR 1.5 recommends but does not generally mandate written fee agreements (except for contingent fees under MR 1.5(c)). California's §6148 imposes a mandatory writing requirement for most fees exceeding $1,000 — a major CA divergence.

### Contingent Fees
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Written Fee Agreements, Fees*

**Rule.** A contingent fee must be in a writing signed by the client that states: the method of calculation, whether the percentage is taken before or after deduction of costs, and what expenses the client is responsible for. ABA MR 1.5(c); Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6147. Contingent fees are prohibited in criminal defense matters (ABA and CA) and in domestic relations matters to the extent the fee is contingent on the amount of alimony or support obtained (ABA MR 1.5(d); CA similar).

**Thus.** Thus, the contingent fee [is/is not] permissible.

### Fee Splitting Between Lawyers / Referral Fees
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Fees, Advertising & Solicitation*

**Rule.** ABA MR 1.5(e): a division of fees between lawyers not in the same firm is permitted if (1) the division is proportional to services performed or the lawyers assume joint responsibility; (2) the client agrees in writing; and (3) the total fee is reasonable. ABA generally prohibits pure referral fees. California under CRPC 1.5.1 permits fee-sharing consistent with ABA MR 1.5(e) standards. Additionally, California permits *referral fees* — fees paid solely for referring a client — if (a) the fee is not unconscionable, (b) the client is informed and consents, and (c) the arrangement does not interfere with independent professional judgment. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6157.2; CRPC 1.5.1 cmt.

**Thus.** Thus, the fee-sharing arrangement [is/is not] permissible under [ABA / CA].

> **CA divergence:** ABA prohibits pure referral fees. CA permits them under specified conditions — a significant difference graders reward.

### Safekeeping Client Property / Client Trust Accounts
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Fees, Candor, Conflicts*

**Rule.** A lawyer must hold client funds and property separate from the lawyer's own property. Client funds must be deposited into a dedicated trust account (IOLTA for nominal or short-term funds). The lawyer must maintain accurate records, promptly notify the client of receipt, and promptly deliver funds to which the client is entitled. Commingling — depositing client funds with the lawyer's personal or operating funds — is a per se violation. When a fee is in dispute, the disputed portion must remain in trust until resolution. ABA MR 1.15; CRPC 1.15.

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [violated/did not violate] the safekeeping rules.

### Candor to the Tribunal
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Confidentiality, Client Perjury, Fairness to Opposing Party*

**Rule.** A lawyer shall not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal, fail to disclose controlling adverse authority in the jurisdiction not disclosed by opposing counsel, or offer evidence the lawyer knows to be false. The duty of candor overrides the duty of confidentiality to the tribunal. ABA MR 3.3; CRPC 3.3 (same framework).

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney] [did/did not] satisfy the duty of candor.

### Client Perjury
🔴  ·  *cross-ref: Candor to Tribunal, Confidentiality, Withdrawal*

**Rule.** If a lawyer knows a client intends to or has testified falsely, the lawyer must: (1) remonstrate with the client; (2) seek withdrawal if possible; and (3) if withdrawal is not permitted and the false testimony remains uncorrected, disclose to the tribunal. ABA MR 3.3(b). California follows a similar approach: CRPC 3.3(a)(3) prohibits offering false evidence; California further permits the "narrative approach" (letting client testify in narrative form without counsel's direct examination) as a middle path. The duty of candor prevails over confidentiality.

**Here.** Here, [client] insisted on [testifying that X happened / presenting document Y] which [attorney] knows is false. [Attorney] must [remonstrate / seek withdrawal / disclose] and [may/must] use the narrative approach under California practice if withdrawal is denied.

> **CA divergence:** California more explicitly recognizes the narrative approach as an acceptable response to the client perjury dilemma than the ABA framework.

### Communication with Represented Persons (No-Contact Rule)
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Fairness to Opposing Party, Unrepresented Persons*

### Communication with Unrepresented Persons

### Advertising & Solicitation
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Fees, Referral Fees*

**Rule.** Lawyer advertising must not be false or misleading. ABA MR 7.1; CRPC 7.1. In-person, live-telephone, or real-time electronic solicitation of a prospective client with whom the lawyer has no prior relationship is prohibited when a significant motive is pecuniary gain and the prospective client is in a vulnerable state. ABA MR 7.3; CRPC 7.3. Written solicitations (direct mail) are generally permissible with appropriate disclosures. Trade names are permissible under ABA but subject to CA restrictions on misleading names.

**Here.** Here, [attorney] [ran an ad claiming "best lawyer in California" / sent a targeted mailer to accident victims / approached grieving family at hospital]. The ad [is/is not] misleading; the solicitation [is/is not] prohibited in-person solicitation under CRPC 7.3.

> **CA divergence:** CA requires direct-mail solicitations to include a specific "Advertising Material" label and imposes additional disclosure requirements. CA also restricts firm trade names more broadly than ABA to prevent misleading the public. CRPC 7.1–7.5.

### Declining and Terminating Representation
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Conflicts, Competence, Client Trust Accounts*

**Rule.** Mandatory withdrawal is required when: (1) continued representation would require violating a professional conduct rule or law; (2) the lawyer's physical or mental condition materially impairs representation. Permissive withdrawal is allowed when the client persists in criminal or fraudulent conduct, the client fails to fulfill obligations, the representation imposes unreasonable financial burden, the client–lawyer relationship has broken down, or other good cause exists — provided no material adverse effect on the client results. Upon termination, the lawyer must: (a) give reasonable notice; (b) allow time to retain new counsel; (c) return the client's file and papers; and (d) refund any unearned advance fees. ABA MR 1.16; CRPC 1.16.

**Thus.** Thus, [attorney]'s termination [was/was not] proper and [attorney]'s post-termination duties [were/were not] met.

### Supervisory and Subordinate Lawyer Duties
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Imputed Disqualification, Malpractice*

**Rule.** A supervising lawyer with direct supervisory authority over another lawyer is responsible for that lawyer's misconduct if the supervising lawyer orders or ratifies the conduct, or knows of the conduct at a time when its consequences can be avoided or mitigated and fails to take reasonable remedial action. ABA MR 5.1; CRPC 5.1. A subordinate lawyer is bound by the rules of professional conduct and does not escape liability because a supervisor ordered the act — unless the supervisory lawyer ordered an act that reasonably appeared to be a legitimate resolution of an arguable question of professional duty. ABA MR 5.2; CRPC 5.2.

**Here.** Here, [supervising attorney] [directed/knew of] [associate]'s [conduct]. [Supervising attorney] [did/did not] take remedial action when [the conduct could still be avoided]. [Associate] [cannot claim the supervisor ordered the act / may have a defense if the question was arguable].

**Thus.** Thus, [supervising attorney] [is/is not] liable for [associate]'s misconduct; [associate] [is/is not] independently liable.

### Reporting Misconduct
🟢  ·  *cross-ref: Candor, Supervisory Duties*

**Rule.** ABA MR 8.3 imposes a mandatory duty to report to bar authorities if a lawyer knows another lawyer has committed a violation that raises a substantial question as to that lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness, or fitness as a lawyer, unless the information is protected by confidentiality (MR 1.6) or obtained through a lawyer assistance program. There is no equivalent duty to report judges, although MR 8.3(b) addresses that separately.

**Here.** Here, [attorney] learned that [other lawyer] [committed specific violation]. Under ABA, [attorney] [must/need not] report, depending on whether [the violation is substantial / confidentiality applies].

**Thus.** Thus, under ABA [attorney] [must/need not] report; under CA, there is no equivalent obligation.

> **CA divergence:** California has NO counterpart to ABA MR 8.3. California lawyers have *no duty* to report other attorneys' misconduct to the State Bar. This is one of the highest-yield CA divergences on the bar exam — always flag it explicitly.

### Legal Malpractice
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Competence, Diligence, Conflicts*

**Rule.** Legal malpractice requires proof of four elements: (1) existence of an attorney-client relationship establishing a duty of care; (2) breach of that duty by acting below the standard of care (what a reasonably competent attorney would do); (3) causation — the "case within a case" rule requires the plaintiff to prove the underlying matter would have succeeded but for the malpractice; and (4) actual damages. In California, a former criminal defendant seeking malpractice must also demonstrate actual innocence of the underlying offense.

**Here.** Here, [attorney]'s [error] caused [client] to [lose the case / suffer damages]. [Client] must show [attorney]'s conduct fell below the standard of care and that but for the error, [client] would have [prevailed / obtained better result]. In a criminal matter, [client] must also establish actual innocence.

**Thus.** Thus, [client] [has/does not have] a viable malpractice claim against [attorney].

### Unauthorized Practice of Law
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Supervisory Duties, Out-of-State Lawyers*

**Rule.** A lawyer shall not assist a nonlawyer in the unauthorized practice of law and shall not practice law in a jurisdiction where the lawyer is not licensed, except as permitted by that jurisdiction's rules. ABA MR 5.5; CRPC 5.5. Temporary multijurisdictional practice may be permitted for: (a) matters associated with a pending proceeding in which the lawyer is authorized or reasonably expects to be authorized (pro hac vice); (b) matters reasonably related to the lawyer's home jurisdiction practice; (c) alternative dispute resolution; or (d) matters reasonably expected to conclude without establishing an ongoing presence.

**Here.** Here, [out-of-state lawyer] [set up office in CA / regularly handled CA matters without pro hac vice admission]. This [constitutes/does not constitute] UPL because [the temporary practice exceptions do/do not apply].

**Thus.** Thus, [lawyer] [did/did not] engage in unauthorized practice.

### Imputed Disqualification
🟡  ·  *cross-ref: Former Client Conflicts, Concurrent Conflicts, Screening*

**Rule.** Under ABA MR 1.10, when any lawyer in a firm is disqualified from representation due to a conflict (other than from personal-interest conflicts under MR 1.8), no lawyer in the firm may represent the affected client, *unless*: (1) the disqualification is based on personal interest and does not present a significant risk of material limitation; or (2) the disqualified lawyer is timely screened from the matter, receives no fee portion from the matter, and prompt written notice is given to the affected former client. California CRPC 1.10 similarly permits timely screening to rebut imputation when a laterally moving lawyer is the disqualified attorney.

**Here.** Here, [Lawyer A] moved from [Firm X] to [Firm Y] with a conflict from the prior firm. [Firm Y] [did/did not] timely screen [Lawyer A]; [did/did not] ensure no fee-sharing; [did/did not] provide prompt notice. If all three steps were taken, imputation [is/is not] rebutted under both ABA and CA.

**Thus.** Thus, [Firm Y] [is/is not] disqualified from [the representation].

## Part 3 — Crossovers & Exam Tactics

- **Common pairings:**
  - PR + Torts (malpractice): breach of the Rules as evidence of negligence; standard of care analysis runs parallel.
  - PR + Contracts: fee agreement enforceability under §6147–6148; contingent fee disputes; quantum meruit.
  - PR + Evidence: attorney-client privilege is an evidence rule; distinguish from the broader ethical duty constantly.
  - PR + Civil Procedure: disqualification motions; imputed conflicts in litigation; ex parte rules.
  - PR + Criminal Law/Procedure: client perjury, criminal defense contingent fee prohibition, malpractice actual-innocence requirement.

- **Sleeper issues:**
  - Duty to prospective clients — examinees forget that even a one-time consultation creates confidentiality obligations and potential disqualification.
  - Written fee agreement requirement for fees over $1,000 — examinees cite ABA (which doesn't require it) and miss CA §6148.
  - CA referral fee permission — examinees apply the ABA prohibition without noting CA's divergence.
  - No ABA 8.3 equivalent in CA — examinees assume the duty to report exists in California; it does not.
  - Imputation screening — examinees conclude an entire firm is disqualified without analyzing whether timely screening was implemented.
  - "Relating to the representation" in confidentiality — examinees narrow the duty to communications; it covers *all* information regardless of source.

- **CA-divergence cheat list:**
  1. Confidentiality exceptions: CA only permits disclosure to prevent death/substantial bodily harm; no financial-crime exception (§6068(e) / CRPC 1.6).
  2. Fee standard: CA = "unconscionable" (higher client bar); ABA = "reasonable."
  3. Written fee agreements: CA §6148 requires writing for fees foreseeably exceeding $1,000; ABA has no such general requirement.
  4. Referral fees: CA permits under specified conditions; ABA generally prohibits pure referral fees.
  5. Reporting misconduct: CA has NO duty to report other lawyers (no MR 8.3 equivalent).
  6. Client perjury / narrative approach: CA more explicitly recognizes the narrative approach as a permissible response.
  7. Sexual relations with clients: CA CRPC 1.8.10 expressly prohibits absent a pre-existing relationship; same policy as ABA but separately codified.
  8. Imputed disqualification / screening: CA CRPC 1.10 permits timely screening for lateral hires, consistent with amended ABA MR 1.10.

- **Call-of-the-question patterns:**
  - "What ethical violations, if any, has Attorney committed?" — broad: cover every issue in fact pattern, both ABA and CA.
  - "May Attorney represent Client B?" — focus on conflicts analysis (concurrent or former client); always include consent analysis.
  - "Is the fee agreement enforceable?" — go to §6147/§6148 writing requirements first, then reasonableness/unconscionability.
  - "What should Attorney do?" — signals candor, client perjury, withdrawal, or trust account issues; answer procedurally.
  - "Discuss the ethical obligations of all attorneys" — flag supervisory/subordinate duties for multiple actors.
  - "Does Attorney have a duty to disclose?" — triggers confidentiality vs. candor tension; apply both ABA and CA exceptions.
