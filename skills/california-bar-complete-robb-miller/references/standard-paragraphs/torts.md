## A. Intentional Torts — General Framework

### General Intent & Volitional Act 🟢
An intentional tort requires a volitional act by the defendant, coupled with either specific intent (the defendant's conscious purpose is to produce the prohibited consequence) or general intent (the defendant knows with substantial certainty that the consequence will result). There are no incapacity defenses — minors, persons with mental illness, and intoxicated individuals are all capable of forming tortious intent. A plaintiff's hypersensitivity is never factored into whether an element of the claim is satisfied.
*Apply: [D]'s act of [doing X] was volitional; [D] either specifically sought to [result] or knew with substantial certainty that [result] would follow because [facts].*
> **Thus,** the intent element is [satisfied / not satisfied].

### Transferred Intent 🔴
Under the transferred-intent doctrine, a defendant's intent to commit one of the five eligible torts — battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land, or trespass to chattels — against one person transfers to support liability when that same or a different eligible tort is instead inflicted upon a different victim. The doctrine permits the intent aimed at Person A to satisfy the intent element of the tort actually committed against unintended Person B, or to satisfy intent for a different but eligible tort than the one originally planned.
*Apply: [D] intended to [commit tort X against Person A] but instead [committed tort Y against Person B]; because both torts are within the five eligible torts, intent transfers.*
> **Thus,** [D] satisfies the intent element of [tort] against [P].

## B. Intentional Torts to the Person

### Battery 🔴
Battery is the intentional infliction of a harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff's person. "Harmful" means causing physical injury; "offensive" means contact that a reasonable person of ordinary sensibilities would find objectionable. The contact need not be directly with the plaintiff's body — contact with anything closely connected to the plaintiff (clothing, a cane, a held object) suffices. The plaintiff need not be aware of the contact at the time it occurs.
*Apply: [D] [struck / touched / caused object to contact] [P or something closely connected to P]; the contact was [harmful because [injury] / offensive because a reasonable person would find [the nature of contact] objectionable]; [D] acted with [specific / general] intent.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for battery.

### Assault 🟢
Assault is an intentional act by the defendant that creates in the plaintiff a reasonable apprehension of an imminent harmful or offensive contact to the plaintiff's person. The plaintiff must actually perceive and be apprehensive of the threatened contact; words alone generally do not constitute assault, though words combined with overt threatening conduct may. The defendant's apparent — not necessarily actual — ability to carry out the threat is sufficient.
*Apply: [D] [raised fist / pointed weapon / made threatening gesture] in [P]'s presence; [P] reasonably apprehended [imminent harmful/offensive contact] because [D appeared able and ready to carry it out]; [D] acted with intent.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for assault.

### False Imprisonment 🟢
False imprisonment is the intentional confinement of the plaintiff to a bounded area against the plaintiff's will, brought about by the defendant's act or omission. Confinement may be achieved through physical barriers, physical force, threats of immediate force, or the assertion of legal authority. The plaintiff must be aware of the confinement or suffer actual harm from it. A means of escape that is reasonable and known to the plaintiff negates confinement; an unreasonable or unknown means of escape does not.
*Apply: [D] [locked door / blocked exit / threatened P with force / asserted false authority] confining [P] to [the room / vehicle / area]; [P] was aware of the confinement; no reasonable means of escape was [available / known to P].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for false imprisonment.

### Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (IIED) 🔴
IIED requires that the defendant engage in extreme and outrageous conduct — conduct that exceeds all bounds tolerated by a civilized society — with the intent to cause severe emotional distress or with reckless disregard of the high probability that such distress will result, and that the plaintiff actually suffer severe emotional distress as a result. Merely insulting, annoying, or hurtful conduct does not rise to extreme and outrageous. Where the defendant directs outrageous conduct toward a third person and a bystander plaintiff suffers severe emotional distress, the bystander may recover if: (1) the plaintiff was present at the scene, (2) the plaintiff is a close relative of the person directly targeted, and (3) the defendant knew of those facts.
*Apply: [D]'s conduct of [describe conduct] — [continuous harassment / threats / humiliation in public / exploiting known vulnerability] — was extreme and outrageous because [reasons]; [D] [intended / was reckless as to] causing severe emotional distress; [P] suffered [severe manifestations of distress].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for IIED.

## C. Intentional Torts to Property

### Trespass to Land 🟢
Trespass to land is the intentional physical invasion of the plaintiff's real property. The defendant need only intend the act that causes the intrusion; the defendant need not know the land belongs to another. The invasion may be by the defendant's person, by an object the defendant placed or set in motion, or by causing a third person to enter. Actual damages are not required — the tort is complete upon the intentional entry, and nominal damages are available.
*Apply: [D] [physically entered / drove vehicle onto / threw object onto / caused water to flood] [P]'s land; [D] intended the act; [D need not have known the land was P's].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for trespass to land.

### Trespass to Chattels 🟡
Trespass to chattels is an intentional act that interferes with the plaintiff's right to possess a chattel, causing actual damage. Unlike conversion, trespass to chattels addresses interferences that are less serious in degree — temporary dispossession, damage falling short of the chattel's full value, or impairment of the chattel's condition or utility. The plaintiff must show actual harm: dispossession for a meaningful time, impairment of the chattel's value or condition, or deprivation of the plaintiff's use of it.
*Apply: [D] [damaged / temporarily took / interfered with P's use of] [chattel]; the interference was less than a full deprivation or complete destruction; [P] suffered actual harm in the form of [loss of use / reduced value / damage].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for trespass to chattels.

### Conversion 🟢
Conversion is an intentional act that interferes so seriously with the plaintiff's right to possess a chattel that it is fair to require the defendant to pay the chattel's full fair market value. Courts assess severity by the extent and duration of the defendant's control, the defendant's intent to assert dominion, the harm done to the chattel, and the inconvenience and expense caused to the plaintiff. The remedy is forced sale: the defendant pays the full value, not just repair costs.
*Apply: [D] [destroyed / sold / substantially altered / held for a substantial period] [P]'s [chattel]; the interference was so serious — [reasons for severity] — that [D] should be required to pay its full value of $[X].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for conversion.

## D. Defenses to Intentional Torts

### Consent 🟢
Consent is a complete defense to an intentional tort that would otherwise be established. Consent may be express (given verbally or in writing) or implied (arising from custom, usage, or the defendant's reasonable interpretation of the plaintiff's objective conduct). Consent obtained by fraud or duress is invalid. The defendant must not exceed the scope of the consent given — conduct beyond what was consented to remains tortious. Only individuals with legal capacity can give effective consent.
*Apply: [P] [expressly signed a release / participated in activity where consent is implied by custom / made no objection when a reasonable person would have objected]; [D]'s conduct [did / did not] stay within the boundaries of that consent; [P] [had / lacked] legal capacity.*
> **Thus,** consent [is / is not] a valid defense.

### Self-Defense 🟢
A person who reasonably believes they are being or are about to be attacked may use such force as is reasonably necessary to protect against the threatened injury. The privilege extends to the use of force proportionate to the perceived threat; deadly force is justified only where the defendant reasonably fears death or serious bodily harm. A reasonable mistake about the necessity of self-defense does not vitiate the privilege, but an unreasonable mistake does.
*Apply: [D] reasonably believed [P] was [about to strike / attacking] [D]; [D] used [force proportionate to the threat]; [D]'s belief was [reasonable / unreasonable] given [facts].*
> **Thus,** self-defense [is / is not] available to [D].

### Defense of Others 🟢
A defendant may use reasonable force to defend a third person when the defendant reasonably believes the third person would have been privileged to use that force in their own self-defense. The modern majority rule focuses on the reasonableness of the defendant's belief rather than requiring the third party to actually have been entitled to self-defense. The amount of force used must still be proportionate to the perceived threat.
*Apply: [D] used force to protect [third person] from [P]; [D] reasonably believed [third person] faced imminent [harm]; the force [D] used was [proportionate / disproportionate].*
> **Thus,** defense of others [applies / does not apply].

### Defense of Property 🟡
An owner or possessor of property may use reasonable force to prevent the commission of a tort against their real or personal property. Before resorting to force, the defendant must first make a demand that the intruder stop or leave, unless such a request would be futile or dangerous. The privilege permits only that force reasonably necessary to protect the property and does not extend to deadly force — one may not use a spring gun or similar lethal device to protect property alone. The defense is unavailable against someone who has a privilege to enter or use the property.
*Apply: [D] used force to prevent [P]'s [trespass / taking]; [D] [did / did not] first request [P] to desist; the force used was [reasonable / excessive — deadly force not permitted to protect property alone].*
> **Thus,** defense of property [applies / does not apply].

### Necessity — Public 🟡
Public necessity arises when the defendant destroys or interferes with another's property to prevent a greater harm to the community at large. Because the burden falls on many and the benefit flows to the public, public necessity is a complete defense — the defendant owes no compensation. The defendant must act reasonably and in good faith, and the threatened harm to the community must substantially outweigh the harm inflicted on the individual property owner.
*Apply: [D] [destroyed / damaged] [P]'s property to prevent [fire / flood / epidemic] from spreading to [the community]; the community threat was substantially more serious than the individual loss; the privilege is complete.*
> **Thus,** public necessity is a complete defense and [D] owes no compensation.

### Necessity — Private 🔴
Private necessity arises when the defendant interferes with another's property to protect the defendant's own person or property (or a third person's) from a greater threatened harm. Unlike public necessity, private necessity is an incomplete defense: it provides a privilege to enter or remain on the property — the property owner may not use force to eject the defendant — but the defendant must pay for any actual damage caused. The defendant avoids liability for the act of trespass itself but remains liable for resulting harm.
*Apply: [D] [entered / damaged] [P]'s property to save [D / a third person / D's property] from [threatened harm]; the threatened injury was substantially more serious; but [D] is still liable for the actual damage of $[X] caused to [P]'s property.*
> **Thus,** private necessity excuses the trespass but [D] must compensate [P] for actual harm.

## E. Defamation

### Defamation — Common-Law Elements 🔴
Defamation is a false statement of fact about the plaintiff, communicated to a third person, that damages the plaintiff's reputation. The statement must be defamatory — meaning it tends to harm the plaintiff's reputation by lowering the plaintiff in the estimation of the community or by deterring third persons from associating with the plaintiff. Publication requires only that the statement be intentionally or negligently communicated to at least one person other than the plaintiff. Libel (written or recorded) traditionally requires no proof of special damages; slander (spoken) requires proof of special damages unless the statement falls into a slander per se category.
*Apply: [D] made a statement that [P committed a crime / has a loathsome disease / lacks professional fitness / is unchaste]; the statement was communicated to [third person]; the statement [is / is not] libel per se or slander per se.*
> **Thus,** [D] has [satisfied / failed] the common-law defamation elements.

### Defamation — Constitutional Overlay (Falsity & Fault) 🔴
Under the First Amendment framework established in *New York Times v. Sullivan* and its progeny, where the defamatory statement involves a matter of public concern, the plaintiff must additionally prove that the statement was false and that the defendant acted with the requisite degree of fault. A public official or public figure must prove the defendant acted with actual malice — knowledge that the statement was false or reckless disregard of its truth or falsity — by clear and convincing evidence. A private plaintiff need only prove negligence as to falsity (the *Gertz* standard), though some states require actual malice for presumed or punitive damages even for private plaintiffs.
*Apply: [P] is a [public official / public figure / private individual]; the statement concerned a [matter of public / private] concern; [D] acted with [actual malice because D knew it was false / recklessness / negligence] because [facts].*
> **Thus,** [P] [can / cannot] satisfy the constitutional fault requirement.

### Defamation — Defenses and Privileges 🟡
Truth is an absolute defense to defamation — a true statement cannot be defamatory regardless of the defendant's motive. Consent is also an absolute defense. Absolute privileges — which can never be lost — apply to statements made in legislative proceedings, judicial proceedings, and between spouses. Qualified (conditional) privileges exist where there is a legitimate social interest in candid communication, such as statements made to protect a common interest or in good-faith employment references; these privileges may be lost if the defendant acts outside the scope of the privilege or with actual malice.
*Apply: [D]'s statement was [true / made in a judicial proceeding / made in a qualified-privilege context such as an employment reference]; [D] [did / did not] abuse the qualified privilege by acting [maliciously / beyond its scope].*
> **Thus,** the [absolute / qualified] privilege [applies / has been forfeited].

### Libel vs. Slander & Slander Per Se 🟡
Libel is defamation in written or otherwise recorded and permanent form; slander is defamation in transitory spoken form. For libel, general damages to reputation are presumed without proof of pecuniary loss. For slander, the plaintiff must ordinarily prove special damages (actual economic loss) unless the statement falls within one of four slander per se categories: (1) the plaintiff committed a crime of moral turpitude; (2) the plaintiff has a loathsome or communicable disease; (3) the statement relates to the plaintiff's trade, business, or profession in a way that is incompatible with its proper exercise; or (4) the plaintiff is unchaste or sexually immoral (traditionally applied to women, though the category survives in many jurisdictions). Statements within a slander per se category support presumed general damages without proof of specific economic loss.
*Apply: [D]'s spoken statement accused [P] of [crime / disease / professional misconduct / sexual immorality]; because the statement falls within slander per se category [#], [P] need not prove special damages; general damages are presumed.*
> **Thus,** [D]'s statement is slander per se and [P] may recover general damages without proof of pecuniary loss.

## F. Privacy Torts

### Appropriation of Name or Likeness 🟢
The tort of appropriation protects the plaintiff's right to control the commercial use of their own identity. The plaintiff must show that the defendant made an unauthorized use of the plaintiff's name, photograph, likeness, or identity for the defendant's commercial advantage. Liability is generally limited to uses in advertising or promotion of products or services; mere incidental or newsworthy use does not create liability.
*Apply: [D] used [P]'s [name / photo / likeness] in [an advertisement / commercial promotion] without [P]'s consent; the use was for [D]'s commercial advantage by selling/promoting [product/service].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for appropriation of name or likeness.

### Intrusion Upon Seclusion 🟢
The intrusion tort imposes liability for intentionally intruding — physically or otherwise — upon the plaintiff's solitude, seclusion, or private affairs in a manner that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. The intrusion itself is the wrong; publication or further dissemination is not required. The plaintiff must have had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the area or matter intruded upon — there is no privacy interest in matters exposed to the public.
*Apply: [D] [installed hidden camera / wiretapped / physically entered] [P]'s [home / private space / private communications]; the intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person; [P] had a reasonable expectation of privacy in [the space/matter].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for intrusion upon seclusion.

### False Light 🟡
The false-light tort protects against the widespread attribution to the plaintiff of views, statements, or conduct that the plaintiff does not hold or engage in, where the false impression created would be highly offensive to a reasonable person and the defendant acted with the requisite fault. Unlike defamation, false light need not harm reputation in the traditional sense — the concern is a false impression of the plaintiff's beliefs or conduct. Because false light often overlaps with defamation, courts applying constitutional standards require actual malice where the plaintiff is a public figure or the matter involves public concern.
*Apply: [D] published/broadcast [content] attributing to [P] views/actions [P] did not hold or take; the false impression was widespread; a reasonable person would find the portrayal highly offensive; [D] acted with [actual malice / negligence].*
> **Thus,** [D] is [or is not] liable for false light.

### Public Disclosure of Private Facts 🟡
Liability arises where the defendant publicly discloses genuinely private information about the plaintiff, the disclosure would be highly offensive to a reasonable person of ordinary sensibilities, and the matter disclosed is not of legitimate public concern. The disclosure must be widespread — communication to a small group does not suffice. Truth is not a defense to this tort because the gravamen is the violation of privacy, not falsity.
*Apply: [D] widely disclosed [private medical / financial / sexual] information about [P]; [P] did not consent; the information was genuinely private, not previously made public; the disclosure would be highly offensive to a reasonable person; the information was [not] of legitimate public concern.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for public disclosure of private facts.

## G. Misrepresentation

### Intentional Misrepresentation (Fraud/Deceit) 🔴
Intentional misrepresentation (fraud) requires: (1) a misrepresentation of a material fact by the defendant — opinion and pure puffery are generally not actionable, and there is ordinarily no duty to disclose absent a special relationship or active concealment; (2) scienter — the defendant knew the statement was false or had no basis for believing it was true; (3) intent to induce the plaintiff's reliance; (4) actual and justifiable reliance by the plaintiff; and (5) resulting damages. There are no affirmative defenses to an otherwise established fraud claim.
*Apply: [D] stated [specific false fact]; [D] knew it was false because [facts]; [D] made the statement to induce [P] to [buy / contract / invest]; [P] actually and justifiably relied by [action taken]; [P] suffered damages of [amount/type].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for intentional misrepresentation.

### Negligent Misrepresentation 🟡
Negligent misrepresentation imposes liability when the defendant, in the course of a business or profession, provides false information without exercising reasonable care to verify its accuracy, and the plaintiff justifiably relies on that information to their detriment. Unlike fraud, scienter is not required; the plaintiff need only show a breach of the professional duty of care toward a foreseeable plaintiff who would rely on the information. Recovery is typically limited to economic loss in commercial transactions.
*Apply: [D], acting in [professional / business] capacity as [accountant / broker / appraiser], provided [P] with false information regarding [matter]; [D] failed to exercise reasonable care in verifying [the information]; [P] justifiably relied and suffered [damages].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for negligent misrepresentation.

## H. Wrongful Institution of Proceedings

### Malicious Prosecution 🟡
Malicious prosecution requires the plaintiff to establish: (1) the defendant initiated or continued criminal proceedings against the plaintiff; (2) those proceedings terminated in the plaintiff's favor; (3) the defendant lacked probable cause to initiate the proceedings; (4) the defendant acted with malice — an improper motive beyond mere desire to see justice done; and (5) the plaintiff suffered damages. Prosecutors acting within their official capacity enjoy absolute immunity and cannot be sued for malicious prosecution.
*Apply: [D] filed [criminal charges] against [P]; the proceedings terminated in [P]'s favor by [acquittal / dismissal]; [D] lacked probable cause because [facts]; [D]'s motive was [improper — revenge / harassment / improper purpose]; [P] suffered [reputational / financial / emotional] damages.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for malicious prosecution.

### Abuse of Process 🟡
Abuse of process lies where the defendant uses a legal process — civil or criminal — for an ulterior purpose not proper to that process, accompanied by a willful act in the use of that process to accomplish the improper end. Unlike malicious prosecution, abuse of process does not require termination of the underlying proceedings in the plaintiff's favor; the wrong is the misuse of a properly-initiated proceeding as a weapon to coerce or injure the plaintiff for some collateral purpose.
*Apply: [D] [filed subpoena / issued attachment / initiated proceeding] for the ulterior purpose of [coercing settlement / extracting concession unrelated to the litigation]; [D] then took the overt act of [threatening / pressuring P] to accomplish that improper purpose.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for abuse of process.

## I. Interference with Business Relations

### Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations 🟢
A plaintiff may recover where: (1) a valid contractual relationship existed between the plaintiff and a third party, or the plaintiff had a valid business expectancy; (2) the defendant had knowledge of that relationship or expectancy; (3) the defendant intentionally induced a breach or disruption of the relationship or expectancy; and (4) the plaintiff suffered resulting damages. The defendant's interference must be improper — a defendant who acts for legitimate competitive reasons and uses proper means may have a privilege to compete.
*Apply: [P] had a [contract / business expectancy] with [third party]; [D] knew of the [relationship]; [D] intentionally [persuaded third party to breach / offered better terms to undermine] the relationship; [P] suffered damages of [amount / loss of business].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for intentional interference with contractual relations.

## J. Negligence — Duty

### Duty of Care — General (Cardozo/Andrews) 🔴
Every person owes a duty of reasonable care to all foreseeable plaintiffs — those in the zone of foreseeable danger created by the defendant's conduct. Under the majority Cardozo view (*Palsgraf*), a defendant owes a duty only to plaintiffs foreseeably at risk from the defendant's negligent act; there is no duty to unforeseeable plaintiffs. The minority Andrews view treats duty as running to the world at large and addresses unforeseeable plaintiffs through proximate cause analysis. On the California bar, identify which view applies if the plaintiff's foreseeability is in question.
*Apply: [D]'s negligent act of [conduct] created a foreseeable risk of harm to [P] because [P was in the zone of danger / P was foreseeable in light of the circumstances]; [address Andrews view if plaintiff seems unforeseeable].*
> **Thus,** [D] [did / did not] owe [P] a duty under the [Cardozo / Andrews] view.

### Duty — General Standard of Care (Reasonable Person) 🟢
The standard of care in negligence is that of a reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances — an objective standard that does not account for a particular defendant's subjective intentions, idiosyncrasies, or lack of skill. The reasonable person is endowed with the same physical characteristics as the defendant (a blind person is measured against a reasonable blind person) but mental or emotional limitations are generally not taken into account. The standard demands only ordinary care, not perfection.
*Apply: A reasonably prudent person in [D]'s position, faced with [the circumstances], would have [taken precautions / acted differently] because [the foreseeable risk / the ease of precaution]; [D]'s conduct of [doing X] [fell short of / met] that standard.*
> **Thus,** [D] [breached / did not breach] the reasonable person standard.

### Duty — Professionals 🟢
A professional — such as a physician, attorney, or accountant — is held to the standard of care exercised by the reasonably competent member of that profession in good standing, not the general reasonable person standard. A specialist is held to the standard of the reasonably competent specialist in that field. In medical malpractice cases, the plaintiff generally must establish the professional standard through expert testimony.
*Apply: [D], a [physician / attorney / engineer], was required to exercise the skill and care of a reasonably competent [specialist / general practitioner] in that field; [D]'s conduct of [omission or act] [fell below / met] that professional standard because [expert evidence / obvious deviation].*
> **Thus,** [D] [did / did not] breach the professional standard of care.

### Duty — Children 🟢
A child defendant is held to the standard of care of a reasonably prudent child of the same age, intelligence, maturity, and experience — a subjective-objective standard more lenient than the adult reasonable person standard. The exception applies when the child is engaged in an adult activity — such as operating a motorized vehicle or power equipment — in which case the child is held to the adult standard of care.
*Apply: [D], a [age]-year-old child, was [playing / engaged in activity]; the standard is that of a reasonably prudent child of [D]'s age, intelligence, and experience [unless D was operating a motorized vehicle — then apply adult standard].*
> **Thus,** [D]'s conduct [was / was not] negligent under the applicable child standard.

### Duty — Landowners to Land Entrants (CA Rowland Approach) 🔴
The traditional common-law approach categorizes land entrants as trespassers, licensees, or invitees and assigns different duty standards to each. California has largely abandoned this classification under *Rowland v. Christian* (1968), replacing it with a general duty of reasonable care owed by landowners to all persons on the premises, with the entrant's status as merely one factor in the reasonableness analysis. Factors include foreseeability of harm, burden of the precaution, and the landowner's ability to prevent the risk.
*Apply: Under CA's [Rowland] general-duty approach, [D] landowner owed [P] a duty of reasonable care; the relevant factors — foreseeability [high/low], burden of precaution [low/high], [P]'s status as [trespasser/licensee/invitee] — [do/do not] support imposing a duty.*
> **CA divergence:** CA applies *Rowland v. Christian*'s general reasonable-care standard for land entrants rather than the traditional trespasser/licensee/invitee trichotomy; that traditional classification governs on the MBE.

> **Thus,** under California law, [D] [owed / did not owe] a duty of reasonable care to [P].

### Duty — Trespassers (Traditional Rule) 🟡
Under the traditional common-law rule, a landowner owes no duty to undiscovered trespassers. Once a trespasser is discovered or their presence is reasonably anticipated, the landowner owes a duty to warn of, or make safe, known and concealed artificial (human-made) conditions that pose a risk of death or serious bodily harm. With respect to the landowner's active conduct (activities), the landowner must exercise reasonable prudence under the circumstances once the trespasser is discovered.
*Apply: [P] was a trespasser; [D] [knew of / should have anticipated] [P]'s presence because [facts]; the [concealed artificial condition / activity] posed a risk of death or serious injury; [D] [did / did not] warn [P] or make the condition safe.*
> **Thus,** [D] [did / did not] breach the duty owed to a discovered/anticipated trespasser.

### Duty — Attractive Nuisance (Child Trespassers) 🟢
A landowner has a duty of ordinary care to avoid a reasonably foreseeable risk of harm to trespassing children caused by artificial conditions on the property. The plaintiff must show: (1) a dangerous artificial condition exists on the land that the owner knows or should know of; (2) the owner knows or should know that children are likely to trespass in the area; (3) the condition poses a risk of death or serious bodily harm to children who, because of their youth, cannot appreciate the danger; and (4) the burden of eliminating the hazard is slight compared to the risk.
*Apply: [D]'s [pool / machinery / pit] was an artificial dangerous condition; [D] knew/should have known children frequented the area because [facts]; children [of P's age] could not appreciate the danger because [lack of experience/understanding]; the cost of a fence/barrier was minimal compared to the risk.*
> **Thus,** [D] owed a duty of care under the attractive nuisance doctrine.

### Duty — Licensees 🟡
A social guest or other licensee enters the land with the landowner's express or implied permission but for the licensee's own purpose, not the landowner's business benefit. The landowner owes a licensee: (1) a duty to warn of all known concealed dangerous conditions on the land that the licensee is unlikely to discover; and (2) a duty to exercise reasonable prudence in conducting active operations on the premises. There is no duty to inspect for unknown dangers for licensees.
*Apply: [P] was a social guest / licensee; [D] knew of the hidden [defect / condition] and [did / did not] warn [P]; [alternatively, D was conducting an activity and exercised / failed to exercise reasonable care].*
> **Thus,** [D] [breached / did not breach] the duty owed to [P] as a licensee.

### Duty — Invitees 🟢
A business invitee enters the land for a purpose connected with the landowner's business, or is a member of the public invited to enter premises open to the public. An invitee is owed the highest duty: (1) reasonable care in conducting activities; (2) a duty to warn of all known concealed dangers; and (3) a duty to make reasonable inspections of the premises and make safe any dangerous conditions discovered — this inspection duty distinguishes the invitee standard from the licensee standard.
*Apply: [P] was a business invitee / public invitee because [visiting store / attending event]; [D] had a duty to inspect and [D] [did/did not] discover the condition through reasonable inspection; [D] [warned / failed to warn] [P] of [the danger].*
> **Thus,** [D] [breached / did not breach] the duty owed to an invitee.

### Duty — Negligence Per Se 🔴
When a statute or regulation imposes a specific standard of conduct with a criminal penalty, a defendant who violates that statute is negligent per se — meaning breach of duty is conclusively established — if: (1) the statute clearly defines the required conduct; (2) the plaintiff is within the class of persons the statute was designed to protect; and (3) the harm suffered is of the type the statute was designed to prevent. Exceptions excuse violation where compliance was impossible (sudden emergency, emergency created by another) or more dangerous than noncompliance.
*Apply: [D] violated [statute/code section] requiring [specific conduct]; [P] is within the class of [persons protected]; [P]'s harm — [type of injury] — is within the type of harm the statute was designed to prevent; no exception applies because [no impossibility / emergency].*
> **Thus,** [D] is negligent per se and breach is established.

### Duty — No Affirmative Duty to Act / Special Relationships 🟡
Generally, a person has no legal duty to come to the aid of another who is in peril. However, a duty to act arises where: (1) the defendant's own negligent conduct created the peril; (2) the defendant voluntarily assumed the duty by beginning a rescue and must not leave the victim in a worse position; (3) a special relationship exists between the parties (carrier-passenger, employer-employee, innkeeper-guest, jailer-prisoner); or (4) a statute imposes an affirmative duty to act.
*Apply: [D] had no general duty to aid [P] unless [D's prior negligence created P's peril / D undertook a rescue and then abandoned P in a worse condition / a special relationship existed between D and P].*
> **Thus,** [D] [did / did not] owe an affirmative duty to act.

### Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress (NIED) — Direct Victim 🟡
A direct-victim plaintiff may recover for negligent infliction of emotional distress where the defendant's negligent conduct placed the plaintiff within a physical zone of danger — so that the plaintiff reasonably feared for their own physical safety — and the plaintiff subsequently suffered physical manifestations of the resulting emotional distress. Some jurisdictions require both the zone-of-danger element and a physical manifestation; others allow recovery for foreseeable emotional harm without requiring physical symptoms.
*Apply: [D]'s negligent act of [conduct] placed [P] in the zone of danger because [P was physically threatened]; [P] did not sustain an immediate physical impact but suffered physical manifestations including [symptoms] from the resulting emotional distress.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable to [P] as a direct-victim NIED plaintiff.

### NIED — Bystander (CA Dillon/Thing Factors) 🔴
Under California's bystander NIED rule, a plaintiff who witnesses negligent injury to a third person may recover for emotional distress if: (1) the plaintiff and the directly injured person are closely related (the *Thing* requirement); (2) the plaintiff was present at the scene of the injury-causing event at the time it occurred; and (3) the plaintiff personally observed or perceived the injury (not learning of it through subsequent news). California moved from the flexible foreseeability approach of *Dillon v. Legg* to the categorical *Thing v. La Chusa* (1989) factors, which must all be satisfied.
*Apply: [P] is the [parent / spouse / sibling] of [injured person] — a close relative; [P] was present at the scene when [D] negligently [caused the accident]; [P] contemporaneously perceived [the injury] not through later news; [P] suffered serious emotional distress.*
> **CA divergence:** CA uses the categorical *Thing v. La Chusa* (1989) three-part test — close relation, presence at the scene, direct sensory perception — not a general foreseeability standard.

> **Thus,** [P] [can / cannot] recover as a bystander NIED plaintiff under California law.

## K. Negligence — Breach

### Breach of Duty — Reasonable Care Standard 🟢
Breach occurs when the defendant's conduct falls short of the applicable standard of care. The assessment is objective: would a reasonably prudent person have acted differently under the same circumstances? Courts examine the foreseeable probability of harm, the foreseeable severity of harm, the burden of taking precautions, and the social utility of the defendant's conduct (the *Carroll Towing* balancing approach). Where the calculus shows the burden of precaution was low and the risk was high, breach is easily established.
*Apply: The foreseeable probability of harm was [high/low] because [facts]; the foreseeable severity was [serious/minor]; the burden of precaution — [wearing safety gear / installing a guard / posting a warning] — was [minimal / significant]; [D]'s failure to [take the precaution] therefore [was / was not] a breach.*
> **Thus,** [D] [did / did not] breach the duty of care.

### Res Ipsa Loquitur 🔴
Res ipsa loquitur ("the thing speaks for itself") permits an inference of negligence where: (1) the accident is of a type that would not ordinarily occur in the absence of negligence; and (2) the negligence is attributable to the defendant because the instrumentality causing the harm was within the defendant's exclusive control (or, in some jurisdictions, that the defendant was a responsible party). Res ipsa does not shift the burden of proof — it creates a permissible inference allowing the plaintiff to survive summary judgment and reach the jury without direct evidence of what went wrong.
*Apply: [P]'s injury — [falling elevator / surgical sponge left inside / barrel rolling from warehouse window] — is the type that does not ordinarily occur absent negligence; the [elevator / instrumentality] was within [D]'s exclusive control because [facts]; [D] has offered no explanation.*
> **Thus,** [P] may rely on res ipsa loquitur to establish an inference of breach.

## L. Negligence — Causation

### Actual Cause — But-For Test 🟢
For actual (factual) causation, the plaintiff must show that but for the defendant's negligent conduct, the plaintiff's injury would not have occurred. This counterfactual inquiry asks whether the injury would have happened in the absence of the breach. Where a single defendant's act is at issue, the but-for test is the default standard. If the answer to "would the harm have occurred anyway?" is no, actual cause is established.
*Apply: But for [D]'s failure to [act / take precaution], [P] would not have suffered [the injury] because [explain how the precaution would have prevented it].*
> **Thus,** [D]'s negligence was the but-for actual cause of [P]'s injury.

### Actual Cause — Substantial Factor (Joint Causes) 🟡
Where two or more defendants each acted negligently and either cause alone would have been sufficient to produce the plaintiff's indivisible harm, the but-for test yields unsatisfactory results (each defendant could argue their conduct was not necessary). In such cases, a defendant's conduct is a cause in fact if it was a substantial factor in producing the harm. Both defendants remain liable under joint and several liability.
*Apply: Both [D1]'s [conduct] and [D2]'s [conduct] contributed to [P]'s injury, and either alone would have been sufficient to cause it; each was a substantial factor in bringing about the harm; the but-for test would exonerate both, so the substantial-factor standard applies.*
> **Thus,** both [D1] and [D2] are actual causes of [P]'s injury.

### Actual Cause — Alternative Liability (Summers v. Tice) 🟡
Where two or more defendants engaged in identical negligent conduct and only one could have caused the plaintiff's injury but it is impossible to identify which one, courts apply alternative liability: the burden shifts to each defendant to prove they did not cause the harm. If neither can do so, both are held jointly and severally liable. This doctrine requires that all potentially responsible parties be joined in the litigation.
*Apply: Both [D1] and [D2] negligently [fired guns toward P]; only one shot hit [P] and it is impossible to determine which defendant fired the harmful shot; both acted tortiously; the burden shifts to each [D] to prove they did not cause the injury.*
> **Thus,** under alternative liability, both [D1] and [D2] are liable unless one can exculpate themselves.

### Proximate Cause — General 🔴
Proximate cause (legal causation) limits a defendant's liability to the foreseeable results of their negligent conduct. A defendant is generally liable for all harmful results that are the normal and foreseeable consequences of the increased risk created by their negligence. In direct-cause cases — where no intervening force interrupts the chain — proximate cause is readily established if the harm was a foreseeable type resulting from the negligent act. The exact manner of harm need not be foreseeable; only the general type of harm must be foreseeable.
*Apply: [D]'s negligence created a foreseeable risk of [type of harm]; [P]'s [specific harm] was [the foreseeable type / an unforeseeable variation of the same type]; no intervening force interrupted the causal chain [address if one is present].*
> **Thus,** [D]'s negligence was [the / not the] proximate cause of [P]'s injury.

### Proximate Cause — Intervening and Superseding Causes 🔴
An intervening cause is a force that operates after the defendant's negligent act and contributes to the plaintiff's injury. A dependent intervening cause (one that was set in motion by or is a normal response to the defendant's negligence) does not break the causal chain — the defendant remains liable. An independent intervening cause may or may not break the chain. An intervening cause is superseding — cutting off the defendant's liability — when it is unforeseeable, extraordinary, and of a type so disconnected from the original risk that it would be unjust to hold the defendant responsible. Foreseeable intervening acts, including foreseeable negligence of rescuers, medical providers, or third parties responding to the defendant's created peril, do not supersede.
*Apply: After [D]'s negligent act, [intervening force — third party / natural event / plaintiff's own act] intervened to cause [P]'s injury; this force was [foreseeable because it is a normal response to D's negligence / unforeseeable and extraordinary] and therefore [does / does not] supersede [D]'s liability.*
> **Thus,** the intervening act [is / is not] superseding, and [D]'s liability [continues / is cut off].

## M. Negligence — Damages

### Damages — General (Eggshell Plaintiff) 🔴
In negligence, the plaintiff must prove actual damages — unlike intentional torts to land, nominal damages alone are insufficient. The eggshell (thin-skull) plaintiff rule provides that a defendant takes the plaintiff as they find them: a defendant who negligently causes harm to a plaintiff with an unknown pre-existing vulnerability is fully liable for the aggravated extent of the injury, even if a normal plaintiff would have suffered a minor injury. The rule applies to physical and psychological fragility alike once the defendant's negligence triggers an injury.
*Apply: [P] had a pre-existing [condition / fragility]; [D]'s negligence caused [P] to suffer [greatly aggravated harm] that a typical person would not have suffered to the same degree; [D] takes [P] as found and is liable for the full extent.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for [P]'s full damages under the eggshell plaintiff rule.

### Damages — Duty to Mitigate & Collateral Source 🟡
A plaintiff has a duty to take reasonable steps to mitigate their damages after the injury — failure to do so bars recovery for damages that could have been avoided by reasonable efforts. Separately, the collateral source rule provides that damages are not reduced merely because the plaintiff received compensation for the same loss from a source independent of the defendant (insurance, government benefits, gifts). Under the collateral source rule, the defendant does not receive a windfall from the plaintiff's foresight in obtaining insurance.
*Apply: [P] [failed to seek timely medical treatment / unreasonably refused a simple curative procedure]; damages attributable to [P]'s unreasonable failure to mitigate are not recoverable; [P] received insurance proceeds — under the collateral source rule, [D] may not reduce the damages award by that amount.*
> **Thus,** [address mitigation reduction / collateral source rule as applicable].

## N. Defenses to Negligence

### Comparative Negligence (CA Pure Rule) 🔴
California abolished contributory negligence and replaced it with pure comparative fault under *Li v. Yellow Cab Co.* (1975). Under pure comparative negligence, the plaintiff's recovery is reduced in proportion to their own percentage of fault — but the plaintiff may recover even if they are 99% at fault. The trier of fact apportions fault among all parties, and the plaintiff's award is reduced by their own percentage of fault. This is distinct from the modified comparative negligence rule (used in many other states) that bars recovery once the plaintiff's fault equals or exceeds 50%.
*Apply: [P] was [X]% at fault for [P]'s own injury because [P's negligent conduct]; [D] was [Y]% at fault; under California's pure comparative fault, [P]'s $[total damages] award is reduced by [X]% to $[reduced amount]; [P] is not barred from recovery even though [P was more than 50% at fault].*
> **CA divergence:** CA follows pure comparative fault (*Li v. Yellow Cab*, 1975) — plaintiff may recover regardless of degree of fault, with damages reduced proportionally. Most other states use modified (50%) comparative negligence.

> **Thus,** [P] recovers $[reduced amount] after the [X]% comparative-fault reduction.

### Assumption of Risk (CA — Folded into Comparative Fault) 🔴
Express assumption of risk arises where the plaintiff contractually agreed in advance to accept the risk of the defendant's negligence; such agreements are generally enforceable unless they are unconscionable or violate public policy. Implied assumption of risk — where the plaintiff, by their conduct, voluntarily encountered a known risk — has been largely folded into the comparative-fault analysis in California after *Li v. Yellow Cab*. Under California's current framework, secondary implied assumption of risk (plaintiff unreasonably encountered a known risk) reduces but does not bar recovery; primary implied assumption of risk (defendant owed no duty due to the inherent nature of the activity) remains a complete defense because there is no breach of duty to begin with.
*Apply: [P] [signed a release / knowingly and voluntarily entered a situation with a known risk]; [if express — enforce if not unconscionable/violating public policy]; [if implied — determine whether primary (no duty, complete defense) or secondary (unreasonable conduct, reduces recovery under comparative fault)].*
> **CA divergence:** CA folds secondary implied assumption of risk into comparative negligence analysis after *Li v. Yellow Cab*; primary implied assumption of risk remains a complete defense because it negates duty.

> **Thus,** [P]'s assumption of risk [completely bars / proportionally reduces] recovery under California law.

## O. Strict Liability

### Strict Liability — Wild Animals 🟢
The keeper of a wild animal (one that belongs to a species that is not normally domesticated) is strictly liable for any harm the animal causes to persons or property, regardless of the owner's precautions or the animal's past docile behavior. No prior showing of dangerous propensities is required. The strict liability rule applies because wild animals retain their inherently dangerous nature.
*Apply: [D] kept [wild animal — lion / tiger / bear / venomous snake]; [the animal] injured [P]; no showing of fault is required; strict liability applies because the animal is a wild species not commonly domesticated.*
> **Thus,** [D] is strictly liable for [P]'s injuries.

### Strict Liability — Domesticated Animals (Known Dangerous Propensity) 🟡
The keeper of a domesticated animal — such as a dog or cat — is generally held to a negligence standard, not strict liability. However, if the owner knows or has reason to know that their specific animal has abnormally dangerous propensities beyond those normal for the species, the owner is strictly liable for injuries caused by those known propensities. The common-law "one-bite rule" captures this idea: prior dangerous conduct puts the owner on notice.
*Apply: [D]'s dog is a domestic animal, ordinarily subject to a negligence standard; however, [D] knew the dog had [bitten before / shown aggression toward persons], giving [D] knowledge of its dangerous propensity; therefore, strict liability applies.*
> **Thus,** [D] is strictly liable because of [D]'s knowledge of the animal's dangerous propensity.

### Strict Liability — Abnormally Dangerous Activities 🔴
A defendant who conducts an abnormally dangerous (ultrahazardous) activity is strictly liable for all harm that results from that activity materializing, regardless of the level of care exercised. Courts assess whether an activity qualifies by examining: (1) whether it involves a serious risk of harm that cannot be eliminated even with the utmost care; (2) whether the activity is not commonly engaged in the particular community or area; and (3) the appropriateness of holding the defendant liable given the gravity of the potential harm and the benefit of the activity. Classic examples include blasting, storing explosive materials in quantity, releasing radioactive materials, and crop-dusting.
*Apply: [D]'s activity of [blasting / storing explosives / using toxic chemicals in a residential area] involves a serious risk of harm that cannot be eliminated by any amount of care; it is not commonly conducted in [this community]; the harm that materialized — [explosion / contamination] — was within the risk that made the activity abnormally dangerous.*
> **Thus,** [D] is strictly liable without regard to care taken.

### Strict Liability — Defenses 🟡
Ordinary contributory negligence — where the plaintiff simply failed to recognize the existence of a risk or take precautions against it — is not a defense to strict liability because strict liability imposes responsibility regardless of the care taken by the defendant. However, if the plaintiff knew of the specific danger posed by the abnormally dangerous activity or the animal and unreasonably chose to encounter it, that knowing and voluntary encountering may constitute a defense. Assumption of risk (both express and implied) is a complete defense to strict liability claims: a plaintiff who voluntarily proceeds in the face of a known and appreciated risk cannot recover.
*Apply: [P] [failed to notice the risk / unreasonably ignored warnings]: ordinary inadvertent contributory negligence [is / is not] a defense to strict liability; if [P] instead [knew of the blasting / the dangerous animal] and voluntarily proceeded, assumption of risk [bars / does not bar] recovery.*
> **CA divergence:** Under CA's pure comparative fault regime, even assumption-of-risk arguments in SL cases are analyzed through the comparative-fault lens where the conduct is unreasonable; express assumption of risk remains a complete defense.

> **Thus,** [P]'s conduct [does / does not] provide a defense to strict liability.

## P. Products Liability

### Products Liability — Manufacturing Defect 🔴
A manufacturing defect exists when a specific unit of the product deviated from the manufacturer's own design specifications and proved more dangerous than expected. Liability attaches under both strict liability and negligence when the defect existed when the product left the defendant's control and caused the plaintiff's injury. A manufacturing defect is established by showing the product failed to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect — i.e., it differed from every other unit of the same product rolling off the production line.
*Apply: [D]'s [product] contained a manufacturing defect because [it differed from the intended design — cracked component / missing safety guard]; the defect existed when the product left [D]'s control (normal distribution chain / direct purchase); the defect caused [P]'s injury.*
> **Thus,** [D] is strictly liable for the manufacturing defect.

### Products Liability — Design Defect 🔴
A design defect affects every unit of the product because the entire product line was designed in an unreasonably dangerous manner. Two tests are used: (1) the consumer expectation test — the product failed to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect; and (2) the risk-utility test — a reasonable alternative design existed that would have reduced the risk without substantially impairing the product's utility or making it cost-prohibitive. California applies both tests; plaintiffs typically prefer whichever test supports their case.
*Apply: Every unit of [D]'s [product] shares the same design; an ordinary consumer would not expect [the danger] from normal use; alternatively, a safer alternative design — [describe alternative] — was feasible, would have materially reduced the risk, and would not have substantially impaired the product's utility or price.*
> **Thus,** [D]'s [product] contained a design defect.

### Products Liability — Failure to Warn (Warning Defect) 🟢
A product is defective for failure to warn when it carries risks that are not obvious to ordinary users and the manufacturer or seller failed to provide adequate warnings or instructions to allow safe use. The duty to warn extends to foreseeable users, including foreseeable misusers. A warning that is buried in fine print, incomprehensible, or insufficient in prominence is inadequate. A learned intermediary (e.g., a prescribing physician in pharmaceutical cases) may satisfy the manufacturer's warning duty if the intermediary is expected to convey the risk to the ultimate user.
*Apply: [D]'s [product / drug] carried a non-obvious risk of [harm]; [D] [failed to warn / provided inadequate warning] about that risk; a reasonable warning would have [prompted [P] to avoid the risk / led [P] to use the product differently]; the failure to warn caused [P]'s injury.*
> **Thus,** [D]'s product was defective for failure to warn.

### Products Liability — Strict Liability (Scope of Defendants) 🟡
Strict products liability under the *Restatement (Second) of Torts §402A* applies to all commercial sellers in the chain of distribution — manufacturers, component-part makers, assemblers, wholesalers, and retail sellers. Bystanders and ultimate purchasers are within the protected class of plaintiffs. The plaintiff must show: (1) the product was sold in an unreasonably dangerous defective condition; (2) the defect existed when it left the defendant's control; and (3) the defect caused the plaintiff's physical harm or property damage. Purely economic loss without physical injury is not recoverable under strict tort liability.
*Apply: [D] was a [manufacturer / retailer / wholesaler] — a commercial supplier; [P] [purchased / was a bystander injured by] the product; the product was unreasonably dangerous when it left [D]'s control because [defect]; [P] suffered [physical injury / property damage].*
> **Thus,** [D] is strictly liable as a commercial supplier in the chain of distribution.

### Products Liability — Negligence Theory 🟢
A products liability claim based on negligence requires the same elements as ordinary negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages. Manufacturers and sellers owe a duty to all foreseeable users to design, manufacture, and sell products that are reasonably safe. Breach is shown through negligent conduct — defective design, assembly errors, failure to inspect, or inadequate testing. Unlike strict liability, negligence liability may extend to foreseeable bystanders and requires proof of the defendant's fault, not just the defect.
*Apply: [D] had a duty to design / manufacture / test the product with reasonable care; [D] breached that duty by [specific negligent conduct]; the breach caused [P]'s injury; [P] suffered [physical harm / property damage].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for products liability negligence.

### Products Liability — Implied Warranties (Merchantability & Fitness) 🟡
Two warranties are implied by law in every sale of goods under the UCC. The implied warranty of merchantability requires that goods be of average acceptable quality and fit for the ordinary purpose for which such goods are used; this warranty is breached if the product fails to meet that ordinary-use standard. The implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose arises when the seller knows or has reason to know the buyer's specific intended use and that the buyer is relying on the seller's skill or judgment in selecting the goods; it is breached if the selected goods are unfit for that particular purpose. These warranty theories allow recovery for personal injuries, property damage, and purely economic loss — broader than strict tort liability.
*Apply: [D] sold [product] to [P]; the product [failed to function for ordinary use — merchantability / failed for the specific purpose [D] recommended it for — fitness]; [P] suffered [personal injury / property damage / economic loss] as a result of the breach.*
> **Thus,** [D] breached the implied warranty of [merchantability / fitness for a particular purpose] and is liable for [P]'s damages.

### Products Liability — Express Warranty & Misrepresentation 🟡
An express warranty is created by any affirmation of fact or promise about goods made by the seller that becomes part of the basis of the bargain — it need not use the word "warranty" and need not be supported by separate consideration. Mere sales puffery ("best on the market") does not create an express warranty; a specific factual representation does. A seller is also liable in tort for a misrepresentation of fact concerning a product's material qualities or uses where the statement was intended to induce reliance and the buyer reasonably relied on it to their detriment, regardless of whether the seller knew the statement was false.
*Apply: [D] represented that [product] would [specific factual claim]; the statement was an affirmation of fact (not mere puffery) and became part of the basis of the bargain; [P] reasonably relied on the representation; the product failed to conform to [D]'s representation, causing [P]'s injury.*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for breach of express warranty [and/or misrepresentation].

## Q. Nuisance

### Private Nuisance 🟢
A private nuisance is a substantial and unreasonable interference with a private individual's use and enjoyment of their property. "Substantial" means the interference is not trivial or purely a matter of personal taste but rather something a normal person in the community would find significant. "Unreasonable" is assessed by weighing the gravity of the harm against the social utility of the defendant's conduct, considering factors such as the character of the neighborhood, the duration of the interference, and the feasibility of avoiding it. The plaintiff must have a possessory or immediate right to possess the affected property.
*Apply: [D]'s [noise / odors / vibrations / pollution] substantially interfered with [P]'s use and enjoyment of [P]'s property; the interference was unreasonable because [the harm outweighed the utility / the activity could have been conducted elsewhere / the neighborhood character does not include such activities].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for private nuisance.

### Public Nuisance 🟡
A public nuisance is an unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public — including the health, safety, morals, or comfort of the community at large. A private plaintiff may bring a public nuisance claim only if they suffered a particular harm different in kind from that suffered by the general public. Otherwise, enforcement is through government action. Remedies include injunctive relief, damages, and self-help abatement (where applicable).
*Apply: [D]'s [pollution / obstruction of public road / spread of disease risk] interfered with the public's right to [safe air / use of a public way / community health]; [P], as a private plaintiff, suffered [particular damage different in kind from the public's injury] by [specific harm].*
> **Thus,** [D] is liable for public nuisance and [P] has standing because [P] suffered particular harm distinct from the public's.*

## R. Vicarious Liability

### Respondeat Superior (Employer-Employee) 🔴
An employer is vicariously liable for the tortious acts of an employee committed within the scope of employment. Scope of employment encompasses acts the employee was hired to perform, acts occurring substantially within authorized time and space limits, and acts motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. A minor deviation from the work route (a detour) remains within scope; a substantial departure for the employee's own personal purposes (a frolic) falls outside scope and breaks the employer's vicarious liability. Intentional torts are generally outside scope unless the nature of the employment involves a risk that the employee would commit that type of intentional tort (e.g., a bouncer using force).
*Apply: [D-employee] committed a [tort] while [driving the delivery route / performing job duties]; this was within the scope of employment because [timing, place, purpose to serve employer]; [if frolic — D-employee had departed entirely for personal reasons, placing the act outside scope].*
> **Thus,** [D-employer] is [vicariously liable / not vicariously liable] under respondeat superior.

### Vicarious Liability — Independent Contractors 🟡
A hiring party is generally not vicariously liable for the tortious acts of an independent contractor because the hiring party lacks the right to control the details of how the contractor performs the work. However, two major exceptions apply: (1) the independent contractor is engaged in an inherently dangerous activity (blasting, working with toxic substances), in which case the duty is non-delegable; and (2) the duty is non-delegable as a matter of public policy (common carrier duty of safe transportation, duty to maintain a safe premises for business visitors). In those cases, the hiring party remains liable despite delegating performance to an independent contractor.
*Apply: [D] hired [contractor] to perform [task]; [contractor] would ordinarily insulate [D] from liability; however, [the task was inherently dangerous / the duty was non-delegable by public policy], so [D] cannot escape liability by delegation.*
> **Thus,** [D] is [not / vicariously] liable for [contractor]'s tort because [general rule / exception applies].

### Vicarious Liability — Owner-Driver & Family Purpose Doctrine 🟡
A vehicle owner is generally not vicariously liable for the negligence of another person driving the vehicle merely because the owner permitted that person to use it. However, most states impose vicarious liability on an owner when they have consented to the driver's use — the owner's negligence in entrusting the vehicle may provide an independent theory of liability. The family purpose doctrine, recognized in many jurisdictions, holds that the head of a household who provides a vehicle for the family's general use is vicariously liable for the tortious operation of that vehicle by any family member using it for a family purpose. Parents are not automatically vicariously liable for the torts of their minor children solely because of the parental relationship, but may be independently liable for negligent supervision or entrustment.
*Apply: [D] [owned the car / provided the family vehicle]; [driver] was [a family member using the car for a family errand / a permittee]; the family purpose doctrine [applies if D maintained the car for family use]; [alternatively, analyze D's independent negligence in entrusting the vehicle to an incompetent driver].*
> **Thus,** [D] [is / is not] vicariously liable under the [family purpose doctrine / owner-consent statute / negligent entrustment theory].

### Joint and Several Liability & Contribution 🟡
Where two or more defendants' independent negligent acts combine to proximately cause a single indivisible injury to the plaintiff, each defendant is jointly and severally liable — the plaintiff may collect the entire judgment from any one defendant. California has modified joint and several liability by statute: defendants are jointly and severally liable for economic damages but severally liable only (proportionate to fault) for non-economic damages. Among defendants, contribution allows a defendant who has paid more than their fair share to seek reimbursement from co-defendants based on proportionate fault; indemnity allows complete shifting of the loss from a less culpable to a more culpable party.
*Apply: [D1]'s and [D2]'s independent negligence combined to cause [P]'s indivisible injury; [P] may collect the full judgment from either; as between the defendants, fault is apportioned [D1: X%, D2: Y%] for contribution purposes.*
> **CA divergence:** CA limits joint and several liability — defendants bear joint/several liability for economic damages but only proportionate (several) liability for non-economic damages.

> **Thus,** [D1] and [D2] are jointly and severally liable for [P]'s economic damages; for non-economic damages, each pays their proportionate share.

### Survival of Tort Actions 🟡
Survival statutes allow a tort cause of action that accrued during the victim's lifetime to continue after the victim's death, brought by the personal representative of the estate. The estate steps into the decedent's shoes and may recover all damages the decedent could have recovered had they lived — including pre-death pain and suffering, medical expenses, and lost earnings up to the moment of death. Survival actions apply to property torts and personal injury torts; they generally do not extend to purely intangible personal interests such as defamation (though some statutes vary). The action is separate from — and cumulative with — a wrongful death action.
*Apply: [Decedent] suffered [injury] before dying; [D]'s tortious conduct occurred before [Decedent]'s death; the estate's survival claim covers [pre-death pain and suffering / medical bills / lost wages from injury to death]; the claim is brought by [personal representative] on behalf of the estate.*
> **Thus,** the estate may maintain [Decedent]'s survival claim and recover pre-death damages against [D].

### Wrongful Death 🟢
Wrongful death statutes create a new cause of action for the pecuniary losses suffered by surviving family members — typically the spouse and next of kin — when the defendant's tortious conduct causes the decedent's death. Recovery is limited to the economic value of what the decedent would have provided: lost future earnings (minus personal consumption), loss of services, and loss of support. The action is derivative — it is available only to the extent the decedent could have recovered in a personal injury action had they survived. In most jurisdictions, including California, damages for grief and emotional distress of survivors are not recoverable (economic damages only under the traditional rule), though some jurisdictions permit loss of companionship.
*Apply: [D]'s negligence [caused the death] of [Decedent]; [surviving spouse / children] lost [D]'s financial support of $[X]/year, domestic services valued at $[Y], and future earning capacity; the wrongful death action is subject to any defenses [D] could have asserted against [Decedent].*
> **CA divergence:** CA's wrongful death statute (CCP §377.60) allows recovery for lost financial support and services; heirs may not recover purely for grief; the survival action is brought separately by the estate.

> **Thus,** [surviving family] may recover [economic losses] through a wrongful death action against [D].

### Loss of Consortium 🟡
Loss of consortium is a derivative claim brought by the spouse (or, in many jurisdictions, a registered domestic partner or parent/child) of a person seriously injured by the defendant's tortious conduct. The claimant seeks compensation for three recognized losses: (1) loss of services (household assistance, companionship tasks); (2) loss of society (companionship, affection, and moral support); and (3) loss of the marital relationship (including sexual relations). Because the claim is derivative, any defense available against the directly injured spouse — including comparative fault — reduces or bars the consortium claimant's recovery proportionally.
*Apply: [D]'s negligence caused serious injury to [Spouse]; [Claimant-Spouse] suffered loss of [companionship / services / sexual relations]; the claim is derivative — if [Spouse]'s own comparative fault was [X]%, [Claimant]'s consortium damages are also reduced by [X]%.*
> **Thus,** [Claimant] may recover for loss of consortium, subject to any reduction for [Spouse]'s comparative fault.

## S. Contributory Negligence & Last Clear Chance (Traditional Rules)

### Contributory Negligence (Traditional Rule) 🟡
Under the traditional common-law contributory negligence rule, any negligence on the plaintiff's part that contributes to their own injury completely bars the plaintiff's recovery — even if the plaintiff was only 1% at fault and the defendant 99%. Contributory negligence is not a defense to intentional torts or to a defendant's wanton and willful misconduct. This all-or-nothing rule has been replaced by comparative fault in the vast majority of states (including California), but it remains tested on the multistate portion and in jurisdictions that have not adopted comparative fault.
*Apply: [P]'s own negligent conduct of [failing to look / jaywalking / ignoring a warning] contributed to [P]'s injury; under the traditional contributory negligence rule, this bars [P]'s recovery entirely; [note: CA does NOT apply this rule — CA uses pure comparative fault].*
> **CA divergence:** CA abolished contributory negligence in *Li v. Yellow Cab* (1975) and replaced it with pure comparative fault; contributory negligence applies on MBE questions unless the question specifies comparative fault.

> **Thus,** under traditional contributory negligence, [P] is completely barred from recovery.

### Last Clear Chance 🟡
The last clear chance doctrine operates as a plaintiff's rebuttal to a contributory negligence defense: even if the plaintiff was contributorily negligent, the plaintiff may recover in full if the defendant had the last clear chance to avoid the harm and failed to take it. The doctrine requires that the defendant, after discovering (or after they should have discovered) the plaintiff's peril, had the actual ability to avoid the harm and negligently failed to do so. Last clear chance effectively nullifies the contributory negligence bar where the defendant's failure to act was temporally closest to the injury.
*Apply: [P] was contributorily negligent by [placing themselves in peril]; [D] then [discovered / should have discovered] [P]'s helpless peril; [D] had sufficient time and ability to avoid the harm by [braking / steering]; [D]'s failure to act was the last proximate cause of the injury.*
> **Thus,** [P] may invoke last clear chance to recover despite [P]'s own contributory negligence.

### Duty — Common Carriers & Innkeepers (Highest Duty) 🟢
Common carriers (airlines, railroads, buses, taxis while transporting passengers) and innkeepers owe their passengers or guests the highest degree of care consistent with the practical operation of their business — a standard above that of the ordinary reasonable person. This heightened duty reflects the carrier's or innkeeper's superior ability to protect persons who have entrusted themselves to their care and who have limited ability to protect themselves once aboard or in residence. Any slight departure from the utmost care may constitute a breach. The duty extends to safe ingress and egress as well as during transit or lodging.
*Apply: [D] was a [common carrier / innkeeper]; [P] was a [passenger / guest]; [D]'s conduct of [failing to maintain safe conditions / failing to assist] fell below the highest degree of care required; even a slight deviation from the utmost care constitutes breach.*
> **Thus,** [D], as a [common carrier / innkeeper], breached the heightened duty of care owed to [P].
