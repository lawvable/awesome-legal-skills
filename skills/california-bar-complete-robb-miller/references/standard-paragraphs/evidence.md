## A. Form of Questions and Witness Examination

### Improper Question Forms — Leading Questions 🟢
A leading question is one that suggests its own answer, and it is generally prohibited on direct examination. However, leading questions are permissible on cross-examination, on preliminary or introductory matters, when a witness is having difficulty remembering, and when examining a hostile or adverse witness.
*Apply: [counsel] asked [witness] a leading question during [direct/cross] examination by suggesting [answer]; this is [permissible/impermissible] because [basis].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** No material split; CEC § 767 mirrors FRE 611(c).

### Other Improper Question Forms 🟢
Beyond leading questions, courts exclude questions that are: (1) misleading or argumentative; (2) compound (embedding two questions in one); (3) assumes facts not yet in evidence; (4) conclusory or calls for speculation; (5) cumulative; or (6) unduly harassing or embarrassing to the witness.
*Apply: [counsel's] question was objectionable because it [assumed the fact that / was compound in that it asked both X and Y].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 760–795 codify these grounds; no substantive split.

### Present Recollection Revived (Refreshing Recollection) 🟢
Any writing, object, or thing may be used to refresh a witness's present memory. The procedure requires: (1) the witness first acknowledges a memory failure; (2) the witness reviews the writing but does not read from it while testifying; (3) the witness then testifies from refreshed memory; and (4) opposing counsel must be shown the writing and may introduce it into evidence. The writing itself is not admitted into evidence.
*Apply: [counsel] showed [witness] [document] after [witness] could not recall [fact]; [witness] must testify from refreshed memory, not read the document aloud.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 771 tracks FRE 612; no material split.

### Past Recollection Recorded 🟢
Where a witness cannot recall a matter even after attempting to refresh memory, a writing may be admitted as past recollection recorded if: (1) the witness had personal knowledge of the facts; (2) the writing was made or adopted by the witness; (3) it was made while the matter was fresh in the witness's memory; (4) the writing accurately reflects that knowledge; and (5) the witness can no longer recall the facts. Under FRE, the document is read into evidence but not admitted as an exhibit unless offered by the adverse party.
*Apply: [witness] cannot recall [fact] even after reviewing [document]; if [document] meets all five foundational requirements, it may be [read to the jury] as past recollection recorded.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1237 is substantively consistent with FRE 803(5).

## B. Relevance

### Logical Relevance 🟢
Evidence is logically relevant if it has any tendency to make a material fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence. The tendency need not be strong — the standard is minimal probative value. Evidence is irrelevant if it concerns a different time, place, person, or event unless a recognized exception applies.
*Apply: [evidence] is logically relevant because it makes [material fact] [more/less] probable by [reason].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 210 requires the fact of consequence also be "in dispute" — a marginally higher threshold than FRE 401, which only requires the fact be "of consequence."

### Legal Relevance — Balancing Test 🔴
Even logically relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, waste of time, or presentation of cumulative evidence. Notably, "unfair surprise" is not a listed ground for exclusion under either code.
*Apply: [evidence] is relevant, but [risk of unfair prejudice] substantially outweighs its probative value on [issue] because [reason].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 352 mirrors FRE 403's balancing standard; however, in criminal cases, Proposition 8 (CA Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) makes all relevant evidence admissible, subject only to § 352 as one of its surviving limits. See Proposition 8 block below.

### Habit and Business Routine 🟢
Evidence of a person's habit — a regular practice of responding to a specific situation in a particular way, characterized by specificity and regularity — is admissible to prove conduct in conformity with that habit on a particular occasion. An organization's routine business practice is admissible on the same basis to infer that the organization followed its usual routine at the relevant time.
*Apply: evidence that [person/organization] [specific repeated conduct] is habit/routine evidence admissible to show [person/organization] [acted consistently] on [date].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 1105–1106 are consistent with FRE 406; no material split.

### Industrial or Trade Custom 🟢
Evidence of an industry or trade custom is admissible as non-conclusive evidence of the applicable standard of care in negligence cases. It shows what a reasonable person in that industry would do, but it does not by itself establish the legal standard.
*Apply: evidence that [industry] customarily [practice] is admissible as non-conclusive evidence of the standard of care [defendant] owed.*

### Prior Accidents, Claims, and Comparable Sales 🟡
A plaintiff's prior accidents or claims are generally inadmissible, except to show a common plan of fraud or where they bear on the plaintiff's damages. Evidence of other accidents involving the same instrumentality under the same or similar circumstances is admissible to show notice or knowledge of the danger. Sales prices of comparable property (same kind, time, and location) are admissible to establish fair market value.
*Apply: [prior accidents/comparable sales] involving [same instrumentality/similar property] are admissible to show [notice/value] because [circumstances are sufficiently similar].*

## C. Policy Exclusions

### Subsequent Remedial Measures 🔴
Evidence that a party took remedial measures after an injury or harm is inadmissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning. The rule may not be used as a shield against admissibility when the evidence is offered to prove ownership, control, or the feasibility of precautionary measures where the party contends feasibility is in dispute, or to impeach on those points.
*Apply: [defendant's] post-accident [repair/design change] is inadmissible to prove [negligence/defect], but [is/is not] admissible to show [ownership/feasibility] because [defendant placed that issue in dispute].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1151 generally mirrors FRE 407. However, in CA product liability cases, evidence of subsequent design changes is admissible to prove defective design — a significant split from the federal rule, which excludes such evidence even in design-defect cases.

### Settlements and Offers to Compromise 🟢
Evidence of an offer to settle, an actual settlement, or conduct and statements made during compromise negotiations is inadmissible to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim. The rule requires that a claim be asserted and disputed as to liability or amount. Unilateral offers to pay medical expenses are also inadmissible, but any admission of fact accompanying such an offer — unlike the offer itself — is admissible under FRE.
*Apply: [party's] offer to pay $[X] in settlement of [claim] is inadmissible to show liability, but [admission of fact, if any,] [is/is not] admissible depending on [FRE or CEC].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1152 tracks FRE 408 on settlement offers. CEC § 1152(a) bars statements made during mediation as well — a broader exclusion than FRE. Critically, under CEC § 1160, statements related to an offer to pay medical expenses are also inadmissible (the opposite of the FRE, which makes accompanying admissions of fact admissible).

### Liability Insurance 🟢
Evidence that a person carried or did not carry liability insurance is inadmissible to prove the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully, or to show the party's ability to pay. Such evidence is admissible if offered for another purpose, such as to prove ownership or control of property when that is disputed, or to establish a witness's bias or interest.
*Apply: evidence that [defendant] carried [insurance] is inadmissible to show [negligence/ability to pay], but [is/is not] admissible to show [ownership/bias] because [that issue is disputed / witness is employed by insurer].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1155 mirrors FRE 411; no material split.

### Sympathy Statements (CA Only) 🔴
In California civil actions, an expression of sympathy, condolence, or benevolence regarding the suffering, injury, or death of an individual is inadmissible to prove liability. However, any accompanying statement of fault or admission of liability made in the same communication remains admissible.
*Apply: [defendant's] statement "I'm so sorry this happened to you" is inadmissible as an expression of sympathy, but [defendant's] accompanying statement that [he was driving too fast] [is/is not] admissible as [an admission of fault].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1160 creates this distinction; FRE has no equivalent rule.

## D. Character Evidence

### Character Evidence in Civil Cases 🟢
In civil cases, evidence of a person's character trait is generally inadmissible when offered to prove that the person acted in conformity with that trait on a particular occasion. Character evidence is admissible in civil cases only when character itself is a material element of a claim or defense — such as in defamation, negligent hiring, or fraud/deceit actions. When character is a material element, it may be proven by specific acts, opinion testimony, or reputation testimony.
*Apply: evidence of [party's] [character trait] is [inadmissible/admissible] in this civil case because character [is not/is] a material element of the [claim/defense].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1101 generally prohibits character-propensity evidence; consistent with FRE 404(a).

### Character Evidence — Criminal Defendant Opens the Door 🟢
In criminal cases, the prosecution may not offer evidence of the defendant's bad character to prove propensity to commit the charged offense. The defendant may, however, introduce evidence of a character trait relevant to the offense (e.g., peacefulness in an assault case) through reputation or opinion testimony. Once the defendant opens the door, the prosecutor may cross-examine the defendant's character witness about whether the witness has heard of or knows about specific instances of the defendant's conduct, and may call rebuttal witnesses to testify to the defendant's bad reputation or their negative opinion of the defendant. The prosecutor must accept the answer given on cross-examination and may not introduce extrinsic evidence of the specific misconduct.
*Apply: defendant called [witness] to testify to [defendant's] [character trait]; the prosecution may [cross-examine / call rebuttal witnesses] because defendant opened the door.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1102 mirrors FRE 404(a)(2)(A); consistent structure.

### Victim Character — Self-Defense 🟢
In homicide or assault cases where the defendant claims self-defense, the defendant may offer reputation or opinion evidence of the victim's violent character to support the inference that the victim was the first aggressor. The prosecutor may rebut with reputation or opinion evidence of the victim's peaceful character, and may also offer evidence of the defendant's violent character. Additionally, if the defendant was aware of specific prior violent acts of the victim before the incident, those acts are admissible to show the defendant's reasonable fear.
*Apply: [defendant] claims self-defense; evidence of [victim's] reputation for violence is admissible, and [defendant's] knowledge of [victim's prior act] is admissible to show [defendant] had reason to fear.*

### Rape Shield — Victim's Sexual History 🟢
In criminal sexual misconduct cases, evidence of a victim's prior sexual behavior or sexual predisposition is generally inadmissible. It may be admitted only in narrow circumstances: (1) to show that someone other than the defendant was the source of physical evidence (e.g., semen or injury); or (2) to show prior consensual sexual activity between the victim and the defendant specifically, where excluding it would violate the defendant's constitutional rights. In civil cases, evidence of a victim's sexual behavior or predisposition is admissible only if its probative value substantially outweighs the danger of harm to the victim and unfair prejudice to any party, and the defendant must provide notice and request an in camera hearing.
*Apply: [defendant's] evidence of [victim's] prior sexual history is [admissible/inadmissible] because [it does/does not fall within the narrow exceptions for another source of semen / prior conduct with defendant].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 782–783 track FRE 412; no material split.

### MIMIC — Prior Acts for Non-Character Purpose 🔴
Evidence of a defendant's prior crimes, wrongs, or other acts is inadmissible to prove propensity, but may be admissible for other non-character purposes, including: (1) Motive; (2) Intent or guilty knowledge; (3) Absence of Mistake or accident; (4) Identity (modus operandi or connection through physical evidence); and (5) Common plan or scheme. The evidence is also admissible to show opportunity or knowledge. All MIMIC evidence is subject to the court's balancing test — exclusion if probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice.
*Apply: evidence of [defendant's] prior [act/crime] is offered not for propensity but to prove [motive/intent/absence of mistake/identity/common scheme] because [specific reason connecting prior act to charged conduct].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1101(b) tracks FRE 404(b)(2); no material split. Note also FRE 413–415 and CEC §§ 1108–1109 for sexual assault/domestic violence, below.

### Character in Sexual Assault and Domestic Violence Cases 🔴
Federal Rules 413–415 and CA CEC §§ 1108–1109 create a major exception to the general bar on propensity evidence: in criminal cases involving sexual assault or child molestation, evidence of the defendant's prior similar acts is admissible to prove propensity to commit such acts, regardless of whether the prior acts resulted in conviction and regardless of whether the defendant has opened any door. Under CEC § 1109, a similar rule applies to domestic violence cases. This evidence remains subject to the § 352 / FRE 403 balancing test.
*Apply: evidence of [defendant's] prior [sexual assault/domestic violence] is admissible under [FRE 413/CEC § 1108] to prove propensity because [this is a sexual assault/domestic violence case] and probative value [does/does not] substantially outweigh prejudice.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1109 extends the propensity exception to domestic violence and elder/dependent adult abuse — FRE has no domestic violence analog. Both codes require § 352/FRE 403 balancing.

### Proposition 8 — California's Truth-in-Evidence Rule (Criminal Cases) 🔴
Under California Constitution Article I, § 28(f)(2) (Proposition 8), all relevant evidence is admissible in a criminal case, even if it would otherwise be excluded by the California Evidence Code. The following objections survive Proposition 8 and remain viable in criminal cases: (1) exclusionary rules required by the U.S. Constitution; (2) the hearsay rule; (3) privileges; (4) statutory limits on character evidence; and (5) the secondary evidence (best evidence) rule. Proposition 8 also permits impeachment of any witness by prior felony conviction involving moral turpitude, even if the CEC would otherwise bar it.
*Apply: [evidence] is arguably inadmissible under the CEC, but in this criminal case, Prop. 8 overrides that bar unless [hearsay / privilege / constitutional exclusion / character limit / best evidence] applies.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** This is a California-only rule with no FRE analog. It is the single most important CA-specific doctrine in criminal evidence essays.

## E. Authentication and the Best Evidence Rule

### Authentication 🟢
Before a writing or document may be admitted into evidence, it must be authenticated — the proponent must introduce evidence sufficient for a reasonable jury to find that the document is what the proponent claims it to be. Authentication may be established through: (1) direct evidence, such as an admission by a party, eyewitness testimony, lay or expert handwriting comparison, or the proponent's familiarity with the handwriting; or (2) circumstantial evidence, such as the ancient documents rule (document 20+ years old, appearing aged on its face, found in a place of natural custody), or the solicited reply rule (the document arrived in response to a prior communication addressed to the purported author).
*Apply: [proponent] must lay a foundation showing [document] is what [proponent] claims; [authentication method] is sufficient/insufficient because [reason].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 1400–1454 are broadly consistent with FRE 901–902; CEC ancient document rule uses 30 years, not 20 years.

### Self-Authenticating Documents 🟢
Certain categories of documents authenticate themselves and require no extrinsic evidence of genuineness: certified copies of public records, official government publications, newspapers and periodicals, trade inscriptions and labels, notarized (acknowledged) documents, and certain instruments bearing commercial paper signatures under the UCC.
*Apply: [document] is [a certified copy of a public record / trade label / notarized instrument] and is therefore self-authenticating under [FRE 902/CEC § 1452].*

### Photographs and Recordings 🟢
A photograph, video, or recording is authenticated when any witness with knowledge testifies that it is a fair and accurate representation of the scene, person, or event it purports to depict. The authenticating witness need not be the photographer.
*Apply: [witness] may authenticate [photograph] by testifying that it accurately depicts [scene/person] as it appeared at the relevant time.*

### Best Evidence Rule (Original Document Rule) 🟡
When a party seeks to prove the content of a writing, recording, photograph, or film, the original must be produced or its absence explained. The rule applies to: (1) legally operative or dispositive documents (contracts, deeds, wills) where the document itself creates the legal relationship; and (2) situations where a witness's only knowledge of a fact comes from having read a document. The rule does not apply to facts that exist independently of any writing, or to collateral (unimportant) documents. Duplicates are admissible to the same extent as originals unless there is a genuine dispute about the original's authenticity or it would be unfair to admit the duplicate. Exceptions include public records, voluminous records (with originals made available), and situations where the original has been lost, destroyed (without bad faith), or is otherwise unobtainable.
*Apply: [party] seeks to prove the contents of [document]; the [original/duplicate/secondary evidence] [is/is not] admissible because [the rule does/does not apply / an exception exists].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CA calls this the "Secondary Evidence Rule" (CEC §§ 1520–1523), which is broader than FRE 1002–1004 in that secondary evidence is generally admissible unless the opponent shows it would be unfair or the original's content is genuinely disputed. The Prop. 8 list of surviving objections includes the Secondary Evidence Rule, so it can exclude evidence in criminal cases notwithstanding Prop. 8.

## F. Witnesses — Competency and Opinion

### Witness Competency 🟢
Every person is presumed competent to testify. A witness must: (1) have personal knowledge of the matters about which they testify; (2) testify from present recollection; (3) be capable of communicating; and (4) take an oath or affirmation to tell the truth.
*Apply: [witness] is [competent/incompetent] to testify about [matter] because [she does/does not have personal knowledge / can/cannot communicate / did/did not take an oath].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 700 adds that the witness must understand the legal duty to tell the truth — a slightly heightened requirement compared to FRE 601.

### Lay Opinion 🟡
A lay witness may offer opinion testimony only if the opinion is: (1) rationally based on the witness's own perception; (2) helpful to a clear understanding of the testimony or to determination of a fact in issue; and (3) not based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge.
*Apply: [witness's] opinion that [X] is [admissible/inadmissible] as lay opinion because it [is/is not] rationally based on personal perception and [does/does not] require specialized knowledge.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under CEC § 800, lay opinion may be based on the witness's special knowledge, skill, experience, or training, subject to court discretion — a broader standard than FRE 701's exclusion of opinions derived from specialized knowledge.

### Expert Opinion 🔴
Expert opinion testimony is admissible if: (1) the subject matter is one where expert knowledge would assist the trier of fact; (2) the witness is qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education; (3) the expert holds the opinion to a reasonable degree of [professional] certainty; and (4) the opinion is supported by an adequate factual basis — which may include personal knowledge, facts hypothesized at trial, or facts reasonably relied upon by experts in the field. An expert may give an opinion on the ultimate issue, except that in federal criminal cases an expert may not state an opinion on whether the defendant had the mental state required for the offense.
*Apply: [expert's] opinion on [issue] is [admissible/inadmissible] because [expert is/is not qualified, opinion is/is not based on sufficient facts, and the methodology is/is not reliable/generally accepted].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** This is a major split. Federal courts apply the Daubert standard (FRE 702, as interpreted in *Daubert v. Merrell Dow*): the trial court acts as gatekeeper and assesses reliability by factors such as testing, peer review, error rates, and general acceptance. California courts apply the Kelly/Frye standard (CEC § 801; *People v. Kelly*): novel scientific evidence must be based on methods "generally accepted" within the relevant scientific community. Daubert's flexibility does not apply in CA state court.

### Learned Treatises 🟢
A learned treatise may be admitted as substantive evidence (an exception to the hearsay rule) if: (1) it is relied upon by an expert witness during direct or cross-examination; and (2) it is established as a reliable authority by the expert, by another expert, or by judicial notice. Under FRE, the treatise may be read into evidence but is not admitted as an exhibit.
*Apply: [expert] relied on [treatise] in forming her opinion; if [treatise] is established as a reliable authority, relevant passages may be read to the jury as substantive evidence.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CA has no equivalent learned treatise hearsay exception; treatises may be used only for impeachment of an expert, not as substantive evidence.

## G. Judicial Notice and Presumptions

### Judicial Notice 🟡
A court may take judicial notice of adjudicative facts that are either (1) generally known within the court's territorial jurisdiction, or (2) capable of accurate and ready determination by reference to sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned (e.g., almanac data, official government publications, undisputed scientific facts). Under FRE 201, judicial notice is discretionary unless a party requests it and supplies the necessary information, in which case it is mandatory. In a federal civil case, the jury must accept judicially noticed facts as conclusive. In a federal criminal case, the jury may, but is not required to, accept the noticed fact.
*Apply: the court [should/should not] take judicial notice of [fact] because it [is/is not] generally known or capable of ready and accurate determination.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under CEC §§ 451–452, a court must take judicial notice of facts within CEC § 451 (e.g., state law, court rules, official acts) and has discretion under § 452. Critically, in both civil and criminal cases in California, the jury must accept judicially noticed facts as conclusive — unlike federal criminal cases where the jury retains discretion.

### Presumptions 🟡
A presumption is a rule of law that directs a finding of one fact (the presumed fact) upon proof of another fact (the basic fact). Under FRE, most presumptions in civil cases operate as "bursting bubbles" (the Thayer-Wigmore model): the presumption shifts the burden of production to the opposing party, and once the opposing party produces credible contrary evidence, the presumption vanishes. Under California law, some presumptions also shift the burden of persuasion and do not merely burst. In criminal cases, mandatory presumptions that shift the burden of proof to the defendant on an element of the offense are unconstitutional.
*Apply: proof of [basic fact] triggers a presumption of [presumed fact], shifting the burden of [production/persuasion] to [opposing party] to produce evidence that [presumed fact] is not true.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 600–668 classify presumptions as either "conclusive" (irrebuttable) or "rebuttable," with rebuttable presumptions further divided into those affecting only the burden of producing evidence and those affecting the burden of proof — a more nuanced scheme than FRE 301–302.

### Burden of Proof 🟢
The burden of producing evidence (burden of going forward) rests on the party who has the burden of pleading the issue. The burden of persuasion requires the party bearing it to convince the trier of fact to a specified standard: preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not) in most civil cases; clear and convincing evidence in cases involving fraud, punitive damages, or certain civil status determinations; and beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases.
*Apply: [party] bears the burden of [production/persuasion] on [issue]; [party] must establish [issue] by [preponderance / clear and convincing / beyond a reasonable doubt].*

## H. Impeachment and Rehabilitation

### Prior Inconsistent Statements — Impeachment 🔴
A witness may be impeached by showing that on a prior occasion the witness made a statement inconsistent with the witness's trial testimony. Under FRE, the prior inconsistent statement is admissible only for impeachment (not as substantive evidence) unless the statement was made under oath at a formal proceeding, in which case it is also admissible for its truth as a non-hearsay statement (FRE 801(d)(1)(A)).
*Apply: [witness] testified at trial that [X]; [prior statement] in which [witness] said [Y] may be used to impeach, and [is/is not] admissible for its truth because it was [/was not] made under oath at a formal proceeding.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under CEC § 1235, any prior inconsistent statement of a witness is admissible for its truth, regardless of whether it was made under oath or at a formal proceeding — a significantly broader rule than FRE 801(d)(1)(A).

### Bias, Interest, and Motive — Impeachment 🟢
A witness may always be impeached by showing that the witness has a bias, interest in the outcome of the litigation, or motive to lie or exaggerate. This may be shown through cross-examination or extrinsic evidence. No foundation requirement bars extrinsic evidence, though courts retain discretion to limit cumulative impeachment.
*Apply: evidence that [witness] [is employed by / is related to / has financial interest in] [party] is admissible to show the witness has a [bias/motive/interest] to testify favorably.*

### Prior Conviction — Impeachment (Federal) 🔴
Under FRE 609, a prior criminal conviction may be used to impeach any witness. Two distinct rules apply: (1) for crimes involving dishonesty or false statement (crimen falsi), the conviction must be admitted — the judge has no discretion to exclude it; (2) for other felonies (punishable by imprisonment exceeding one year), the judge has discretion to admit the conviction, weighing probative value against prejudicial effect. When the witness being impeached is a criminal defendant, the prosecution bears the burden of showing that probative value outweighs prejudice. A conviction more than ten years old is generally excluded unless the court determines that its probative value substantially outweighs prejudice.
*Apply: [witness/defendant's] prior conviction for [crime] [must be admitted as a crimen falsi crime / is subject to discretionary balancing under FRE 609(a)(1)] and [is/is not] too remote.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** See next block.

### Prior Conviction — Impeachment (California / Prop. 8) 🔴
Under California law, the general rule is that a witness (other than the criminal defendant) may be impeached by a prior felony conviction. In criminal cases, Prop. 8 permits impeachment by any prior felony conviction (involving moral turpitude) without the limitations that the CEC might otherwise impose; the trial court retains § 352 discretion to exclude if probative value is outweighed by prejudice. Misdemeanor convictions are generally not usable for impeachment under the CEC, but the court may inquire into the underlying conduct if it involves moral turpitude.
*Apply: in this criminal case, [witness's] prior [felony/misdemeanor] conviction for [crime] [is/is not] admissible under Prop. 8 to impeach because [it does/does not involve moral turpitude] and § 352 balancing [favors/disfavors] admission.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under Prop. 8, prior felony convictions (moral turpitude) are broadly admissible to impeach in criminal cases without the FRE 609 crimen falsi/felony distinction; the § 352 balancing test replaces FRE 609's balancing analysis.

### Bad Reputation/Opinion — Impeachment 🟢
A witness may be impeached through the testimony of another witness who (1) is familiar with the first witness's reputation in the community for truthfulness or untruthfulness, or (2) gives a personal opinion about the first witness's character for truthfulness. This testimony is admissible for its truth.
*Apply: [second witness] may testify that [first witness] has a [reputation for dishonesty / in [second witness's] opinion is not truthful] to impeach [first witness's] trial testimony.*

### Specific Instances of Misconduct — Impeachment 🔴
Under FRE 608(b), a witness may be cross-examined about specific prior acts of misconduct that are probative of untruthfulness, even if those acts did not result in a conviction. The cross-examiner must have a good-faith basis for the question. The examiner must accept the witness's answer and may not introduce extrinsic evidence of the prior misconduct.
*Apply: [counsel] may ask [witness] about [prior act of dishonesty] on cross-examination in good faith, but may not introduce extrinsic evidence if [witness] denies it.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under CEC § 787, specific instances of misconduct are generally inadmissible to impeach. However, in criminal cases, Prop. 8 may render such acts admissible if they constitute moral turpitude, and they may then be proven by any means (including extrinsic evidence) — a major split from FRE 608(b)'s bar on extrinsic evidence.

### Bolstering 🟢
A party may not bolster its own witness's credibility before any attack has been made on that witness. Introducing prior consistent statements or character evidence for truthfulness is premature until the opposing party has impeached the witness.
*Apply: [counsel's] attempt to introduce [prior consistent statement / character testimony] before [witness] has been impeached is improper bolstering.*

### Rehabilitation After Impeachment 🟢
Once a witness has been impeached, the proponent may rehabilitate by: (1) on redirect, allowing the witness to explain or clarify facts raised on cross-examination; (2) calling other witnesses to testify to the impeached witness's good reputation for truthfulness; or (3) introducing a prior consistent statement to rebut a charge that the witness fabricated testimony or has an improper motive, provided the consistent statement was made before the alleged motive arose. A prior consistent statement used for rehabilitation is also admissible for its truth.
*Apply: after [opposing counsel] impeached [witness] with [prior inconsistent statement / bias], [counsel] may rehabilitate by [redirect explanation / reputation evidence / prior consistent statement predating the alleged motive].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 791 permits prior consistent statements for rehabilitation; consistent with FRE 801(d)(1)(B).

## I. Hearsay — Definition and Exclusions

### Hearsay Definition 🟢
Hearsay is an out-of-court statement — oral, written, or assertive conduct — offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted. All three elements must be present: the statement was made out of court, it is being introduced into court, and it is offered for its truth. If the statement is offered for any purpose other than its truth, it is not hearsay and is admissible (subject to other evidentiary rules).
*Apply: [statement] is [/is not] hearsay because it [is/is not] being offered to prove [the truth of / something other than] [the matter asserted].*

### Non-Hearsay Uses — Verbal Acts and Effect on Listener 🟢
A statement is not hearsay when offered not for its truth but: (1) as a verbal act or legally operative fact (e.g., words constituting a contract, defamatory statement, or bribe); (2) to show its effect on the hearer or reader (e.g., to establish notice, knowledge, or state of mind of the recipient); or (3) as circumstantial evidence of the declarant's state of mind (e.g., statements reflecting delusional thinking as evidence of insanity).
*Apply: [statement] is offered not for its truth but to show [it created the contract / recipient had notice / defendant's mental state]; it is therefore not hearsay.*

### Non-Human Assertions — Not Hearsay 🟢
The hearsay rule applies only to human statements. Output generated by a machine or animal — such as the reading displayed by a radar gun, the alert of a drug-detection dog, a breathalyzer printout, or a computer-generated log — is not a "statement" within the hearsay definition and is therefore not hearsay. The proponent must authenticate the device and establish that it was functioning properly, but no hearsay exception is needed.
*Apply: the [radar reading / dog alert / computer-generated record] is not hearsay because it is not a human assertion; [party] must instead authenticate the [device/animal] and show it was [calibrated/certified/reliable] at the time of the [reading/alert].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** No material split; both the FRE and CEC define hearsay as a "statement" made by a person, so machine and animal output falls outside the rule under both codes.

### Non-Hearsay — Prior Statements by Witnesses (FRE) 🔴
Under FRE 801(d)(1), three categories of prior witness statements are classified as non-hearsay (and thus admissible for their truth): (1) prior inconsistent statements made under oath at a formal proceeding; (2) prior consistent statements offered to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive, where the statement predates the alleged motive; and (3) prior statements of identification made after perceiving a person.
*Apply: [witness's] prior statement [identifying defendant at lineup / made before alleged motive arose] is non-hearsay under FRE 801(d)(1) and is admissible for its truth.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1236 treats prior consistent statements as hearsay but admits them under an exception when offered to rebut a charge of recent fabrication. CEC § 1235 admits all prior inconsistent statements for their truth (not limited to those made under oath). CEC § 1238 admits prior identification statements.

### Admissions by Party Opponent (FRE) / Party Admissions (CEC) 🟢
Under FRE 801(d)(2), a party's own out-of-court statement offered against that party is not hearsay. The admission need not have been against the party's interest when made, may consist of opinion, and requires no personal knowledge. Categories include: (1) individual admissions (party's own words or conduct); (2) adoptive admissions (express or implied agreement with another's statement — silence may be an adoptive admission if a reasonable person would have denied the statement); (3) authorized admissions (statement by a party's agent authorized to make the statement); (4) vicarious (agent/employee) admissions (statement by an employee or agent concerning a matter within the scope of employment, made during the existence of the relationship); and (5) co-conspirator admissions (statement made by a co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy, before its conclusion).
*Apply: [party's] statement that [X] is a party admission [in category] offered against [party]; it is not hearsay and is admissible for its truth.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1220 covers party admissions as a hearsay exception (not a non-hearsay exclusion as in FRE), but the practical result is the same. CEC § 1223 covers co-conspirator statements.

## J. Hearsay Exceptions — Declarant Unavailable

### Unavailability 🟢
The declarant-unavailable exceptions require that the declarant be unavailable. Unavailability includes: (1) assertion of privilege; (2) refusal to testify despite court order; (3) lack of memory of the subject matter; (4) death, physical illness, or infirmity; and (5) absence from the proceeding despite reasonable efforts by the proponent to procure the declarant's attendance. A declarant is not unavailable if the proponent caused the unavailability.

### Former Testimony 🟢
Testimony given under oath at a prior proceeding by a now-unavailable declarant is admissible as a hearsay exception if the party against whom it is offered (or, in civil cases, a predecessor in interest) had an opportunity and similar motive to examine (develop) the testimony at the prior proceeding. In criminal cases, the party against whom the testimony is offered must have had the actual opportunity and motive to cross-examine.
*Apply: [witness] is now unavailable; [witness's] prior testimony at [proceeding] is admissible because [opposing party] had an opportunity and similar motive to cross-examine about the same subject at that time.*

### Statement Against Interest 🟡
A statement made by an unavailable declarant is admissible if, at the time of making, it was so contrary to the declarant's pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest that a reasonable person would not have made the statement unless believing it to be true. The declarant must have had personal knowledge and no motive to fabricate. Statements offered to exculpate an accused in criminal cases require corroboration. Under the FRE, the "interests" covered include financial, property, and penal interests.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement that [X] was against [her financial/criminal] interest when made and is admissible because [declarant is now unavailable and had no motive to fabricate].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1230 includes interests against "social interest" — defined broadly enough to include statements that would subject the declarant to ridicule or social disgrace — a broader scope than FRE 804(b)(3).

### Dying Declaration 🔴
A statement made by a declarant who believed death was imminent, concerning the cause or circumstances of the impending death, is admissible as a dying declaration. The declarant need not actually die, but must be unavailable to testify.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement that [X caused her injuries] made while [declarant] believed death was imminent is a dying declaration; [declarant's] current unavailability satisfies the rule.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under FRE 804(b)(2), the dying declaration exception applies only in homicide prosecutions and civil cases. Under CEC § 1242, the dying declaration is admissible in any type of proceeding — a significant expansion.

### Statement of Personal or Family History 🟢
A statement by an unavailable declarant concerning births, deaths, marriages, divorces, or other facts of personal or family history is admissible if the declarant was a member of the family in question or was intimately associated with the family, and the statement was based on the declarant's personal knowledge or knowledge of family reputation.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement regarding [family relationship/birth/death] is admissible as a family history statement because [declarant was a family member/intimate associate] and the statement concerned family history.*

### Forfeiture by Wrongdoing 🟢
When a party intentionally procures the unavailability of a declarant — through murder, intimidation, or other wrongdoing — in order to prevent the declarant from testifying, the party forfeits the right to object to the declarant's out-of-court statements on hearsay grounds. The forfeiture extends to all of the declarant's statements, not just statements made in anticipation of the proceeding.
*Apply: [party] procured [declarant's] unavailability by [wrongdoing]; [party] forfeits hearsay objections to [declarant's] prior statements.*

## K. Hearsay Exceptions — Availability Immaterial

### Present Sense Impression 🔴
A statement describing or explaining an event or condition is admissible when made while the declarant was perceiving the event or condition, or immediately thereafter. No requirement of stress or excitement distinguishes this exception from an excited utterance.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement "[X]" made while [declarant] was observing [event] is a present sense impression admissible regardless of [declarant's] current availability.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1241 is substantially narrower than FRE 803(1): it admits only statements that explain the declarant's own conduct, made while the declarant was engaged in that conduct. It does not broadly admit contemporaneous descriptions of events perceived.

### Excited Utterance 🟢
A statement relating to a startling event or condition is admissible when made while the declarant was still under the stress of excitement caused by the event. There is no strict time limit; the inquiry focuses on whether the declarant remained in a state of excitement when the statement was made.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement "[X]" was made while [declarant] was still under the stress of [startling event]; it is an excited utterance admissible regardless of [declarant's] availability.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1240 mirrors FRE 803(2); no material split.

### Then-Existing Mental, Emotional, or Physical Condition (State of Mind) 🟢
A contemporaneous statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind, emotion, sensation, or physical condition — including intent, plan, or motive — is admissible to prove that condition or, through the Hillmon doctrine, as circumstantial evidence that the declarant carried out the stated intent. The exception does not encompass statements of memory or belief about a past condition or event.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement "I intend to [do X]" is admissible as a then-existing state of mind statement; it [can/cannot] be used to infer that [declarant] carried out [intent].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1250 is substantively consistent with FRE 803(3); consistent.

### Statements for Medical Diagnosis or Treatment 🔴
A statement made by any person to a medical professional for the purpose of diagnosis or treatment — describing present symptoms, past symptoms, or the general nature and cause of the condition — is admissible as a hearsay exception. Statements identifying the fault or identity of the wrongdoer (beyond general cause) are not covered.
*Apply: [patient's] statement to [doctor] that [she had pain in her left knee following the accident] is admissible for medical treatment; [patient's] statement that [defendant ran the red light] is not admissible under this exception.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1253 dramatically narrows this exception: CA admits such statements only when made by a minor victim of child abuse or neglect. Adult statements for medical treatment do not qualify under the CEC — a major split from FRE 803(4).

### Business Records 🟡
A writing or record made in the regular course of a business or organization is admissible if: (1) it was made at or near the time of the recorded event by, or based on information transmitted by, a person with knowledge; (2) it was made and kept in the regular course of business; (3) it was the regular practice of that business to make such a record; and (4) it is authenticated by testimony or a written certification. Police reports qualify as business records in civil cases but not in criminal cases (against the accused).
*Apply: [document] is a business record because [entity] regularly kept such records, it was made at or near the time of [event] by a person with knowledge, and [authenticating witness/certification] has established those foundations.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 1270–1272 track FRE 803(6)–(7); no material split in basic elements.

### Public Records 🔴
Records of a public office or agency are admissible if they set forth: (1) the activities of the office or agency; (2) matters observed pursuant to a duty imposed by law — except that in criminal cases, observations by law enforcement officers are excluded when offered against the accused; or (3) in civil cases and against the government in criminal cases, factual findings from a legally authorized investigation.
*Apply: [public record] is admissible to show [agency activities / observed matter / investigative findings] unless [this is a criminal case and the record reflects police officer observations against the defendant].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 1280 is broader than FRE 803(8): it admits any public employee record made within the scope of duty, at or near the time of the event, if circumstances show trustworthiness. Critically, this broader standard permits police reports and factual findings to be admitted even in criminal cases — the opposite of FRE 803(8)(A)(ii)'s exclusion of law enforcement observations in criminal cases.

### Present Physical Condition / Declaration of Physical Condition 🟢
A contemporaneous statement concerning the declarant's then-existing physical condition (such as pain or symptoms) is admissible regardless of the declarant's availability. This is closely related to the then-existing state of mind exception and shares its foundational requirements: the statement must be contemporaneous and must not concern memory of a past condition.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement that [she is in pain] is admissible as a then-existing physical condition statement.*

### Past Physical or Mental Condition (CA Only) 🔴
Under CEC § 1251 (a California-only exception), a statement by an unavailable declarant concerning a past physical or mental condition — including past intent — is admissible if the condition is itself at issue in the case. No requirement of seeking medical care applies.
*Apply: [declarant's] past statement regarding [prior mental condition] is admissible under CEC § 1251 because [declarant is unavailable] and [declarant's past condition] is directly at issue.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** FRE 803(3) expressly excludes statements of memory or belief about past conditions. CEC § 1251 fills that gap for unavailable declarants. High-yield CA-only exception.

### CA Hearsay Exception — Statement Describing Physical Abuse (CA Only) 🔴
Under CEC § 1370, a statement describing the infliction or threat of physical injury to the declarant is admissible if: (1) the declarant is unavailable; (2) the statement was made at or near the time of the injury or threat; (3) the statement was recorded in writing, by audio or video recording, or was made to a law enforcement officer, medical professional, or similar official; and (4) the circumstances indicate trustworthiness.
*Apply: [victim's] statement to [police officer/nurse] describing [defendant's] physical attack is admissible under CEC § 1370 because [victim is unavailable], the statement was made shortly after the attack, and [circumstances indicate trustworthiness].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** FRE has no direct equivalent exception; the closest analogues would be excited utterance or present sense impression.

### Residual/Catch-All Exception (Federal) 🟡
Under FRE 807, a hearsay statement not covered by any specific exception may still be admitted if: (1) it has circumstantial guarantees of trustworthiness equivalent to the enumerated exceptions; (2) it is offered as evidence of a material fact; (3) it is more probative on that point than any other reasonably obtainable evidence; and (4) the proponent gives advance notice to the adverse party.
*Apply: [statement] does not fall within a specific exception but [possesses circumstantial guarantees of trustworthiness] and [is the most probative evidence available]; the proponent [did/did not] provide notice.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC has no general residual exception; this is a federal-only rule.

## L. Confrontation Clause and Crawford

### Confrontation Clause — Crawford Doctrine 🔴
Under the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause, a criminal defendant has the right to confront witnesses who testify against him. In *Crawford v. Washington* (2004), the Supreme Court held that "testimonial" out-of-court statements by a witness who is unavailable at trial may not be admitted against the defendant unless the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine that witness. Testimonial statements include formal police interrogations, preliminary hearing testimony, grand jury testimony, depositions, and statements made to law enforcement officers when the primary purpose is to create evidence for a later prosecution. Non-testimonial statements — those made in the course of an ongoing emergency — are governed by the Roberts reliability test rather than Crawford.
*Apply: [declarant's] statement to [police officer during structured interrogation] is testimonial; it may not be admitted against [defendant] unless [defendant] had a prior opportunity to cross-examine [declarant].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** The Confrontation Clause is a federal constitutional rule that applies equally in California criminal courts. California has no independent state confrontation clause analog that provides greater protection; federal Crawford doctrine controls in CA criminal cases.

## M. Privileges

### Attorney-Client Privilege 🟢
Confidential communications between an attorney and a client made during the course of seeking or providing legal advice are privileged from disclosure. The privilege belongs to the client, who may waive it voluntarily, and it survives the client's death. The privilege covers oral and written communications, documents prepared by the attorney for the client, and documents prepared by the client for the attorney. Exceptions include: (1) the crime-fraud exception (services sought to aid a future crime or fraud); (2) the client places the communication at issue in litigation; and (3) a dispute between attorney and client about the professional relationship itself.
*Apply: [client's] statement to [attorney] during a legal consultation is [privileged/not privileged] because [it was/was not a confidential communication / an exception applies].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 950–962 codify the attorney-client privilege in greater statutory detail than FRE, but the substance is consistent. CA distinction: the attorney-client privilege survives only through the winding up of an estate (not indefinitely, as in most other states). Prop. 8 does not override privileges in criminal cases.

### Spousal Immunity Privilege 🔴
In criminal proceedings, a spouse may not be compelled to testify adversely against the other spouse. Requirements: (1) a valid marriage must exist at the time of trial; (2) the privilege protects against all adverse testimony, not just confidential communications; (3) the witness spouse holds and may assert or waive the privilege; and (4) the privilege applies only in criminal cases.
*Apply: [spouse A] may not be compelled to testify against [spouse B] in this criminal case because a valid marriage exists at the time of trial; [spouse A] holds the privilege and [may/may not] choose to testify.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** Under FRE, the witness-spouse holds the privilege (as in Trammel). Under CEC § 970, the defendant-spouse also holds a privilege to prevent the other spouse from testifying — both spouses hold the privilege in California. This is a meaningful split.

### Confidential Marital Communications Privilege 🟢
A spouse may not be compelled to disclose, and may prevent the other spouse from disclosing, confidential communications made between them during the marriage. Requirements: (1) the parties must have been married at the time of the communication (no need to be currently married at time of trial); (2) the communication must have been made in confidence; (3) both spouses hold the privilege; and (4) the privilege applies in both civil and criminal proceedings.
*Apply: [spouse's] statement to [other spouse] made during the marriage is a confidential marital communication protected by privilege; [either spouse] may assert it to prevent disclosure.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC § 980 is substantively consistent with FRE; no material split. Note: this privilege covers only confidential communications (narrower than spousal immunity), but applies in all proceedings.

### Physician-Patient Privilege 🔴
Confidential communications between a patient and a physician made in the course of a professional relationship for the purpose of treatment are privileged. Requirements: (1) a professional relationship; (2) information acquired from or about the patient during the course of treatment; and (3) the information is necessary for diagnosis or treatment. Exceptions: (1) the patient is not genuinely seeking treatment; and (2) the patient-litigant exception — the privilege is waived when the patient voluntarily places physical or mental condition in issue in litigation.
*Apply: [patient's] communication to [doctor] about [condition] is privileged because it was confidential and made in the course of seeking treatment; the privilege [is/is not] waived because [patient did/did not place condition at issue].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** This is a critical split: the FRE does not recognize a physician-patient privilege. California (CEC §§ 990–1007) does. Whenever physician-patient privilege appears on a CA bar essay, remember it does not exist in federal court.

### Psychotherapist-Patient Privilege 🟢
Confidential communications between a patient and a licensed psychotherapist, psychiatrist, or clinical social worker are privileged from disclosure. This privilege is recognized in both federal courts (since *Jaffee v. Redmond*, 1996) and California. Exceptions: (1) the patient has placed mental condition at issue in litigation; (2) the therapist has reason to believe the patient is a danger to herself or others (the Tarasoff duty to warn exists in CA); and (3) in California, the privilege does not apply when the patient is a crime victim under age 16.
*Apply: [patient's] communications to [therapist] are privileged; the privilege [is/is not] overcome because [patient placed mental state at issue / therapist had reason to believe patient was a danger to others].*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** CEC §§ 1010–1027 codify the psychotherapist privilege; consistent with federal *Jaffee*. The CA-specific exceptions (danger to self/others; minor crime victim) are the key nuances.

### Additional California Privileges (CA Only) 🔴
California recognizes several privileges with no federal analog: (1) Parent-Child Privilege (CEC § 970-ish; protects confidential communications between parent and child); (2) Accountant-Client Privilege (CEC § 1010 et seq.; confidential communications with a licensed accountant); (3) Sexual Assault Counselor-Victim Privilege (CEC § 1035 et seq.); (4) Domestic Violence Counselor-Victim Privilege (CEC § 1037 et seq.); and (5) Clergy-Penitent Privilege (CEC § 1030 et seq.).
*Apply: [communication] between [parent and child / client and accountant / victim and counselor / penitent and clergy] is privileged under California law; this privilege [does/does not] exist under the FRE.*
> **CA divergence (CEC vs FRE):** All of these are California-only privileges. The parent-child and accountant-client privileges are especially high-yield on the CA bar.
