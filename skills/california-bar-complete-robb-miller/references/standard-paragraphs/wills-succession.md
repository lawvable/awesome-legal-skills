## A. Components of a Will

### Integration 🟢
A will may consist of multiple pages or papers, all of which are treated as a single instrument if two conditions are met: (1) the testator intended those papers to form part of the will at the time of execution, and (2) the papers were physically present when the will was executed. Courts look to internal references, physical attachment, and consistent page numbering as evidence of both intent and presence.
*Apply: [Paper/exhibit] [was/was not] physically present at execution of [T]'s will, and [internal language / attachment / numbering] [does/does not] reflect T's intent to incorporate it.*
> Thus, the disputed pages [are/are not] integrated into the will.

---

### Incorporation by Reference 🟢
A writing that does not otherwise qualify as part of the will may nonetheless be given testamentary effect if the proponent establishes four elements: (1) the document was a writing, (2) it was already in existence when the will was signed, (3) the will clearly identifies the document, and (4) the testator intended to incorporate it into the will. The document need not itself satisfy will formalities; its validity derives from the incorporating will.
*Apply: [Document] [existed/did not exist] before [T] executed the will on [date]; the will [identifies/does not identify] it as "[quoted language]"; and T's [stated/apparent] purpose shows [intent/no intent] to incorporate.*
> Thus, the document [is/is not] incorporated by reference and its terms [will/will not] govern distribution.

---

### Facts of Independent Significance 🟢
A will may fill in blanks or make gifts by reference to acts or events that have significance independent of their effect on the disposition of the estate. Because such facts—like the contents of a bank account or the persons employed by the testator—exist for purposes entirely separate from the will, they are considered trustworthy enough to supply testamentary meaning without satisfying will formalities.
*Apply: [T's reference to "[blank in will]"] is explained by [act/event], which has independent significance as [reason unrelated to will, e.g., normal business practice, family relationship].*
> Thus, the gift is given effect through the doctrine of facts of independent significance.

---

### Pour-Over Wills and UTATA 🟡
A testamentary gift that "pours over" into a pre-existing inter vivos trust may be validated by three distinct theories. First, if the trust instrument was in existence before will execution and is clearly identified in the will, incorporation by reference applies. Second, even without will reference, facts of independent significance may validate the disposition because the trust has legal effect apart from the will. Third, and most broadly, the Uniform Testamentary Additions to Trusts Act (UTATA) allows pour-over provisions to be effective if a valid trust was executed before or concurrently with the will—even if the trust is subsequently amended—so long as the trust is identified in the will and the trust's terms are set out in a written instrument.
*Apply: T's will directs the residue to [trust name] executed on [date]; that trust [was/was not] in existence at will execution; [trust was/was not] later amended; [which theory/theories] validate(s) the pour-over.*
> Thus, the pour-over [is/is not] valid under [theory].

---

### Probate Jurisdiction and Choice of Law 🟢
A will is subject to primary probate in the state of the testator's domicile at death; real property located elsewhere requires ancillary probate in the situs jurisdiction. A will is admissible to probate if it complies with the law of: (1) the place where probate is sought, (2) the place where the will was executed, or (3) the testator's domicile at the time of execution. California follows this multi-state validation approach, meaning a will valid in its state of execution will typically be honored in California.
*Apply: T was domiciled in [state] at death and owned [real property] in [other state]; the will was executed in [state] and [satisfies/does not satisfy] the formalities of [applicable law].*
> Thus, the will [can/cannot] be admitted to probate in [jurisdiction].

## B. Attested Will Formalities

### Attested Will — Elements 🔴
To create a valid attested will under California law, four requirements must be satisfied: (1) the will must be in writing; (2) it must be signed by the testator, or by another person in the testator's presence and at the testator's direction, or by a conservator acting pursuant to court order; (3) the testator must sign or acknowledge the signature in the simultaneous presence of at least two witnesses; and (4) each witness must understand that the instrument being signed is the testator's will. Notably, the witnesses need not sign in each other's presence or in the testator's presence, there is no required order of signing, and the signature may appear anywhere on the document—so long as all signings constitute one continuous transaction.
*Apply: [T] [signed/acknowledged] the document in the [presence/non-presence] of [W1] and [W2] simultaneously; [W1 and W2] [did/did not] understand the instrument was T's will; signing occurred as [one transaction/separate occasions].*
> Thus, the attested will [does/does not] satisfy California's formal requirements.

---

### Harmless Error / Dispensing Power 🔴
California has adopted a dispensing power under Probate Code § 6110(c)(2): a document that does not fully comply with attested-will formalities may nonetheless be admitted to probate if the proponent establishes by clear and convincing evidence that the decedent intended the document to constitute a will. This is a narrow safety valve—courts look to the totality of circumstances, including the document's content, the decedent's statements, and context of execution.
*Apply: [T]'s document [fails which formality]; the proponent offers [evidence] to show by clear and convincing evidence that T intended the document as [a will/codicil].*
> Thus, the document [may/may not] be saved by California's harmless-error rule.

> **CA divergence:** California's § 6110(c)(2) dispensing power is broader than the common law strict compliance rule. Always flag when formalities are imperfect.

---

### Interested Witness 🟡
A witness who is also a beneficiary under the will does not invalidate the will itself, but creates a rebuttable presumption that the witness-beneficiary procured the gift through wrongdoing. Unless at least two other disinterested witnesses attested the will, that presumption arises; if the witness-beneficiary cannot rebut it, they may take only the amount they would have received by intestate succession—and only if that amount does not exceed the devise. The presumption does not arise if the witness takes solely in a fiduciary capacity. A witness who receives no direct gift but obtains a substantial indirect benefit (e.g., a parent whose child is the devisee) may also be an interested witness; argue both ways.
*Apply: [W] is named as [devisee/indirect beneficiary]; [only two / more than two] disinterested witnesses also attested; [W] offers [rebuttal evidence].*
> Thus, [W] [takes the full gift / takes only the intestate share / takes nothing].

---

### Joint and Mutual Wills 🟢
A joint will is a single testamentary instrument signed by two or more testators. Mutual wills are separate instruments with reciprocal provisions. The mere execution of joint or mutual wills does not, by itself, create a presumption that the parties entered into a contract not to revoke; however, the joint or mutual character of the wills may constitute evidence of such a contract when considered alongside other circumstances such as a recital of agreement or consistent treatment over time.
*Apply: [T1 and T2] executed [joint/mutual] wills on [date]; the will [does/does not] contain an express recital of a binding agreement; [other evidence of contract]*.*
> Thus, [T1/T2]'s later revocation [does/does not] breach an enforceable contract not to revoke.

## C. Holographic Wills

### Holographic Will — Validity 🔴
California recognizes holographic wills under Probate Code § 6111. A holographic will is valid if: (1) the material provisions—those affecting who takes what—are in the testator's own handwriting, and (2) the testator signs the document anywhere on it. No witnesses are required, and no date is technically mandatory. Printed text on a pre-printed form may appear alongside handwritten material provisions without invalidating the will; the statute requires only that the material terms themselves be handwritten. Extrinsic evidence is admissible to establish testamentary intent, and a statement of intent need not itself be handwritten.
*Apply: T's document contains [material provisions] in T's handwriting; T's signature appears at [location]; [printed/typed text] appears but does not include material terms; extrinsic evidence [supports/undermines] testamentary intent.*
> Thus, the document [qualifies/does not qualify] as a valid California holographic will.

> **CA divergence:** Most common-law jurisdictions reject holographic wills; California expressly validates them under § 6111. This is a frequent exam trap.

---

### Holographic Will — Undated 🟡
Although a date is not required for a holographic will in California, the absence of a date creates two potential problems: (1) if an undated holograph is inconsistent with another testamentary instrument, the undated holograph is invalid to the extent of the inconsistency unless its time of execution can be established as later; and (2) if the testator lacked capacity at any time during which the undated holograph might have been executed, the holograph is invalid unless it is affirmatively established that it was executed during a period of capacity. Where two undated holographs are both inconsistent and neither can be dated, neither is probated to the extent of the inconsistency.
*Apply: T's holograph is undated; T also executed [other instrument]; T [had/lacked] capacity during [time periods]; extrinsic evidence [can/cannot] establish when the holograph was signed.*
> Thus, the undated holograph [is/is not] valid [in full / to the extent of inconsistency].

## D. Testamentary Capacity and Intent Defects

### Testamentary Capacity 🟢
A testator must possess testamentary capacity at the moment of execution. This requires that the testator: (1) is at least eighteen years of age; (2) understands the nature and extent of the property being disposed of; (3) knows the natural objects of their bounty—typically spouse, children, parents, and others whose interests are affected; and (4) understands the nature of the act being performed, namely that they are making a will. The capacity standard is the same whether the testator is making or revoking a will. If the entire will is executed without capacity, it is void and the estate passes by intestate succession—unless a prior validly executed will exists, which then may be probated because the defective later will could not effectively revoke it.
*Apply: At the time T executed the will, T [did/did not] understand [extent of property / natural objects / nature of act]; capacity is [established/disputed] by [medical records / witness testimony / T's conduct].*
> Thus, T [had/lacked] testamentary capacity and the will is [valid/void].

---

### Insane Delusion 🔴
Even if a testator possesses general testamentary capacity, a specific insane delusion may invalidate any portion of the will affected by it. An insane delusion is a false belief that: (1) is held by the testator; (2) is the product of a disordered mind rather than a merely mistaken inference; (3) under the California standard, is unsupported by even a scintilla of evidence (some jurisdictions ask whether no rational person could hold the belief given available facts); and (4) actually influenced the testamentary act. Only the portion of the will tainted by the delusion is invalid; that portion falls into the residuary estate or, if there is none, passes by intestate succession.
*Apply: T believed [false proposition]; no evidence supports this belief [under CA's "not a scintilla" test]; T's disposition of [gift to X / disinheritance of Y] [was/was not] caused by that belief.*
> Thus, the delusion [does/does not] invalidate the [specific gift / disinheritance] and that portion [passes by residue / passes by intestacy].

> **CA divergence:** California uses the "not even a scintilla of evidence" standard—stricter than the majority "no rational person" test.

---

### Fraud in the Execution 🟢
Fraud in the execution occurs when the testator signs a document believing it to be non-testamentary—such as a deed or contract—when it is in fact a will, or when someone forges the testator's signature on a testamentary instrument. All four fraud elements must be shown: (1) a misrepresentation of a material fact; (2) knowledge by the perpetrator that the representation is false; (3) intent to induce the testator's action or inaction; and (4) actual inducement. Because the testator never intended to execute a will at all, the entire instrument is void, and the estate passes by intestate succession or under a prior valid will.
*Apply: [Wrongdoer] [presented document as / forged signature on] [instrument]; T [signed believing it was / did not sign] a will; fraud elements [are/are not] satisfied.*
> Thus, the entire will is void for fraud in the execution.

---

### Fraud in the Inducement 🟢
Fraud in the inducement occurs when the wrongdoer's misrepresentation operates on the testator's mind to produce a specific gift or omission, while the testator understands they are executing a will. Only the tainted portion is invalid; the court may: (1) redirect the affected gift to the residuary clause, (2) apply intestate succession to that portion, or (3) impose a constructive trust on the wrongdoer for the benefit of the intended beneficiary. The remaining, untainted provisions of the will stand.
*Apply: [Wrongdoer] misrepresented [fact] to T, inducing T to [give gift to X / disinherit Y]; absent the misrepresentation, T would have [alternative disposition]; the [specific gift / omission] is the only affected provision.*
> Thus, only the portion induced by fraud is invalid; the rest of the will stands and the court may impose a constructive trust.

---

### Fraud in Preventing Revocation 🟢
When a wrongdoer uses fraud, duress, or undue influence to prevent the testator from revoking a will, the testator's true intent cannot be expressed through formal revocation. Because the will remains technically valid but the wrongdoer must not profit from the misconduct, a constructive trust is imposed: the wrongdoer holds any benefit received for the benefit of those who would have taken had revocation occurred.
*Apply: T expressed intent to revoke the will in favor of [intended beneficiary], but [wrongdoer] [prevented T from doing so by fraud/duress]; the will remained unrevoked at T's death.*
> Thus, a constructive trust is imposed on [wrongdoer]'s share for the benefit of [intended beneficiary].

---

### Undue Influence — Prima Facie Case 🔴
A will or gift may be invalidated for undue influence when the testator's free agency is so subjugated by another that the testamentary act reflects the will of the influencer rather than the testator. A prima facie case requires all five elements: (1) the testator was susceptible due to some weakness—physical, emotional, mental, or financial; (2) the alleged influencer had opportunity to exercise influence; (3) the influencer actively participated in procuring the gift; (4) the resulting disposition is unnatural or unexplained; and (5) the influencer benefits financially, including substantial indirect benefit (e.g., a parent whose child is the devisee). Examine both direct and indirect financial benefit carefully.
*Apply: T [was/was not] susceptible because [weakness]; [influencer] had opportunity through [relationship/access]; [influencer] actively participated by [specific acts]; the disposition [is/is not] unnatural because [reason]; [influencer] benefits [directly/indirectly] by [amount/relationship].*
> Thus, the prima facie case for undue influence [is/is not] established.

---

### Undue Influence — Common-Law Presumption 🟡
Where a full prima facie case cannot be proven, a presumption of undue influence arises from four elements: (1) a confidential relationship between the testator and the alleged influencer (California recognizes any relationship in which one person reasonably reposes trust and confidence in another, including close friends); (2) active participation by the influencer in procuring the gift; (3) an unnatural or unexplained result; and (4) financial benefit to the influencer. The presumption shifts the burden to the beneficiary to prove the gift was the product of the testator's free and independent judgment.
*Apply: T and [influencer] had a confidential relationship because [facts]; [influencer] actively participated by [acts]; the gift is unnatural because [reason]; [influencer] benefits financially.*
> Thus, the presumption arises and [influencer] must rebut it or forfeit the gift.

---

### Undue Influence — CA Statutory Presumption (§ 21380) 🔴
California Probate Code § 21380 creates a presumption—conclusive in some cases, rebuttable in others—that a donative transfer is the product of undue influence when the transferee is: (1) the person who drafted the instrument; (2) a fiduciary who transcribed the instrument; (3) a "care custodian" of a "dependent adult" when the instrument was executed during the period of care or within 90 days before or after; or (4) a spouse, domestic partner, cohabitant, employee, or relative within the third degree of any person in categories (1)–(3). The presumption is conclusive as to the drafter and those acting through the drafter; it is rebuttable for others. Two exceptions take a transfer outside the statute: (a) the transferee is the spouse or cohabitant of the transferor (even if that person was the drafter), or (b) the instrument was reviewed and approved by an independent attorney who counseled the transferor. When the presumption applies, the disqualified person is treated as having predeceased the transferor without issue for purposes of the excess above the intestate share; the remainder passes by residue, intestacy, or constructive trust.
*Apply: [Beneficiary] [drafted the instrument / acted as fiduciary / was care custodian] for T, a dependent adult; [exception applies/does not apply]; [beneficiary] [did/did not] obtain independent attorney review.*
> Thus, the § 21380 presumption [conclusively/rebuttably] applies and [beneficiary] [forfeits the entire gift / may rebut the presumption].

> **CA divergence:** The § 21380 statutory presumption is a CA-specific rule with no common-law equivalent. It is a high-yield exam trigger whenever a drafter, caregiver, or fiduciary takes under the instrument.

## E. Mistake

### Mistake in Execution 🟢
A mistake in execution occurs when the testator signs the wrong document—believing it to be non-testamentary or believing it to be a different will. If the testator signs a document mistaking it entirely for a non-testamentary instrument, the document is not probated as a will because the testator lacked testamentary intent. However, when two testators (e.g., spouses) execute reciprocal wills and each mistakenly signs the other's instrument, a court may exercise its equitable power to reform the wills by substituting the correct names, thereby giving effect to the parties' manifest intent.
*Apply: T signed [document]; T [believed it was / did not understand it was] a will; [if mutual mistake,] T1 and T2 each signed [the other's instrument].*
> Thus, the document [is/is not probated / is reformed] to reflect the parties' actual intent.

---

### Mistake in Inducement 🟢
A mistake in inducement arises when the testator makes or withholds a gift based on a mistaken factual belief that is not a product of another's fraud. The general rule affords no relief: courts will not reform a will based solely on the testator's unilateral mistake in forming the intent to make a particular disposition. The narrow exception allows relief only when both the mistaken belief and what the testator would have done but for the mistake appear on the face of the will itself—a rare circumstance.
*Apply: T gave [gift] to [beneficiary] based on T's mistaken belief that [fact]; the will [does/does not] state both the mistaken belief and the alternative intent T would have had.*
> Thus, relief for mistake in inducement is [unavailable / available under the narrow exception].

---

### Mistake in Description / Ambiguity 🟡
Where a will contains a latent or patent ambiguity—the written words are clear on their face but apply equally to two or more objects or persons, or the written words describe no existing person or property—extrinsic evidence is admissible to identify the testator's actual intent. Courts will consider prior drafts, surrounding circumstances, and statements by the testator to resolve the description and give the gift its intended effect.
*Apply: The will devises "[description]" to "[name]"; [latent ambiguity because two persons match / no such property exists]; extrinsic evidence shows T intended [the gift to go to X / described as Y].*
> Thus, the ambiguity is resolved in favor of [intended beneficiary/property] based on [evidence].

---

### Mistake in Content 🟡
A mistake in content arises when the will names the wrong beneficiary or makes the wrong gift due to a drafting or transcription error. Two sub-rules govern. First, where words were mistakenly *omitted*, courts generally grant no relief: the will is enforced as written, and the intended but absent gift fails—though dependent relative revocation may occasionally provide an indirect remedy. Second, where words were mistakenly *added*—such as when a bequest is inserted by drafting error—a court may strike the surplusage if the extrinsic evidence clearly demonstrates the addition was unintended. Neither scenario permits the court to rewrite the testamentary disposition; the most the court may do is excise language that should never have appeared.
*Apply: T's will [names X instead of Y / includes a gift of [property] that T never intended]; the error is one of [omission / addition]; extrinsic evidence [does/does not] clearly show the mistaken addition.*
> Thus, relief [is unavailable for the omission / the erroneous addition is stricken] and the gift [fails / is corrected by excision].

---

### Dependent Relative Revocation (DRR) 🔴
Under the doctrine of dependent relative revocation, a revocation that is conditioned upon an assumption that later proves false is given no legal effect, and the original will is treated as though it was never revoked. The doctrine applies most commonly when the testator destroys or cancels a valid will in the mistaken belief that a new or substantially similar instrument will effectuate the same intent; if the new instrument is invalid or otherwise fails, the revocation of the first is deemed conditional on the second's validity—and since the second fails, the first is revived by operation of law. Even if the first will has been physically destroyed, it may be probated upon testimony from any witness who knows its contents; that witness need not be one of the attesting witnesses.
*Apply: T [destroyed/cancelled] the first will in reliance on [new instrument]; the new instrument [failed/is invalid] because [reason]; the two instruments [are/are not] substantially identical in purpose; T would [prefer the first will over intestacy / not prefer it].*
> Thus, DRR [does/does not] apply and the first will [is/is not] revived.

> **CA divergence:** DRR is a judge-made equitable doctrine fully recognized in California. The "substantially identical" test and the ability to use non-attesting witnesses to prove a destroyed will's contents are high-yield details.

---

### Pretermitted (Omitted) Child 🔴
A pretermitted child is one who is born or adopted after the execution of all testamentary instruments and who is not provided for in those instruments. Such a child receives an intestate share from the estate, which is funded first from property not passing under the will or a revocable trust, then pro rata from all devisees. Three exceptions defeat the claim: (1) the testamentary instrument itself discloses, on its face, that the omission was intentional; (2) the testator provided for the child outside the testamentary plan with the intent that such provision serve in lieu of a testamentary gift; or (3) at the time all testamentary instruments were executed, the testator already had children and left substantially all of the estate to the other parent of the omitted child. A codicil that republishes the will after the child's birth cuts off pretermission unless the child is also omitted from the codicil. Note that a child born or adopted before execution of all instruments takes nothing unless omitted due to T's mistaken belief that the child was dead or non-existent.
*Apply: [Child] was [born/adopted] on [date]; all testamentary instruments were executed on [earlier date]; [exception (1)/(2)/(3) applies/does not apply]; [codicil executed on date] [did/did not] republish after child's birth.*
> Thus, [child] [is/is not] pretermitted and [receives/does not receive] an intestate share.

---

### Omitted Spouse 🔴
An omitted spouse is a person who marries the testator after the testator executes all testamentary instruments and who is not provided for in those instruments. The omitted spouse receives a statutory share: (1) all of the decedent's half of the community property and quasi-community property (bringing the survivor to 100% of CP/QCP), plus (2) an intestate share of the decedent's separate property not to exceed one-half of that separate property. Three exceptions defeat the claim: (1) the instrument's face discloses intentional omission; (2) the testator provided for the spouse outside the testamentary plan; or (3) the spouse signed a valid pre- or postnuptial waiver. The omitted spouse's share is funded first from assets not passing under the will or revocable trust, and pro rata from all beneficiaries thereafter.
*Apply: [Spouse] married T on [date] after T executed the will on [earlier date]; [exception applies/does not apply]; the estate includes [CP/QCP/SP] of [amounts].*
> Thus, [spouse] [is/is not] an omitted spouse and receives [statutory share calculation].

> **CA divergence:** California's omitted-spouse statute is broader than the common law; the spouse takes all CP/QCP automatically plus an intestate share of SP.

## F. Revocation

### Revocation by Physical Act 🟢
A will may be revoked by physical act if three requirements are satisfied: (1) the will is subjected to a recognized act—burning, tearing, cancelling, destroying, or obliterating; (2) the testator has the simultaneous intent to revoke at the time of the act; and (3) the act is performed by the testator personally, or by another person acting in the testator's conscious presence and at the testator's express direction. Cancellation (drawing lines through text) and interlineation (writing between lines) revoke the affected provision but generally do not add new material unless all new material is in the testator's handwriting, creating a holographic codicil. If duplicate originals exist and one is revoked by physical act, the other is also revoked as a matter of law. If a will is found mutilated at the testator's death after having last been seen in the testator's possession, a rebuttable presumption arises that the testator mutilated it with intent to revoke.
*Apply: T [burned/tore/crossed out] the will; T [had/lacked] revocatory intent at the moment of the act; the act was performed [by T personally / by X in T's presence at T's direction].*
> Thus, the will [was/was not] validly revoked by physical act.

---

### Revocation by Subsequent Instrument 🟢
A will may be revoked in whole or in part by a later testamentary instrument—either expressly, through a revocation clause, or implicitly, if the later instrument makes a complete and inconsistent disposition of the entire estate. A later holographic will can revoke a prior attested will; no formal hierarchy between will types controls. A codicil that partially addresses an earlier will is presumed to revoke only inconsistent provisions of the earlier will, leaving the rest intact.
*Apply: T's [second will/codicil] executed on [later date] [expressly states revocation / makes a complete new disposition / addresses only [specific provisions]]; the [earlier will / affected provisions] [are/are not] wholly superseded.*
> Thus, the [first will / affected provisions] [are/are not] revoked by the subsequent instrument.

---

### Revival After Revocation 🟡
If a testator revokes will #2 (which had itself revoked will #1), will #1 is not automatically revived. Revival of will #1 requires affirmative evidence that the testator intended to revive it—mere destruction of will #2 is insufficient. Courts will consider extrinsic evidence of the testator's intent. If the testator executes a codicil to will #1 and later destroys only the codicil, a rebuttable presumption arises that the testator intended to revoke only the codicil, not the underlying will.
*Apply: T executed will #1, then will #2 (which revoked #1), then destroyed will #2; T [expressed/did not express] intent to revive will #1; extrinsic evidence [supports/negates] revival intent.*
> Thus, will #1 [is/is not] revived.

---

### Revocation by Operation of Law — Dissolution of Marriage 🟢
Under California law, when a marriage or registered domestic partnership is finally dissolved or annulled, any testamentary gift to the former spouse and any fiduciary appointment of the former spouse are revoked by operation of law. Legal separation alone does not trigger revocation. These revocations are default rules and apply unless the will expressly provides otherwise. If the testator later remarries the same person without executing a new will, the revoked provisions are reinstated.
*Apply: T's will left [gift] to [spouse]; T and spouse [divorced/separated/annulled] on [date]; T [did/did not] execute a new will afterward; T [did/did not] remarry spouse.*
> Thus, the gift to [spouse] [is/is not] revoked by operation of law.

---

### Codicils and Revocation Interactions 🟡
A codicil is a testamentary instrument that modifies, amends, or revokes an existing will and must satisfy the same formality requirements as any attested or holographic will. Executing a codicil "republishes" the underlying will, treating it as re-executed on the date of the codicil—which may affect pretermission analysis and incorporation by reference. When a testator revokes a codicil without revoking the underlying will, a rebuttable presumption arises that only the codicil was intended to be revoked. Conversely, when a testator revokes the underlying will after executing a codicil, a rebuttable presumption arises that both the will and the codicil were intended to be revoked.
*Apply: T executed a codicil on [date] modifying [provisions]; T later [revoked only codicil / revoked the will]; presumption is [only codicil revoked / both revoked]; [evidence to rebut].*
> Thus, the [codicil / will / both] [are/are not] revoked.

## G. Construing Gifts

### Ademption by Extinction 🔴
Ademption by extinction occurs when a specific devise—a gift of a particular, identifiable item distinct from all others in the estate—is no longer owned by the testator at death. Under the common-law identity test, the gift simply fails and the devisee takes nothing. California modifies this rule with an intent-based approach: the court considers whether the gift is truly specific versus general, and whether the testator intended the gift to fail if the specific property was no longer available. If the property was converted (e.g., sold by a conservator, condemned, or replaced by insurance proceeds), the devisee may have a right to the sale proceeds or substitute property to avoid an unintended forfeiture. Note that general devises (gifts of economic value from general assets) are never adeemed.
*Apply: T devised [specific item] to [beneficiary]; at T's death, [item] [no longer exists / was sold / was destroyed]; the gift is [specific/general/demonstrative]; under CA's intent approach, T [did/did not] intend the gift to fail.*
> Thus, the specific devise [is/is not] adeemed; [beneficiary] [takes/does not take and may have a claim to proceeds].

> **CA divergence:** California's intent-based test for ademption by extinction is more protective of devisees than the common-law identity test.

---

### Ademption by Satisfaction 🟢
A testamentary gift is satisfied—and therefore adeemed—to the extent the testator makes a pre-death transfer to the beneficiary intended as a substitute for the testamentary gift. In California, satisfaction requires a contemporaneous writing signed by either the testator or the beneficiary acknowledging that the transfer is in satisfaction of the testamentary gift. Absent such a writing, the inter vivos transfer is treated as an additional gift, not a satisfaction. Note that ademption by advancement applies the same writing requirement in the intestacy context: an heir apparent's intestate share is reduced by any advancement only if a contemporaneous writing so provides.
*Apply: T transferred [property] to [beneficiary] during T's lifetime; [writing signed by T or beneficiary / no writing exists]; the writing [does/does not] state this is in satisfaction of the testamentary gift.*
> Thus, the testamentary gift [is/is not] satisfied and [beneficiary] [takes the devise in full / takes the devise reduced by the advancement].

### Abatement 🟡
Abatement is the process by which testamentary gifts are reduced or eliminated to pay the testator's debts or to fund the shares of pretermitted or omitted heirs. In the absence of contrary instructions in the will, gifts abate in the following order for debt payment: (1) intestate property; (2) residuary devises; (3) general devises to non-relatives; (4) general devises to relatives; (5) specific devises to non-relatives; (6) specific devises to relatives. Within each class, gifts abate pro rata in proportion to value. Demonstrative devises are treated as specific to the extent funded from the designated source and as general for any excess. For pretermitted or omitted heir shares of testators dying after 1998, abatement runs first from property not passing under the will or revocable trust, then pro rata from all beneficiaries under the will and trust.
*Apply: The estate has [insufficient assets]; outstanding debts/omitted heir share totals [amount]; the will contains [residuary / general / specific] devises to [beneficiaries]; T [died before/after 1998].*
> Thus, [devise] abates [fully/partially] and [beneficiary] receives [reduced amount].

### Exoneration 🔴
At common law, if a testator devised specific property subject to an encumbrance for which the testator was personally liable, the executor was required to pay off the debt from general estate assets before delivering the property to the devisee—known as the right to exoneration. California has rejected this rule: under California law, there is no automatic right to exoneration. A specific devisee takes the property subject to any encumbrance, including mortgages and liens, unless the will expressly provides for exoneration of that particular debt.
*Apply: T devised [encumbered property] to [devisee]; the will [does/does not] expressly direct exoneration of the [mortgage/lien]; [amount of encumbrance].*
> Thus, [devisee] takes the property [subject to the encumbrance / exonerated, per the will's express direction].

> **CA divergence:** California rejects the common-law automatic exoneration rule. Devisee takes subject to the lien unless the will says otherwise.

### Lapse and Anti-Lapse 🔴
A beneficiary must survive the testator to take a devise; a deceased beneficiary's gift lapses. Under the general lapse rule, a lapsed gift falls into the residuary estate (or, if already in the residuary, is distributed to the other co-residuary devisees; if no residuary, it passes by intestate succession). California's anti-lapse statute modifies this result: if the predeceased beneficiary was "kindred" of the testator or of the testator's current or former spouse, and the beneficiary left surviving issue, those issue step into the beneficiary's place and take the gift by representation. The anti-lapse statute applies to wills and revocable trusts. To override anti-lapse and cause the gift to fail, the testator must use language indicating a contrary intent—such as "if [beneficiary] survives me."
*Apply: [Beneficiary] predeceased T; [beneficiary] [was/was not] kindred of T or T's spouse; [beneficiary] [did/did not] leave issue; the will [does/does not] express a contrary survival intent.*
> Thus, the gift [lapses and passes by residue/intestacy / passes to beneficiary's issue under anti-lapse].

### Simultaneous Death 🟢
When the devolution of property depends on the order of death, and it cannot be determined by clear and convincing evidence who survived whom—such as in a common disaster—California law deems each person to have predeceased the other for purposes of their respective estates. This avoids the absurdity of property passing through two estates in quick succession. If the testator requires survivorship by a specified period and the beneficiary dies within that period, the beneficiary is treated as having predeceased the testator.
*Apply: T and [beneficiary] died in [accident/disaster]; no clear and convincing evidence establishes that [beneficiary] survived T; [beneficiary]'s estate [would/would not] benefit if found to have survived.*
> Thus, [beneficiary] is deemed to have predeceased T, and the gift passes [by anti-lapse / by residue / by intestacy].

### Class Gifts 🟡
A gift to a described group—such as "my children," "my nieces and nephews," or "my issue"—is a class gift. Class membership is determined at the time distribution is due (usually T's death), and the class remains open to include all persons who qualify under the description as of that time. A class gift is subject to anti-lapse: if a class member predeceases the testator and was kindred of T or T's spouse, the deceased member's issue take the member's share. If a class member predeceases without issue, or anti-lapse does not apply, the surviving class members divide the entire gift equally. Adopted children and, in California, children of the testator's domestic partner, are generally included in class designations unless a contrary intent appears.
*Apply: T's will gives "[class description]" to [group]; [class member] predeceased T [with/without issue]; [adopted / half-blood / non-marital] child [may/may not] qualify.*
> Thus, [surviving members / deceased member's issue] take [proportionate shares].

---

### After-Acquired Property and Increases 🟢
A will passes all property the testator owns at death, including property acquired after the will was executed, unless the will's terms limit the gift to property owned at execution. Stock dividends and stock splits on a specifically devised security pass to the specific devisee if the testator still owned the underlying shares at death. During the period of estate administration, all increases (dividends, rents, splits) on specifically devised property accrue to the specific devisee; general devisees receive no such increases.
*Apply: T's will devised [property] to [beneficiary]; T [acquired/divested] [additional property] after execution; [stock dividend/split] occurred [before/after] T's death; the devise is [specific/general].*
> Thus, [beneficiary] [takes/does not take] the after-acquired property or increase.

## H. Intestate Succession

### Intestate Succession — Spouse's Share 🔴
When a California decedent dies without a will, or when a will fails to dispose of all property, the surviving spouse or registered domestic partner takes the decedent's entire one-half interest in community property and quasi-community property, resulting in the surviving spouse owning 100% of all CP and QCP. As to the decedent's separate property, the surviving spouse's share depends on the other survivors: (1) if no issue, parents, siblings, or issue of a deceased sibling survive, the spouse takes all separate property; (2) if one child (or that child's issue) survives, the spouse takes one-half of the separate property; (3) if two or more children (or their issue) survive, the spouse takes one-third of the separate property; and (4) if no issue but a parent or sibling (or their issue) survives, the spouse takes one-half of the separate property.
*Apply: T died [intestate / with a partial will] survived by [spouse] and [other heirs]; the estate consists of [CP/QCP: amount] and [SP: amount]; number of surviving children or their issue.*
> Thus, [spouse] takes [calculation of CP/QCP share] plus [SP fraction] totaling [amount].

> **CA divergence:** Surviving spouse takes all CP/QCP automatically. The SP fractions differ from the UPC and most common-law jurisdictions.

---

### Intestate Succession — Other Heirs 🟢
If the decedent leaves no surviving spouse or domestic partner, the estate passes in the following priority order: (1) issue; (2) parents; (3) issue of parents (siblings and their descendants); (4) grandparents and their issue, issue of a predeceased spouse, next of kin by degree of relationship, parents of a predeceased spouse, issue of parents of a predeceased spouse; and ultimately, (5) the state by escheat. Issue take by the California "per capita with representation" method: the estate is divided at the first generational level where at least one living taker exists, each living taker at that level takes one equal share, and the shares of deceased persons at that level are combined and divided equally among the next generational level, repeating down. Pure per stirpes (dividing at the first level of descent even if none survive) applies only when the will expressly says "per stirpes" or "by representation."
*Apply: Decedent died leaving [list of surviving relatives]; [who/how many] at each generational level; per-capita-with-representation calculation begins at [first generational level with survivors].*
> Thus, [heir] takes [fractional share] of the estate.

---

### Half-Blood, Non-Marital, and Adopted Children 🟢
California treats relatives of the half-blood (those sharing only one common ancestor) identically to whole-blood relatives for purposes of intestate succession—a half-sibling inherits as a full sibling. Non-marital children inherit from and through both parents if a parent-child relationship existed; marital status is irrelevant. An adopted child is treated as the natural child of the adopting parent(s) in all respects, and the legal relationship with the natural parent is severed—with two exceptions: the natural-parent relationship is preserved if (1) the adoption is by the spouse or domestic partner of the natural parent, or (2) the adoption occurs after the death of one natural parent. Step- and foster children qualify as children for intestacy purposes if the relationship was established during minority, continued throughout the parties' lifetimes, and the natural or adoptive parent would have adopted but for a legal barrier.
*Apply: [Heir] is T's [half-sibling / non-marital child / adopted child / step-child]; [applicable exception if adoption]; T's [parent / sibling] is T's [whole/half] blood.*
> Thus, [heir] [takes/does not take] as a [relationship] under California intestate succession.

## I. Spousal Property Rights

### Community Property and Quasi-Community Property at Death 🔴
California's community property system gives each spouse an undivided one-half interest in all property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California. A testator may dispose of only their one-half of the community property and one-half of the quasi-community property (property acquired while domiciled elsewhere that would have been CP had the parties been California domiciliaries) by will; the surviving spouse already owns the other half outright and it does not pass through probate. If the testator's will attempts to dispose of more than the testator's one-half share of CP or QCP, the surviving spouse may exercise a "widow's election": either (a) accept the benefits given under the will, or (b) renounce all will benefits and reclaim the surviving spouse's rightful one-half of all CP and QCP.
*Apply: T's will purports to dispose of [all / more than half] of the CP/QCP totaling [amount]; the surviving spouse's own one-half is [amount]; the widow's election [arises/does not arise].*
> Thus, [surviving spouse] [accepts the will's terms / exercises the widow's election and reclaims their half].

> **CA divergence:** No analogous statutory protection exists in common-law property states. Always analyze CP/QCP whenever T was married and property was acquired during marriage.

---

### Illusory Transfers of Quasi-Community Property 🟡
An inter vivos transfer of quasi-community property to a third party without adequate consideration is generally permitted because the surviving (non-acquiring) spouse holds only an expectancy in QCP, not a present property interest. However, if the transfer is illusory—meaning the acquiring spouse retains control or an interest in the property—the transfer will be set aside as a fraud on the surviving spouse's expectancy, and the surviving spouse may invoke the widow's election to recapture the transferred QCP.
*Apply: The acquiring spouse transferred QCP to [third party] during marriage; consideration [was/was not] adequate; the acquiring spouse [retained/did not retain] control or a beneficial interest.*
> Thus, the transfer [is/is not] illusory and the surviving spouse [may/may not] invoke the widow's election.

## J. Unworthy Heirs

### Slayer Rule 🔴
A person who feloniously and intentionally kills the decedent is disqualified from taking any benefit by will, trust, or intestate succession. A final criminal conviction for the killing is conclusive in probate; in the absence of a conviction, the probate court determines guilt by a preponderance of the evidence. The killer is treated as having predeceased the decedent for all purposes of succession, and the anti-lapse statute does not apply to the killer's share—meaning the killer's own issue do not step into the killer's place.
*Apply: [Killer] [was convicted of / is alleged to have] feloniously and intentionally killing T; the probate court [determines / applies the conviction] by [preponderance standard]; killer's issue [do not take under anti-lapse].*
> Thus, [killer] is treated as having predeceased T and takes nothing; the gift passes [by residue/intestacy].

---

### Elder Abuse Disqualification 🟡
California extends the unworthy-heir doctrine to abusers: a person who commits elder or dependent adult abuse against a decedent who, at the time of the abuse, could not manage their own affairs, is disqualified from taking by will or intestate succession. The abuse must be proven by clear and convincing evidence. A disqualified abuser is treated as having predeceased the decedent, and the abuser's share passes as though the abuser died before the decedent.
*Apply: [Person] committed [elder/dependent adult] abuse against T; T [could/could not] manage their own affairs at the time; abuse is proven by [evidence] to the clear and convincing standard.*
> Thus, [abuser] is disqualified and treated as having predeceased T.

## K. No-Contest Clauses and Will Contests

### No-Contest Clauses 🔴
A no-contest clause (in terrorem clause) in a will or trust penalizes a beneficiary who brings a legal challenge to the instrument by causing forfeiture of all benefits. California enforces no-contest clauses only when the contestant brings a contest without probable cause. A contest brought with probable cause—a reasonable belief in the validity of the legal challenge, based on information available before filing—does not trigger forfeiture even if the contest ultimately fails. The clause will not be triggered by: (1) a challenge to the instrument's validity based on forgery or lack of due execution; (2) a petition to determine the scope or effect of the clause itself; or (3) specified other actions expressly listed in the Probate Code. California's approach is more protective of good-faith contestants than many jurisdictions.
*Apply: T's will contains a no-contest clause; [beneficiary] filed a will contest on grounds of [capacity/undue influence]; [beneficiary] [had/lacked] probable cause based on [available information]; the contest [does/does not] fall within a statutory exception.*
> Thus, the no-contest clause [does/does not] apply and [beneficiary] [forfeits / retains] their bequest.

> **CA divergence:** California enforces no-contest clauses only against contests brought without probable cause. A probable-cause contest does not trigger forfeiture. Many states apply these clauses more broadly.

---

### Will Contest — Standing and Grounds 🟢
To contest a will, a party must have standing—they must be a person with a pecuniary interest that would be directly and adversely affected if the contested will were admitted to probate. Typical contestants include intestate heirs who take nothing under the will, beneficiaries of a prior will who receive less under the contested will, and beneficiaries who would receive more if the will were invalid. Grounds for contest include: lack of testamentary capacity, insane delusion, undue influence, fraud, mistake in execution, forgery, and failure to comply with formal requirements. A contest must be filed within a statutory period after the will is admitted to probate.
*Apply: [Contestant] is [heir / prior-will beneficiary]; [contestant] would [take/take more] if the will were denied probate; grounds for contest include [capacity / undue influence / fraud / forgery].*
> Thus, [contestant] [has/lacks] standing and the contest [may proceed / must be dismissed].

## L. Contracts Regarding Wills

### Contracts to Make or Not Revoke a Will 🟡
A contract to make a will, to leave a bequest, or not to revoke a will is governed by contract law, not the law of wills. Such a contract is enforceable only if it is established by one of five means: (1) the will itself or another instrument states the material provisions of the contract; (2) the will expressly references an existing contract; (3) a signed writing by the decedent evidences the agreement; (4) clear and convincing evidence of an agreement between the decedent and the promisee that is enforceable in equity (estoppel); or (5) clear and convincing evidence of an agreement between the decedent and a third party for the benefit of the claimant, enforceable in equity. A cause of action on the contract does not accrue until the promisor dies. Remedies include damages, constructive trust, and specific performance.
*Apply: [Claimant] alleges [decedent] contracted to leave [property] to [claimant]; the contract is evidenced by [will language / signed writing / extrinsic clear-and-convincing proof]; the estate [does/does not] honor the agreement.*
> Thus, the contract [is/is not] enforceable and [claimant] may seek [damages / constructive trust / specific performance].

## M. Posthumous Children and Survivorship Issues

### Posthumous Children 🟢
A child conceived during the lifetime of the decedent but born after the decedent's death is treated as a living heir for all purposes of intestacy and as a beneficiary of the decedent's will. The posthumous child takes the same share they would have received had the decedent survived to the date of birth. For class-gift purposes, the class remains open to include posthumous children if born within the period of gestation following the decedent's death.
*Apply: T died on [date]; [child] was conceived before T's death and born on [later date]; the will / intestacy rules [provide/do not provide] for posthumous children.*
> Thus, [posthumous child] takes as a living [heir/devisee].
