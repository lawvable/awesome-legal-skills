## A. Creation of a Valid Private Express Trust

### Defined — Private Express Trust 🟢
A private express trust is a fiduciary arrangement in which a settlor transfers legal title to property to a trustee, who holds and manages that property for the benefit of one or more identified beneficiaries pursuant to the settlor's expressed intent and a valid legal purpose. All five elements — settlor capacity, intent, trust property (res), ascertainable beneficiary, and valid purpose — must concur at the moment of creation; the absence of any one element is fatal.
*Apply: Identify whether [settlor] had capacity, expressed [mandatory/precatory] language, transferred [identified property], named [specific or class of beneficiaries], and pursued a [lawful] purpose.*
> **Thus,** if all five elements are present, a valid private express trust is established.

---

### Settlor — Capacity and Intent (Mandatory vs. Precatory Words) 🔴
The settlor must have the legal capacity to transfer property (testamentary capacity if created by will; contractual capacity if created inter vivos). The settlor's intent must be manifested by mandatory language — words of direction or command — not precatory language such as "I hope," "I wish," or "I desire," which create only a moral obligation and vest the transferee with fee-simple ownership. Where only precatory words appear, extrinsic parol evidence may be admitted in some jurisdictions to elevate the language to a mandatory command, but the mere hope of a settlor is insufficient without more.
*Apply: Determine whether [settlor's] words — "[quoted language]" — compelled [trustee] to act for [beneficiary's] benefit or merely expressed a wish.*
> **Thus,** precatory words alone create no enforceable trust; the purported trustee takes the property outright unless surrounding evidence establishes a mandatory direction.

---

### Trust Property (Res) 🟢
Any presently existing, transferable interest in property — including a fee simple, a future interest, or a life insurance policy — is sufficient to serve as the trust res. A mere expectancy (such as an anticipated inheritance not yet vested), future profits from a business not yet earned, and a spouse's unilateral transfer of the other spouse's community property interest are illusory and cannot form the corpus of a trust. A debt the settlor owes to the beneficiary may constitute a valid res because it represents an existing legal obligation, not a speculative future right.
*Apply: Determine whether [the property or interest] is [presently existing and transferable] or merely [a future expectancy or illusory interest].*
> **Thus,** only a presently existing, transferable interest can serve as valid trust corpus; a future expectancy fails at the threshold.

---

### Ascertainable Beneficiary 🟢
A valid private express trust requires at least one ascertainable beneficiary — an identifiable person, class of persons, corporation, or (under modern law) unincorporated association capable of enforcing the trust. The beneficiary need not be named by name so long as the class or description allows the court to identify who can demand performance. An unascertainable or wholly indefinite beneficiary — such as "my friends" without further limiting criteria — defeats a private express trust but may support a charitable trust if the purpose benefits society at large.
*Apply: Determine whether [beneficiary description] allows a court to identify [specific individuals or a defined class] who can enforce the trust against [trustee].*
> **Thus,** a trust naming an indefinite class with no limiting principle fails for want of an ascertainable beneficiary unless recast as a charitable trust.

---

### Trustee — Appointment and Court Substitution 🟢
Every private express trust must have a trustee to hold legal title and carry out fiduciary duties, but the trust will not fail merely because the named trustee predeceases the settlor, declines to serve, or is removed — equity will not allow a trust to fail for want of a trustee, and a court of equity will appoint a successor. Until appointment, legal title vests in the court or is held in abeyance.
*Apply: If [named trustee] cannot or refuses to serve, determine whether a court should appoint [a substitute trustee] to preserve the trust.*
> **Thus,** absence of a trustee does not destroy an otherwise valid trust; the court will fill the vacancy.

---

### Valid Legal Purpose 🟢
A trust must be created for a purpose that is neither illegal nor contrary to public policy. If the illegal provision is severable — that is, the trust can stand without it — the court will excise the offending condition and preserve the trust. If the illegality is inseparable from the trust's core purpose, the court will either void the trust from inception or allow the trustee to retain the property outright, depending on the degree of wrongdoing. Where a trust becomes illegal after creation through changed circumstances, a resulting trust is imposed, requiring the trustee to return the property to the settlor (or the settlor's estate if deceased).
*Apply: Identify whether [trust's purpose or condition] is illegal or against public policy, whether it is [severable], and what disposition is appropriate.*
> **Thus,** an inseverable illegal purpose voids the trust and triggers a resulting trust back to the settlor or estate.

## B. Methods of Creation

### Inter Vivos Trust — Transfer in Trust (Third-Party Trustee) 🔴
When the settlor designates a third party as trustee and wishes to create the trust during the settlor's lifetime, the settlor must actually transfer the trust property to that trustee. For real property, the settlor must execute and deliver a deed — actual, symbolic, or constructive delivery is sufficient, but a mere promise to deliver is not. For personal property, the settlor must deliver the property (or an instrument of transfer) to the trustee; an unexecuted intent is insufficient. Without completed transfer, no trust is created and the property remains the settlor's.
*Apply: Determine whether [settlor] completed transfer of [real/personal property] to [third-party trustee] by [deed/delivery] or merely made an unenforceable promise.*
> **Thus,** a trust fails where the settlor never completed delivery of the res to the designated trustee.

---

### Inter Vivos Trust — Declaration in Trust (Settlor as Trustee) 🟡
When the settlor declares herself trustee of property she already owns, no transfer to a third party is required, but formalities still govern. For real property, the declaration must satisfy the Statute of Frauds — it must be in a signed writing that identifies the settlor as trustee of the specific property. For personal property, a present, unambiguous manifestation of intent to hold as trustee is sufficient; no writing is required.
*Apply: Determine whether [settlor's] declaration concerning [real/personal property] satisfies [SOF writing / present manifestation of intent] to constitute herself as trustee.*
> **Thus,** a declaration in trust over real property without a signed writing fails even though no delivery to a third party is needed.

---

### Testamentary Trust 🟢
A trust intended to take effect at the settlor's death must be created within a valid will that satisfies all applicable Probate Code formalities — proper execution, testamentary capacity, and freedom from undue influence or fraud. Because the will is the instrument of creation, no separate delivery of the res is required; the trust comes into existence upon the testator's death and admission of the will to probate.
*Apply: Determine whether the will creating a trust for [beneficiaries] was validly executed under [applicable Probate Code] with [capacity, writing, signature, witnesses].*
> **Thus,** a testamentary trust stands or falls with the validity of the will in which it is embedded.

## C. Trustee Powers and Duties

### Trustee Powers — Enumerated and Implied 🟢
A trustee possesses all powers expressly enumerated in the trust instrument and, in addition, all implied powers that are reasonably necessary and appropriate to carry out the trust's purposes — even if not spelled out. Implied powers include the authority to sell trust property, enter contracts, hire agents, maintain insurance, and take other acts a prudent person would undertake in administering property for another. The two categories together define the outer boundary of permissible trustee action; any act beyond both enumerated and implied powers is a breach of duty.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee's act] — [specific action] — falls within an [express grant in the instrument] or is [impliedly necessary to carry out the trust's purpose], or instead exceeds both categories.*
> **CA divergence:** California Probate Code §§ 16220–16249 set out a detailed statutory list of trustee powers that apply unless the trust instrument expands or restricts them; these statutory powers supplement, rather than replace, implied powers.
> **Thus,** a trustee who acts within both enumerated and implied powers is not liable for that act, even absent an express authorization in the instrument.

---

### Duty of Loyalty — No-Further-Inquiry Rule 🔴
The duty of loyalty requires a trustee to administer the trust solely in the interests of the beneficiaries, free from any conflicting personal interest. Under the no-further-inquiry rule, any transaction in which a trustee self-deals — buying or selling trust assets for the trustee's own benefit, transacting with the trustee's spouse, or borrowing trust funds — is voidable without any inquiry into whether the transaction was fair or made in good faith. The trustee's subjective honesty or the reasonableness of the price are wholly irrelevant. If the self-dealing causes a loss, the trustee is surcharged the full loss; if the self-dealing generates a profit, the trustee holds those profits as a constructive trustee for the benefit of the trust.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee] engaged in [self-dealing transaction] with [trust assets/spouse/borrowing], triggering the no-further-inquiry rule regardless of [fairness/good faith].*
> **CA divergence:** California follows this rule; Probate Code § 16002 codifies the duty of loyalty, and self-dealing transactions remain voidable at the beneficiary's election.
> **Thus,** a trustee who self-deals is liable for losses by surcharge and for profits as a constructive trustee, no questions asked.

---

### Duty of Due Care 🟢
A trustee must exercise the degree of care that a reasonably prudent person would apply when managing another person's property — not merely his own affairs. Under the modern standard (which California follows), the reference point is the management of another's property, imposing a higher standard than the older common-law formulation limited to one's own affairs. A trustee who possesses special professional expertise is held to the standard of that expertise when relevant.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee's] conduct — [specific act or omission] — measured against a reasonably prudent person managing another's property, fell below the required standard.*
> **Thus,** a trustee whose management falls short of a reasonably prudent manager of another's property breaches the duty of care and is liable for resulting losses.

---

### Duty to Account 🟢
The trustee has an ongoing duty to furnish beneficiaries with regular accountings — written statements itemizing trust income, expenses, distributions, and the current value of trust assets — at reasonable intervals throughout the administration of the trust. Beneficiaries who are denied an accounting may bring a formal court action to compel one. In California, the Probate Code requires trustees to account at least annually and upon termination of the trust or upon a change in trusteeship.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee] provided [beneficiary] with [periodic / annual] statements of [income, expenses, distributions] sufficient to satisfy the accounting duty.*
> **CA divergence:** Prob. Code § 16062 requires annual accountings to each beneficiary unless waived in the trust instrument or by the beneficiary.
> **Thus,** a trustee who fails to render periodic accountings breaches fiduciary duty and may be compelled by court order to account.

---

### Duty to Invest — Prudent Investor Standard 🔴
The trustee's investment duty has evolved through several standards: (1) older common-law jurisdictions applied a "prudent man" standard keyed to the trustee's own affairs; (2) the modern common-law standard elevates this to management of another's property; and (3) the Uniform Prudent Investor Act (UPIA), adopted in California, requires the trustee to invest as a reasonably prudent investor, evaluating each investment decision in the context of the total portfolio rather than in isolation — no single investment is assessed without reference to its role within the whole. Under any standard, the trustee has an absolute duty to diversify trust investments so that losses in any sector will not devastate the entire portfolio, absent special circumstances justifying a more concentrated position.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee's] investment of [assets] in [specific instruments] — evaluated against the portfolio as a whole — satisfied the prudent investor standard and the duty to diversify.*
> **CA divergence:** California has adopted the Uniform Prudent Investor Act (Prob. Code §§ 16045–16054); performance is judged on the portfolio as a whole, and delegation of investment functions to agents is permissible under specified conditions.
> **Thus,** a trustee who concentrates assets in a single investment without justification, or fails to diversify, breaches the investment duty regardless of whether any single holding was reasonable standing alone.

---

### Duty to Earmark 🟡
The trustee must label or designate trust property as belonging to the trust — for instance, holding real property in the trustee's name in a representative capacity or recording securities in the name of the trust. Under the common-law approach, a trustee who fails to earmark is personally liable for any resulting loss, regardless of whether the failure to earmark actually caused that loss. The modern and majority rule requires a causal nexus: the trustee is liable only to the extent the failure to earmark caused or contributed to the loss.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee] failed to label [trust property] as trust property, and under [CL/modern] — whether a causal link between the failure and [the loss] must be shown.*
> **Thus,** under the modern rule, a trustee escapes liability for failure to earmark only if the loss would have occurred regardless of proper labeling.

---

### Duty to Segregate 🟢
The trustee must keep trust funds strictly separate from the trustee's own personal funds and must also maintain each separate trust administered by that trustee in its own distinct account. Commingling personal assets with trust assets — or mixing assets from two different trusts — is an independent breach of fiduciary duty. If commingling occurs and a loss follows, the trustee is personally liable for that loss, and where the funds are so intermixed that specific identification is impossible, a court may impose a constructive trust or equitable lien on all commingled funds.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee] deposited [trust funds] into [personal or multi-trust account], breaching the duty to segregate, and trace any resulting loss to [the commingled pool].*
> **Thus,** a trustee who commingles trust and personal funds is liable for any resulting loss and may be subject to constructive trust over the entire commingled fund.

---

### Duty Not to Delegate 🟡
A trustee may consult and rely upon the advice of professional advisors — attorneys, accountants, investment professionals — in forming trustee decisions, but may not surrender decision-making authority to those advisors or to any third party, including a co-trustee. Where two or more trustees serve, they must act unanimously unless the trust instrument provides otherwise; one co-trustee may not lawfully delegate final authority to the other. Under California's adoption of the UPIA, however, a trustee may delegate investment and management functions to an agent if the trustee (1) exercises reasonable care in selecting the agent and establishing the scope of delegation, (2) establishes appropriate scope and terms, and (3) monitors the agent's performance.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee's] grant of authority to [advisor/co-trustee/agent] constituted mere consultation or an impermissible delegation of final decision-making power.*
> **CA divergence:** Prob. Code § 16052 permits delegation of investment functions under the UPIA framework; the traditional categorical bar on delegation is softened in that context.
> **Thus,** a trustee who hands off the final investment or administrative decision to an advisor without oversight has breached the duty not to delegate.

## D. Beneficiary Remedies for Trustee Breach

### Beneficiary Remedies — Overview 🔴
When a trustee breaches any fiduciary duty, beneficiaries may pursue one or more of the following equitable and legal remedies: (1) surcharge — a money judgment against the trustee personally for losses caused by the breach; (2) imposition of a constructive trust — requiring the trustee to hold wrongfully obtained profits for the trust's benefit; (3) tracing the trust property into the hands of a subsequent holder and impressing an equitable lien on it, unless that holder is a bona fide purchaser for value without notice of the breach; (4) ratification of the transaction if the beneficiary determines it was ultimately advantageous; and (5) removal of the trustee through a court proceeding upon a showing of serious misconduct or incapacity.
*Apply: Identify which remedy — [surcharge / constructive trust / tracing / ratification / removal] — best fits [trustee's] breach of [specific duty] and whether any [BFP defense] cuts off tracing.*
> **Thus,** a beneficiary's arsenal of remedies is cumulative; the loss of one avenue (e.g., a BFP cuts off tracing) does not preclude surcharge against the defaulting trustee personally.

## E. Trustee Liability to Third Parties

### Trustee Liability in Contract — CL vs. Modern 🟡
Under the common-law approach, a trustee who contracts on behalf of the trust is personally liable on that contract because the trust has no separate legal personality; however, if the trustee acted without personal fault and fully within the scope of trust authority, the trustee may seek indemnification from trust assets. Under the modern approach, when the counterparty has notice that the trustee is acting in a representative (fiduciary) capacity, the suit must be brought against the trustee in that representative capacity — the trust estate, not the trustee's personal estate, bears primary liability.
*Apply: Determine whether [third party] knew [trustee] was contracting in a [representative/fiduciary] capacity, and under [CL/modern] rule, whether [trustee] faces personal or representative liability.*
> **Thus,** under the modern rule, a well-noticed counterparty can recover only against the trust estate, not the trustee's personal assets, absent personal fault.

---

### Trustee Liability in Tort — CL vs. Modern 🟡
At common law, a trustee is personally liable for torts committed in the course of trust administration but may obtain indemnification from trust assets if the trustee was not personally at fault — for example, where a supervised employee committed the tort. Under the modern view, the trustee is personally liable in tort only when the trustee was personally at fault (e.g., the trustee herself acted negligently or directed the tortious conduct); if the tort arose entirely from the conduct of agents and the trustee exercised due care in their selection and supervision, the trust estate rather than the trustee personally bears the loss.
*Apply: Determine whether [trustee] was personally at fault in [the tort arising from trust administration] or whether it was caused by a properly supervised [employee/agent] under [CL/modern] standard.*
> **Thus,** under the modern rule, a non-negligent trustee escapes personal tort liability; the claim runs against the trust estate.

## F. Charitable Trusts

### Charitable Trust — Definition and Beneficiary Distinction 🟢
A charitable trust is a trust created for a public benefit recognized by equity — such as the advancement of education, religion, relief of poverty, promotion of health, or other purposes beneficial to the community. Unlike a private express trust, a charitable trust has no ascertainable private beneficiary; the public or a sufficiently large segment of society functions as the collective beneficiary. The settlor's motive is irrelevant; what matters is whether the actual effect of the gift benefits the public rather than a private interest. Because there is no private beneficiary to enforce the trust, the state attorney general has standing to do so in equity.
*Apply: Determine whether [trust purpose] benefits [a definable segment of] the public at large rather than [named private individuals], making it charitable rather than private.*
> **Thus,** a trust for public benefit survives even without an ascertainable private beneficiary, and the attorney general enforces it.

---

### Charitable Trust — Rule Against Perpetuities Does Not Apply 🟢
Unlike private express trusts — which must satisfy the Rule Against Perpetuities (RAP) by vesting within a life in being plus twenty-one years — charitable trusts are exempt from the RAP and may endure in perpetuity. This exemption reflects the law's policy preference for encouraging lasting charitable endowments. Note, however, that a gift over from one charitable trust to another charitable trust also escapes the RAP; a gift over from a charitable to a private trust must still comply.
*Apply: Determine whether the perpetual duration of [charitable trust] violates RAP, and note that the RAP exemption does not extend to [any private interest following the charitable gift].*
> **Thus,** a properly constituted charitable trust may operate indefinitely without violating the RAP.

---

### Cy Pres Doctrine 🔴
When a settlor manifests a general charitable intent but the specific mechanism chosen to carry it out becomes impossible, impracticable, or illegal after creation, a court acting in equity may apply cy pres — "as near as possible" — to modify the trust's administrative terms to carry out the settlor's broader charitable purpose. Cy pres is unavailable if the settlor had only a specific (not general) charitable intent, in which case the specific purpose's failure causes the trust to fail and a resulting trust returns the corpus to the settlor or estate. Courts look to the four corners of the trust instrument and extrinsic evidence to determine whether general charitable intent existed.
*Apply: Determine whether [settlor] had a [general or specific] charitable intent; if general and [the mechanism has become impossible/impracticable], argue the court should modify to [the nearest alternative purpose].*
> **Thus,** cy pres preserves the trust by redirecting the charitable purpose as nearly as possible to the settlor's broader intent when the original mechanism fails.

---

## G. Honorary Trusts

### Honorary Trust — Nature and RAP Vulnerability 🟡
An honorary trust is an arrangement that lacks an ascertainable private beneficiary and does not rise to the level of a charitable trust — typical examples are trusts to care for a pet, maintain a grave, or erect a monument. Because no one has standing to enforce it, the trustee is bound only in honor, not by law; the trustee may carry out the purpose voluntarily but is not compelled to do so. If the trustee declines to perform, the trust fails and a resulting trust is imposed, returning the corpus to the settlor or estate. Honorary trusts inherently violate the RAP because there is no human life in being to measure the perpetuities period; courts either strike the trust at inception or limit its duration to twenty-one years.
*Apply: Determine whether [trust for pet/grave/monument] has an ascertainable beneficiary or charitable purpose; if neither, classify as honorary and assess whether the trustee will [voluntarily perform or decline], and how the court addresses the RAP violation.*
> **Thus,** an honorary trust that the trustee refuses to perform, or that exceeds twenty-one years, collapses into a resulting trust to the settlor.

---

## H. Resulting Trusts

### Resulting Trust — Generally 🟢
A resulting trust is an implied-in-fact trust that arises from the presumed intent of the parties when circumstances suggest the transferee was not meant to take the property beneficially. Courts impose a resulting trust — requiring the holder to return the property to the settlor or the settlor's estate — in six recognized situations: (1) a private express trust that terminates by its own terms and makes no provision for the remainder; (2) a private express trust that fails for lack of beneficiary or illegality; (3) excess corpus remains in a private express trust after its purposes are accomplished; (4) a charitable trust ends by impossibility and cy pres is unavailable; (5) a purchase-money resulting trust; and (6) a semi-secret trust.
*Apply: Identify which [of the six categories] applies to [the failed, excess, or incomplete trust] and conclude that [trustee/holder] must return the property to [settlor or estate].*
> **Thus,** whenever trust property has no valid destination, a resulting trust returns it to the source.

---

### Purchase-Money Resulting Trust 🟡
When person A pays the purchase price for property and directs that title be conveyed to person C, a rebuttable presumption arises that C holds the property as trustee for A — the actual payor. This presumption is overcome, however, where C is a close family member of A (spouse, child, or other near relative), in which case the law instead presumes a gift. The presumption of resulting trust vs. gift depends on the closeness of the relationship and any evidence of donative intent.
*Apply: Determine whether [A] paid for property titled in [C's] name, whether [C] is [closely related to A], and whether facts rebut the [gift / resulting trust] presumption.*
> **Thus,** a stranger-titleholder is presumed trustee for the payor; a close-relative titleholder is presumed donee — but both presumptions are rebuttable.

---

### Semi-Secret Trust 🟢
A semi-secret trust arises when a will makes a gift to a named person "as trustee" or "to hold in trust" but fails to identify the beneficiary on the face of the will. Because the trust is apparent on the will's face but the beneficiary is omitted, the trust fails for want of an ascertainable beneficiary and a resulting trust is imposed — the property returns to the testator's residuary estate or passes by intestacy. Extrinsic parol evidence is inadmissible to supply the missing beneficiary in most jurisdictions because admitting it would allow oral additions to a will in violation of the Statute of Wills.
*Apply: Determine whether the will names [trustee] "to hold in trust" but omits the beneficiary, triggering a [resulting trust] rather than allowing [extrinsic evidence] to fill the gap.*
> **Thus,** a semi-secret trust fails and the property results back to the estate; parol evidence cannot cure the omission.

## I. Constructive Trusts

### Constructive Trust — Generally 🟢
A constructive trust is an equitable remedy — not a true trust — imposed by a court to prevent unjust enrichment or remedy fraud. The court declares the wrongdoer a trustee by operation of law and orders immediate transfer of the property to the person who should rightfully have it. Constructive trusts arise in four principal contexts: (1) a trustee profits through self-dealing in violation of the duty of loyalty; (2) a will is procured by fraud in the inducement or undue influence, and the intended gift never reaches the true beneficiary; (3) a transferor relies on an oral promise to hold real property in trust; or (4) a secret trust is established outside the will.
*Apply: Identify which [of the four categories] — [self-dealing / fraud / oral real estate / secret trust] — supports imposing a constructive trust on [wrongdoer] for the benefit of [true intended beneficiary].*
> **Thus,** a constructive trust transfers the property from the unjust enrichee to the rightful owner as equity demands.

---

### Oral Real Estate Trust (Constructive Trust) 🔴
When a settlor conveys real property to a transferee on an oral understanding that the transferee will hold it for the benefit of a third party, the transferee may ordinarily invoke the Statute of Frauds to defeat enforcement — no written instrument evidencing the trust exists. However, the Statute of Frauds cannot be used as a shield for fraud, and equity will impose a constructive trust notwithstanding the oral arrangement in three circumstances: (1) the settlor and transferee were in a preexisting fiduciary or confidential relationship; (2) the transferee committed fraud in the inducement — promising to hold for B while intending from the outset to keep the property; or (3) the intended beneficiary or settlor detrimentally relied on the oral promise.
*Apply: Determine whether [transferee] can invoke the SOF against [oral trust for B], and whether [fiduciary relationship / fraud in inducement / detrimental reliance] defeats that defense.*
> **Thus,** equity imposes a constructive trust on a Statute-of-Frauds-invoking transferee where that invocation would itself perpetrate a fraud.

---

### Secret Trust (Constructive Trust) 🟢
A secret trust arises when a will makes what appears on its face to be an outright gift to a beneficiary, but the devisee made a prior oral promise to the testator to hold the property for the benefit of a third party (the true intended beneficiary). Unlike a semi-secret trust, the will's face reveals no trust at all. In a secret trust, extrinsic parol evidence is admissible to establish both the existence of the promise and the identity of the intended beneficiary, because admission of that evidence does not add to the will — it merely prevents the named devisee from fraudulently keeping property never intended for her own benefit. The court imposes a constructive trust on the devisee for the true beneficiary.
*Apply: Determine whether [named devisee] made an oral promise to [testator] to hold [the devise] for [third party B], and whether parol evidence is admissible to prove [the secret arrangement].*
> **Thus,** a constructive trust prevents the secret-trust devisee from retaining an outright gift contrary to a prior oral promise made to the testator.

---

## J. Totten Trusts

### Totten Trust — Nature, Revocation, and Elevation 🟡
A Totten trust — also called a "tentative trust" — is a revocable bank-account arrangement in which the depositor (settlor) opens an account in the form "A in trust for B," naming B as beneficiary of whatever balance remains at A's death. It confers no present interest on the beneficiary; the depositor retains full dominion and may revoke at any time before death by withdrawing the funds, changing the account designation, or (in some jurisdictions) by an express provision in a will. A Totten trust may be elevated into a full private express trust if the depositor manifests a present irrevocable intent to create a trust — for example, by telling the beneficiary about the arrangement and relinquishing control — in which case all fiduciary duties and trust rules attach.
*Apply: Determine whether [depositor's] account — titled "[A in trust for B]" — was elevated to a private express trust by [notice to beneficiary / relinquishment of control], or remained a revocable Totten trust revocable by [withdrawal / name change / will].*
> **Thus,** a Totten trust remains the depositor's revocable property until either death or an act sufficiently manifesting a present intent to irrevocably create a trust.

---

## K. Restrictive Trust Provisions

### Spendthrift Trust — Voluntary and Involuntary Alienation 🔴
A spendthrift provision in a trust instrument prohibits the beneficiary from voluntarily assigning or transferring her beneficial interest and prevents creditors from levying on that interest before it is paid to the beneficiary. Without such a provision, a beneficiary may freely transfer her interest (voluntary alienation), and creditors of an insolvent beneficiary may attach it (involuntary alienation). A self-settled spendthrift trust — in which the settlor is also the beneficiary — provides no protection from the settlor's own creditors under the involuntary alienation rule, because no jurisdiction permits a person to place assets beyond the reach of creditors while retaining the beneficial enjoyment; there is a split of authority on voluntary alienation in self-settled trusts.
*Apply: Determine whether [trust] contains a valid spendthrift clause, whether [beneficiary or creditor] seeks [voluntary transfer / involuntary levy], and whether [settlor = beneficiary] destroys the protection.*
> **CA divergence:** California generally enforces spendthrift trusts (Prob. Code § 15300–15309) but does not recognize self-settled spendthrift trusts as a shield against the settlor's creditors.
> **Thus,** a spendthrift clause protects a non-settlor beneficiary from both voluntary assignment and creditor attachment until distributions are made.

---

### Support Trust — Alienation Rules 🟢
A support trust limits the trustee to distributions necessary for the beneficiary's health, education, maintenance, and support. Because the scope of distributions is tied to need, courts generally apply the same voluntary and involuntary alienation rules as for spendthrift trusts: the beneficiary's interest is not freely transferable and cannot be attached by creditors to the extent distributions are required for support. A creditor providing goods or services that directly further the support purpose may, in some jurisdictions, have a claim against support trust distributions.
*Apply: Determine whether the trust limits distributions to [health, education, maintenance, support], and whether [beneficiary's creditor] seeks to attach an interest that is shielded by the support restriction.*
> **Thus,** a support trust's restriction on alienation mirrors the spendthrift trust's protection, limiting both voluntary transfers and creditor attachments to the support purpose.

---

### Discretionary Trust — Voluntary and Involuntary Alienation 🟡
A discretionary trust vests the trustee with sole and absolute discretion over whether to make any distributions at all and, if so, in what amount. Because the beneficiary holds no fixed or certain entitlement — the trustee may decide to pay nothing — the beneficial interest is not assignable: a voluntary transferee stands in the beneficiary's shoes and receives only what the trustee chooses to pay. Similarly, a creditor cannot attach the interest because there may be nothing to attach. However, if the trustee has notice of a creditor's judgment against the beneficiary and then exercises discretion to make a payment, the trustee must pay the creditor rather than the beneficiary.
*Apply: Determine whether [trust] gives [trustee] sole discretion over distributions, whether [beneficiary's transferee or creditor] can compel payment, and whether [trustee's notice of judgment] redirects any discretionary payment to [creditor].*
> **Thus,** in a discretionary trust, neither assignees nor creditors can compel distributions, but once the trustee decides to pay, known creditors' claims take precedence.

## L. Modification and Termination

### Modification by Settlor — Reserved Power vs. Power to Revoke 🟢
A settlor who expressly reserves the power to modify may alter any term of the trust consistent with the reserved power. Additionally, a settlor who retains the power to revoke necessarily retains the power to modify, because revocation is the greater power — one who can destroy the trust entirely may logically amend it. A settlor who reserves neither power creates an irrevocable trust that she cannot unilaterally alter.
*Apply: Determine whether [settlor] expressly reserved the power to [modify / revoke], and whether the attempted modification falls within that reserved power.*

### Revocability of Trusts — CA Presumption vs. Common-Law Default 🔴
At common law, the majority rule is that a trust is irrevocable unless the settlor expressly reserves the power to revoke. California reverses this default: under Probate Code § 15400, every trust is presumed revocable unless the trust instrument expressly states that it is irrevocable. This is a high-yield exam divergence that must be identified whenever the essay involves a CA trust and the question of revocability arises. A trust declared irrevocable in its instrument cannot later be unilaterally revoked by the settlor absent consent of all beneficiaries.
*Apply: If [trust instrument] is silent on revocability and the trust is governed by CA law, the trust is [revocable by default under § 15400]; if the instrument says "irrevocable," the settlor [cannot unilaterally revoke].*
> **CA divergence:** Prob. Code § 15400 — presumption of revocability is the opposite of the common-law majority rule. Always flag on a CA essay.
> **Thus,** in California, a settlor can revoke or modify a trust at any time unless the trust instrument expressly forecloses that power.

### Modification by Court — Deviation Power 🟡
Even when a settlor did not reserve a power of modification, a court of equity may exercise its deviation power to modify the administrative or managerial provisions of a trust — but not its distributive provisions or the identities of beneficiaries — where unanticipated circumstances have arisen that the settlor did not foresee and where deviation is necessary to preserve the trust or carry out its purposes. The court modifies only as much as necessary; it cannot rewrite the settlor's beneficial plan.
*Apply: Determine whether [changed circumstances] were unforeseen by [settlor] at creation, whether deviation is [necessary to preserve the trust], and whether the court is changing [administrative terms only vs. beneficiary identities].*
> **Thus,** the court's deviation power is narrow — it adjusts how the trust is run, never who benefits from it.

### Termination of Irrevocable Trusts — Claflin Doctrine and Consent Rule 🔴
An irrevocable trust terminates (1) by its own terms on the date or event specified; (2) prematurely when the settlor and all beneficiaries consent, provided the termination does not frustrate a material purpose of the trust — the Claflin doctrine bars early termination even with unanimous consent if a material purpose (such as a spendthrift or support restriction) would be defeated; (3) when all beneficiaries consent and all material purposes of the trust have already been accomplished; or (4) by operation of the Statute of Uses, where the trustee holds legal title to real property but has no active duties to perform — a passive trust — causing legal title to vest automatically in the beneficiaries. Courts may also analogize the Statute of Uses to personal property when the trustee's duties are wholly ministerial.
*Apply: Determine whether [all beneficiaries including unborn contingent remaindermen] consent, whether any [material purpose — spendthrift / support / minority protection] remains unfulfilled under Claflin, and whether [passive trust / Statute of Uses] applies.*
> **Thus,** even unanimous beneficiary consent cannot override a material purpose of the trust; Claflin requires the court to refuse early termination in that event.
