## A. Basic Elements of Criminal Liability

### Actus Reus 🟢
A crime requires a voluntary, conscious bodily movement; an involuntary act — a reflex, convulsion, or bodily movement during unconsciousness — does not satisfy actus reus. Where the crime is defined by an omission, criminal liability attaches only when the defendant had a pre-existing legal duty to act, arising from statute, relationship, contract, voluntary assumption, or the defendant's own creation of the risk.
*Apply: Ask whether [defendant]'s conduct was a [voluntary act / omission under a legal duty], specifically [describe the movement or failure to act and any duty relationship].*
> **Thus,** the actus reus element is [satisfied / not satisfied].

### Mens Rea — Common-Law Levels 🟢
At common law, mental states are classified as specific intent (the defendant consciously desired to produce the prohibited result or cause a specific harm), malice (the defendant harbored actual ill will or reckless disregard of an obvious risk), general intent (the defendant was aware of, or should have been aware of, the circumstances constituting the offense), and strict liability (no culpable mental state is required, as where a regulatory statute omits any mens rea language and imposes liability for the public welfare).
*Apply: Determine whether [crime] requires specific intent, malice, general intent, or strict liability, then assess whether [defendant] possessed [the required state] when [doing the act].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [did / did not] have the required mens rea.

### Mens Rea — MPC Levels 🟡
The Model Penal Code recognizes four levels of culpability: a person acts *purposely* when it is his conscious object to engage in the conduct or cause the result; *knowingly* when he is practically certain the result will follow; *recklessly* when he consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk; and *negligently* when he should have been aware of a substantial risk that a reasonable person would not have disregarded. Each level is a floor — a statute requiring recklessness is also satisfied by purposeful or knowing conduct.
*Apply: Identify the MPC culpability level attached to [crime], then determine whether [defendant]'s conduct was [purposely / knowingly / recklessly / negligently] directed at [the result or circumstance].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] acted [at / below] the required MPC culpability level.

### Concurrence 🟢
Criminal liability requires that the required mental state exist at the same moment the voluntary act is performed — the mens rea must "concur" with the actus reus. A defendant who forms the required intent only after committing the act, or who acts innocently and later decides to profit from the result, lacks the required temporal concurrence.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] harbored [the required intent] at the time [the act occurred], or whether intent formed only [before / after] the critical moment.*
> **Thus,** concurrence [is / is not] established.

### Causation — Actual and Proximate 🟢
For result crimes, the defendant's act must be both the actual cause and the proximate cause of the harm. Actual causation is established by a but-for test: the result would not have occurred but for the defendant's conduct. Proximate causation asks whether the result was a foreseeable consequence of the act; an independent, unforeseeable intervening act by a third party — a superseding cause — breaks the causal chain. A dependent intervening act (one that flows naturally from the defendant's conduct) does not sever causation even if it was unexpected.
*Apply: Trace [defendant]'s act to [the result], asking whether [result] would have occurred but for [act], and whether [any intervening act by X] was foreseeable or independent.*
> **Thus,** [defendant]'s act [was / was not] the proximate cause of [the result].

## B. Homicide

### Murder — Malice Aforethought 🔴
Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. Malice is established in four ways: (1) express malice — an intent to kill; (2) intent to inflict great bodily harm; (3) depraved-heart malice — a conscious disregard of an extreme risk to human life, evidencing a wanton indifference to the value of human life; or (4) felony murder — the intent to commit an inherently dangerous felony that results in a death. Each form of malice is sufficient to support a murder conviction without proof of premeditation.
*Apply: Identify which form of malice [defendant] harbored — [intent to kill based on X / GBH intent / depraved-heart conduct / felony-murder predicate] — from the facts.*
> **Thus,** malice aforethought [is / is not] established, and [defendant] is [guilty of / cannot be convicted of] murder.

### First-Degree Murder — Premeditation and Deliberation 🟡
First-degree murder requires proof that the defendant not only intended to kill but also premeditated and deliberated. Premeditation means the defendant thought about and planned the killing in advance; deliberation means the decision was made with a calm, clear mind, weighing the killing. The premeditation need not be lengthy — a matter of seconds may suffice — but courts look to factors such as prior planning activity, motive, and the manner of the killing to assess whether the decision was truly reflective rather than impulsive. Killings committed during enumerated felonies (e.g., arson, rape, robbery, burglary, kidnapping) also constitute first-degree murder under the felony-murder rule.
*Apply: Look for evidence that [defendant] [planned the killing / chose a particular method / had motive] before acting, or that the death occurred during [an enumerated felony].*
> **Thus,** the killing [is / is not] first-degree murder.

### Second-Degree Murder 🟢
Second-degree murder is the default murder category — all murder that is not first-degree. It encompasses intentional killings that lack premeditation and deliberation, as well as depraved-heart killings and non-enumerated felony murders. A defendant who kills intentionally but on a sudden impulse, or who drives with extreme recklessness causing death, is typically guilty of second-degree murder.
*Apply: After ruling out premeditation or an enumerated felony, assess whether [defendant]'s killing was intentional but impulsive, or reflected depraved indifference, supporting second-degree murder.*
> **Thus,** the killing is second-degree murder.
> **CA divergence (statutory murder §§187–189):** Penal Code **§187** defines murder as the unlawful killing of a human being **or a fetus** with malice aforethought; **§188** defines express and implied malice (and, post-SB 1437, limits imputed malice); **§189** sets the degrees — first degree covers premeditated/deliberate killings, killing by destructive device, poison, lying in wait, or torture, the enumerated felonies, and **drive-by** shootings. CA also recognizes implied-malice **"Watson" murder** — second-degree murder where a driver (often a previously-warned DUI offender) kills while acting with conscious disregard for life. ⚠️ VERIFY current §§187–189.

### Felony Murder — Rule, Merger Doctrine, and Agency Limit 🔴
Under the felony-murder rule, a killing that occurs during the commission or attempted commission of an inherently dangerous felony is murder, even without independent proof of malice. Three limitations constrain the rule. First, the *merger doctrine* bars using a felony that is an integral part of the killing — typically assault — as the predicate, because such felonies "merge" into the homicide. Second, the *res gestae* requirement demands that the death occur during the perpetration of the felony, from the point of initiation through immediate flight. Third, under the *agency theory* (majority), only killings *by a co-felon* count — if a victim or police officer kills a bystander or co-felon in response to the felony, that death does not trigger felony murder.
*Apply: Identify the predicate felony, ask whether it [merges / is independent], whether death occurred [during the felony / during flight], and whether the killer was [a co-felon or a third party].*
> **CA divergence:** SB 1437 (Penal Code §§ 188–189) significantly narrowed CA felony murder for non-killers. A defendant who did not personally kill may be liable only if: (1) they were the actual killer; (2) they aided and abetted with intent to kill; or (3) they were a **major participant** in the felony who acted with **reckless indifference to human life** (the *Banks/Clark* standard). The natural-and-probable-consequences theory no longer supports murder in CA. ⚠️ VERIFY current §§ 188–189.
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of felony murder.

### Voluntary Manslaughter — Heat of Passion 🟢
Voluntary manslaughter is an intentional killing that would be murder but for adequate provocation that negates malice. Four elements must be met: (1) the defendant was actually provoked; (2) a reasonable person in the defendant's situation would have been provoked to lose self-control; (3) the defendant did not have a reasonable cooling-off period before acting; and (4) a reasonable person would not have cooled off in the time that elapsed. Classic adequate provocations include catching a spouse in the act of adultery, mutual combat, and serious assault; mere words alone generally are insufficient at common law, though some jurisdictions allow circumstance-specific exceptions.
*Apply: Identify [the provocative event], assess whether a reasonable person would have lost control given [facts], and determine whether any [time gap] constituted a cooling-off period.*
> **Thus,** the killing [qualifies / does not qualify] as voluntary manslaughter.

### Voluntary Manslaughter — Imperfect Self-Defense 🟡
Imperfect self-defense reduces a killing from murder to voluntary manslaughter when the defendant had an honest but unreasonable belief that deadly force was necessary to prevent imminent death or great bodily harm. This doctrine is recognized in California and many jurisdictions: the genuine (subjective) belief negates express malice, but the unreasonableness of the belief means the killing is still criminal. It does not apply where the defendant was the initial aggressor who provoked the confrontation.
*Apply: Ask whether [defendant] sincerely (though unreasonably) believed [he/she] faced imminent deadly force, and whether [defendant] started the confrontation.*
> **Thus,** the imperfect-self-defense doctrine [does / does not] reduce the charge to voluntary manslaughter.

### Involuntary Manslaughter — Criminal Negligence 🟡
Involuntary manslaughter is an unintentional killing that results from criminal negligence — conduct that represents a gross deviation from the standard of care a reasonable person would observe — or from an unlawful act that is not a felony (the misdemeanor-manslaughter rule). Criminal negligence is greater than civil negligence: it requires a substantial and unjustifiable risk of death or serious harm that the defendant consciously disregarded or should have recognized as unreasonable. Under the misdemeanor-manslaughter rule, any death that results from a malum in se misdemeanor (or, in some jurisdictions, any misdemeanor) is involuntary manslaughter.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant]'s conduct fell so far below the standard of care as to be criminally negligent — e.g., [describe conduct] — or whether a death resulted from [defendant]'s commission of [unlawful act].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] is [guilty / not guilty] of involuntary manslaughter.

## C. Crimes Against Persons

### Battery 🟢
Battery is the intentional or criminally negligent application of unlawful force to another person, causing bodily harm or an offensive touching. The force need not be great; even the slightest touching is sufficient if it is hostile or offensive. The defendant need not intend injury — only intend the act that constitutes the touching. At common law battery is a general-intent crime; transferred intent applies where the defendant intends to batter one person but strikes another.
*Apply: Ask whether [defendant] [intentionally / negligently] applied unlawful force to [victim], and whether the touching was [harmful / offensive].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of battery.

### Assault 🟢
At common law, assault is an attempted battery — an act with the specific intent to commit a battery that falls short of the physical touching. The majority of modern jurisdictions (and the MPC) define assault as intentionally placing another person in reasonable apprehension of an imminent harmful or offensive contact; present ability to carry out the threat is required. Words alone ordinarily do not constitute assault; there must be an overt act. Note the distinction: common-law assault requires specific intent to commit the battery, while the apprehension-based definition requires only that the apprehension be reasonable.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant]'s act [created reasonable apprehension of imminent contact in victim / constituted a substantial step toward battery], and whether [victim] was aware of the threatened contact.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of assault.

### Kidnapping 🟡
Kidnapping is the unlawful confinement or restraint of a person, combined with movement (asportation) of the victim from one place to another. Simple kidnapping requires only some movement — even slight at common law. Aggravated kidnapping — carrying harsher penalties — applies where the purpose is ransom, reward, facilitating another felony, or causing physical harm, or where the victim is a child. In California and many states, the asportation must be more than incidental to an underlying offense; substantial movement that increases the risk of harm or substantially isolates the victim from rescue is required for felony-murder or other enhancement purposes.
*Apply: Confirm [unlawful restraint plus movement of victim], and assess whether the purpose was [ransom / to facilitate [crime]] and whether movement was [merely incidental or substantial].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of kidnapping.

### False Imprisonment 🟢
False imprisonment is the unlawful confinement of a person within fixed boundaries without consent and without lawful authority, for any length of time. The confinement may be accomplished by physical force, threats, or fraud; there is no requirement of asportation. Unlike kidnapping, false imprisonment requires no movement of the victim. The tort and the crime share the same basic elements; criminal liability typically requires that the confinement be willful and unlawful.
*Apply: Ask whether [defendant] confined [victim] within [specific boundaries] by [force / threat / fraud] without [consent / lawful authority], regardless of the duration.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of false imprisonment.

### Rape (Sexual Assault) 🔴
At common law, rape is the unlawful sexual intercourse by a man with a woman not his wife, by force and against her will. Modern statutes eliminate the marital exception, expand the actus reus beyond vaginal intercourse, and shift the focus from force to the absence of consent. Consent obtained by fraud, intoxication, or threat may be vitiated. Statutory rape — sexual intercourse with a person below the age of consent — is a strict-liability offense; the defendant's reasonable belief as to the victim's age is generally not a defense at common law, though some modern statutes allow a reasonable-mistake-of-age defense.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] engaged in [sexual act] with [victim] by [force / threat / without consent], and whether consent was [freely given / obtained by fraud or coercion / impossible due to incapacity].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of rape/sexual assault.

## D. Property Crimes

### Larceny 🟢
Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of another person's personal property with the intent to permanently deprive the owner of it at the time of taking. Each element is essential: the taking must be trespassory (without consent or by exceeding the scope of consent), there must be asportation however slight, the property must belong to another (even temporary possession counts), and the intent to permanently deprive must exist at the moment of taking — a later-formed intent does not retroactively create larceny. Larceny is a specific-intent crime.
*Apply: Trace [defendant]'s conduct through each element — [trespassory taking at X time], [carrying away however slightly], [property owned by victim], [permanent-deprivation intent at moment of taking].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of larceny.

### Larceny by Trick 🟡
Larceny by trick occurs when the defendant obtains mere possession — not title — of another's property through a false representation of fact. The distinction from false pretenses is critical: in larceny by trick the victim retains legal title and hands over only possession, whereas in false pretenses the victim is induced to transfer title itself. Because larceny by trick is a form of larceny, it carries the same elements: trespassory taking (obtained by fraud), asportation, property of another, and intent to permanently deprive at the time of taking.
*Apply: Ask whether [defendant] obtained only [possession] through [misrepresentation], leaving [victim] with legal title — distinguishing this from a false-pretenses scenario where title passed.*
> **Thus,** the offense is larceny by trick, not false pretenses.

### False Pretenses 🟡
False pretenses is obtaining **title** to another's property by a knowing and intentional false representation of a material, existing fact, with intent to defraud. Three distinctions from larceny by trick: (1) the victim must transfer title, not mere possession; (2) the false representation must be of a past or present fact — a promise of future conduct alone is insufficient; (3) the victim must actually rely on the misrepresentation. If the defendant lies about a past fact but the victim does not rely on it (e.g., would have transferred title anyway), the element fails.
*Apply: Confirm [defendant] made a [false statement of past/present fact], that [victim] actually relied on it and transferred [title] — not mere possession — to [defendant].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of false pretenses.

### Embezzlement 🟢
Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of property by a person who has lawful possession of it — typically an employee, trustee, or bailee — with intent to defraud the owner. The critical element distinguishing embezzlement from larceny is that the defendant receives the property lawfully and then misappropriates it; in larceny, the initial taking is trespassory. The conversion need not involve appropriation of the entire value — any serious interference with the owner's rights is sufficient. Embezzlement is a statutory offense that emerged to fill the gap left when servants and agents were excluded from larceny's common-law scope.
*Apply: Identify whether [defendant] came into [possession] of [property] lawfully — through [employment / trust / bailment] — and then [converted / appropriated] it without authorization.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of embezzlement.
> **CA divergence — theft consolidation (§484/§487):** California **§484** folds larceny, larceny by trick, embezzlement, and false pretenses into a single crime of **"theft,"** which may be pleaded generically — though the prosecution must still prove the elements of **at least one** theory. Value sets the grade: **grand theft** generally above **$950** (**§487**), petty theft below; **Prop 47 (§490.2)** makes most sub-$950 thefts misdemeanor petty theft regardless of the method of taking. ⚠️ VERIFY current thresholds.

### Robbery 🔴
Robbery is larceny aggravated by force or intimidation directed at the person or presence of the victim: it is the taking of personal property from another's person or immediate presence, by force or by putting the victim in fear of imminent bodily harm, with intent to permanently deprive. The force or intimidation must be the means of accomplishing the taking — force used after the taking is complete does not convert a theft to robbery unless it is used to retain the property or escape. Robbery is a specific-intent crime; under Pinkerton, co-conspirators who agreed to the forcible taking are liable for the robbery.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] took [property] from [victim]'s person or presence, using [force / threat of immediate harm], at the time of or immediately preceding the taking.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of robbery.
> **CA divergence (§211):** CA robbery is the felonious taking of property from the person or immediate presence of another, against his will, by **force or fear**. Under **People v. Estes**, force or fear used to **retain** the loot or **escape** before reaching a place of temporary safety converts a theft (e.g., shoplifting) into robbery ("Estes robbery"). Under **People v. Tufunga**, a good-faith **claim of right to specific property** negates the felonious-taking element (not available to collect a debt). Degrees under §212.5 (inhabited dwelling, ATM user, transit driver = first degree). ⚠️ VERIFY.

### Burglary 🔴
At common law, burglary is the breaking and entering of the dwelling house of another at nighttime with the specific intent to commit a felony therein. All six elements are jurisdictionally tested: (1) breaking (actual — using force to open, or constructive — gaining entry by fraud or threat); (2) entering (any portion of the defendant's body or an instrument used to commit the felony crosses the threshold); (3) dwelling house; (4) of another; (5) at nighttime; (6) intent to commit a felony at the time of entry. Modern statutes typically eliminate the nighttime requirement, extend coverage to any building, and reduce the breaking requirement.
*Apply: Walk through each element: [breaking by X], [entry of any part], [dwelling / building of another], [nighttime if CL], [felonious intent at moment of entry — not formed later].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of common-law / statutory burglary.
> **CA divergence (§459/§460):** CA burglary requires **no breaking, no nighttime, and no dwelling** — it is **entry** into any building, room, locked vehicle, etc. with intent to commit larceny or **any** felony, the intent existing **at entry**. **§460** grades burglary of an **inhabited dwelling** as first degree; all else is second degree. **Prop 47 (§459.5)** reclassifies **"shoplifting"** — entering an **open** business during regular hours intending to steal ≤ $950 — as a misdemeanor rather than burglary. ⚠️ VERIFY.

### Arson 🟢
At common law, arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling house of another. "Burning" requires at least a charring of the wood fibers — a mere blackening or scorching does not suffice. "Malicious" means the defendant intentionally or recklessly caused the burning; it does not require spite or ill will. Like burglary, modern statutes expand the offense to cover any structure, the defendant's own property (especially with insurance fraud intent), and personal property. Under the common-law definition, a person cannot commit arson of their own home.
*Apply: Confirm [charring or structural damage] to [a dwelling / structure], caused [intentionally or recklessly] by [defendant], and assess whether the property was [defendant's own or another's].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of arson.
> **CA divergence (§451):** Under CA Penal Code **§451**, arson reaches one who **willfully and maliciously** sets fire to or burns **any** structure, forest land, or property — and, unlike the common law, a defendant **can** be guilty of burning **his own property** where it is done to defraud or where the fire injures another person or another's property. **§452** separately punishes **reckless** burning. ⚠️ VERIFY.

### Receiving Stolen Property 🟢
Receiving stolen property is the knowing acquisition of property that has been stolen, with knowledge that it is stolen and with intent to permanently deprive the rightful owner of it. The critical element is knowledge — the defendant must actually know (or, in some jurisdictions, believe to a near-certainty) that the property is stolen at the time of receipt; mere suspicion is insufficient. Possession need not be actual; constructive possession through an agent is sufficient. The crime is distinct from larceny because the defendant receives property already stolen by another; the two are not the same transaction.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] received [property] knowing at the time of receipt that it had been stolen — look for [suspicious circumstances, low price, prior dealings] as circumstantial proof of knowledge.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of receiving stolen property.

### Forgery 🟢
Forgery is the making or altering of a writing with apparent legal significance so that it is false, with the intent to defraud. The writing must be one that, if genuine, would have legal effect (a contract, deed, or check — not, for example, a painting). Uttering forgery is the separate offense of offering a forged instrument as genuine with intent to defraud.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] made or altered a legally significant writing to be false with intent to defraud.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of forgery.

## E. Inchoate Crimes

### Attempt 🔴
Criminal attempt requires (1) the specific intent to commit the target crime and (2) a significant, overt step toward its commission beyond mere preparation. Courts use various proximity tests — the common law "dangerous proximity" test asks how close the defendant came to completing the crime; the MPC "substantial step" test asks whether the act strongly corroborated criminal purpose. Mere preparation (buying supplies, casing the target) is generally insufficient; crossing into the zone of perpetration is required. Attempt merges into the completed offense at common law — the defendant cannot be convicted of both.
*Apply: Identify the specific intent for [target crime] and the act [defendant] took, then apply the [CL proximity / MPC substantial step] test — was the act [mere preparation or execution-level conduct]?*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [has / has not] committed criminal attempt.

### Attempt — Impossibility and Abandonment 🟡
Factual impossibility is no defense to attempt — that the crime could not be completed due to facts unknown to the defendant (an empty pocket, an unloaded gun) does not excuse, because the defendant had the intent and took a substantial step. Legal impossibility — that what the defendant set out to do is not in fact a crime — is a defense. Abandonment is generally not a defense once the defendant has the requisite intent and has taken a substantial step, even if she changes her mind before completion (though the MPC recognizes a voluntary and complete renunciation).
*Apply: Classify the impossibility as factual (no defense) or legal (a defense), and test any abandonment against the substantial-step line.*
> **Thus,** the [impossibility / abandonment] [does / does not] excuse the attempt.

### Solicitation 🟢
Solicitation is the intentional enticement, encouragement, or commanding of another person to commit a crime, with the specific intent that the crime be committed. The offense is complete at the moment of the request — it is irrelevant whether the solicitee agrees to commit the crime or ever acts. If the solicitee does agree, a conspiracy may be formed at that moment. Solicitation and the completed crime do not merge at common law — some jurisdictions allow conviction for both solicitation and the target offense where the scheme succeeds, though others merge the solicitation into the attempt or completed crime.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] [requested / encouraged / commanded] [person X] to commit [crime], with intent that the crime be carried out — the offense is complete regardless of X's response.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of solicitation.

### Conspiracy — Agreement, Intent, and Overt Act 🔴
Conspiracy is an agreement between two or more persons to accomplish an unlawful objective, combined with the specific intent to agree and the specific intent to achieve the unlawful goal. At common law no overt act beyond the agreement was required; most modern statutes (and the MPC) require at least one overt act by any conspirator in furtherance of the agreement. Conspiracy does not merge with the completed offense — a defendant may be convicted of both conspiracy and the substantive crime. Each co-conspirator may be liable for all crimes committed by any co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy that were reasonably foreseeable (the *Pinkerton* doctrine).
*Apply: Find [the agreement between [D] and [X]], evidence of [specific intent to achieve the unlawful goal], any [overt act in furtherance], and trace [Pinkerton liability for co-conspirator acts].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] guilty of conspiracy, and [is / is not] vicariously liable under Pinkerton for [the co-conspirator's crime].

### Conspiracy — Withdrawal and Scope 🟡
A conspirator may withdraw from a conspiracy by communicating an unequivocal disavowal to all co-conspirators and, in some jurisdictions, taking affirmative steps to defeat the conspiracy's objectives. Withdrawal does not retroactively eliminate liability for crimes committed before withdrawal, but it may cut off liability for subsequent crimes committed by remaining conspirators. The scope of a conspiracy may be bilateral (all-or-nothing agreement among all parties) or unilateral (MPC approach — one person believes he is conspiring even if the other is a government agent or secretly plans not to follow through). Multiple conspiracies versus a single conspiracy is assessed by examining whether there was one common purpose or multiple independent agreements.
*Apply: Determine whether [defendant] effectively withdrew by [communicating to co-conspirators / taking affirmative steps] and whether withdrawal preceded the [subsequent crimes] at issue.*
> **Thus,** [defendant]'s withdrawal [is / is not] effective, and [does / does not] cut off Pinkerton liability for [subsequent crime].

## F. Accomplice Liability

### Accomplice Liability — Aid, Abet, and Intent 🔴
An accomplice is one who, with the intent to facilitate the commission of a crime, aids, abets, counsels, or encourages the principal before or during the offense. Two intents are required: the intent to assist (the accomplice must know what the principal intends) and the intent that the target crime be committed (mere knowledge that a crime will occur is insufficient — the accomplice must desire or consciously promote the criminal objective). An accomplice is liable for the target crime to the same extent as the principal, plus for all other offenses that are the natural and probable consequences of the agreed criminal scheme.
*Apply: Establish [defendant]'s acts of [assistance — describe], the knowledge of [principal]'s plan, and the intent to [promote / facilitate] the specific crime, then trace NPC liability for [other crimes that followed].*
> **Thus,** [defendant] is guilty as an accomplice to [target crime and any NPC offenses].

### Accomplice Liability — Natural and Probable Consequences 🟡
Under the natural-and-probable-consequences doctrine, an accomplice is liable not only for the crime he intended to facilitate but also for any other crime that a reasonable person in his position would have foreseen as a natural and probable result of the agreed criminal enterprise. The test is objective — courts ask what a reasonable person would have foreseen, not what this defendant actually foresaw. The doctrine is controversial: the MPC rejects it for murder-level liability, and California's SB 1437 eliminated it as a basis for murder convictions, though it survives for lesser offenses.
*Apply: From the agreed crime of [X], assess whether a reasonable person would have foreseen [the additional crime Y] as a probable consequence — note if [Y is murder in CA, where NPC is abolished for murder].*
> **CA divergence:** SB 1437 eliminates NPC as a basis for murder in CA. ⚠️ VERIFY.
> **Thus,** [defendant] [is / is not] vicariously liable for [the additional crime] under NPC.

### Principals and Accessories After the Fact 🟢
At common law, parties to crime were classified as principals in the first degree (the perpetrator), principals in the second degree (aiders present at the scene), accessories before the fact (those who aided in advance but were absent at the scene), and accessories after the fact (those who helped the principal escape or avoid detection after the crime was completed). Modern statutes typically abolish these distinctions for pre-crime assistance, treating all accomplices as principals. An accessory after the fact, however, remains separately punishable for the lesser offense of harboring, aiding, or assisting a felon after the crime — typically requiring knowledge that a felony was committed.
*Apply: Classify [defendant]'s conduct as [assistance before the crime / presence at the scene / help after the crime], and determine whether [defendant] knew a felony had been committed at the time of assistance.*
> **Thus,** [defendant] is a [principal / accessory after the fact], liable for [the underlying offense / the separate harboring/obstructing offense].

## G. Defenses

### Self-Defense — Non-Deadly and Deadly Force 🔴
A person may use non-deadly force in self-defense when she reasonably believes such force is necessary to repel an imminent, unlawful attack; no duty to retreat exists for non-deadly force. Deadly force in self-defense is justified only when the defendant reasonably believes she is facing an imminent threat of death or serious bodily harm and has not been the initial aggressor. A number of jurisdictions impose a duty to retreat before using deadly force if retreat is safely available (except within the home — the "castle doctrine"); other jurisdictions allow "stand your ground." The initial aggressor who provokes a confrontation loses the right to self-defense unless she clearly withdraws and the other party continues the attack.
*Apply: Assess whether [defendant] faced [imminent threat of death or GBH], whether [non-deadly / deadly] force was proportionate, whether [defendant] was the initial aggressor, and whether retreat was required and available.*

### Defense of Others 🟢
A person may use force to defend a third party on the same terms as self-defense — the defender must reasonably believe the third party is in imminent danger of unlawful force and that the degree of force used is necessary. At common law, the "alter ego" rule limited the defense: the intervenor "stepped into the shoes" of the person defended and could use force only to the extent that person could; if the third party had no right to self-defense (e.g., was the aggressor), the intervenor had none either. ### Defense of Property 🟢
One may use reasonable, non-deadly force to protect property from unlawful interference or theft. Under the M'Naghten test, a defendant is not criminally responsible if, at the time of the act, he was suffering from a disease of the mind that caused him either (1) not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing, or (2) if he knew it, not to know that what he was doing was wrong. "Wrong" means legally wrong in the majority view (would a reasonable person understand it was prohibited by law?). M'Naghten is a purely cognitive test — inability to control one's behavior is irrelevant. California applies the M'Naghten test.

The irresistible impulse test supplements M'Naghten in some jurisdictions by excusing a defendant who, although knowing his act was wrong, was driven by an uncontrollable impulse produced by mental disease that he could not resist. The test focuses on volitional incapacity — the inability to conform one's conduct to the requirements of law — rather than cognitive awareness. It is narrower than the MPC test; not every impulse qualifies — the disease must render control literally impossible.

### Voluntary Intoxication 🟡
Voluntary intoxication is not a complete defense to crime. At common law, it may negate a specific-intent element — if the defendant was so intoxicated that he could not form the required specific intent, the prosecution cannot prove that element, and the defendant may be convicted only of a lesser general-intent offense. Voluntary intoxication is never a defense to crimes requiring only general intent, recklessness, or strict liability. It does not negate malice in murder — the law imputes the requisite reckless indifference to those who voluntarily become intoxicated and kill.

Involuntary intoxication — caused by coercion, fraud, mistake, or an unexpected drug reaction — is treated like insanity and may be a complete defense to any crime. The defendant must show that the intoxication was truly involuntary (not merely excessive voluntary drinking) and that it produced a state equivalent to legal insanity under the applicable test: either the defendant did not know the nature of the act, did not know it was wrong, or could not conform conduct to law. ### Mistake of Fact 🟢
A mistake of fact negates criminal liability when it negates the mental state required for the crime. For general-intent crimes, only a reasonable mistake of fact is a defense. For strict-liability offenses, mistake of fact is no defense at all, because no mens rea is required. A mistake about a legal conclusion (mistake of law) is generally not a defense, except where the defendant reasonably relied on an official statement of law that was later determined to be incorrect.

### Duress 🟡
Duress is a defense when the defendant committed a crime because of a well-grounded, immediate threat of death or serious bodily harm from another person that a person of reasonable firmness could not have resisted. The threat must be imminent — not future — and must be directed at the defendant or a close family member. Duress is generally not available as a defense to murder, on the theory that one may not kill an innocent third party to save oneself. The defendant must not have recklessly or negligently placed himself in a situation where duress was foreseeable.
*Apply: Confirm [an immediate threat of death or GBH by X], that it was [directed at defendant or close relation], that a [reasonable person] could not have resisted, and that [defendant] did not place himself voluntarily in the coercive situation — note unavailability if [crime is murder].*

### Necessity 🟡
Necessity (or "choice of evils") is a defense when the defendant committed a criminal act to avoid a greater harm, provided: (1) the harm avoided was greater than the harm caused; (2) the harm was imminent; (3) no adequate legal alternative existed; (4) the defendant did not create the emergency; and (5) the harm avoided was not caused by another person (which would be duress, not necessity). ### Entrapment 🟡
Entrapment is a defense when government agents induced or persuaded a defendant who was not predisposed to commit the crime to do so. The **subjective test** (federal and majority of states) focuses on the defendant's own predisposition: if the defendant was ready and willing to commit the crime before government inducement, the defense fails regardless of how elaborate the government's scheme was. The **objective test** (minority, MPC) focuses on government conduct: the defense is available if the government's methods would have induced a hypothetically law-abiding person, regardless of this defendant's predisposition.
*Apply: Under the subjective test, ask whether [defendant] had a prior disposition to commit [crime] before [the government agent's inducement]; under the objective test, ask whether the government's conduct was [sufficiently outrageous to induce a reasonable law-abiding person].*

## H. Fourth Amendment — Search and Seizure

### Arrest and Detentions 🟡
An arrest — taking a person into custody for prosecution or interrogation — must be supported by probable cause, meaning trustworthy facts sufficient for a reasonable person to believe the suspect committed a crime. No warrant is needed to arrest in a public place, but a non-emergency arrest in the suspect's own home requires an arrest warrant. A brief investigatory (Terry) stop requires only reasonable suspicion, while compelling a suspect to the stationhouse for questioning or fingerprinting requires probable cause. Police may set up checkpoints or roadblocks without individualized suspicion only if cars are stopped on a neutral, articulable standard serving a purpose closely tied to vehicles.

Certain searches are reasonable without a warrant or individualized suspicion where special governmental needs beyond ordinary law enforcement apply: searches at the international border, administrative inspections of closely regulated industries, drug testing of public-school students in extracurricular activities, and inventory searches. All wiretapping and electronic eavesdropping require a warrant, except that a person assumes the risk that someone with whom he speaks is consenting to government monitoring or is wired.
*Apply: Identify the special-needs context and confirm the search fits a recognized warrantless category.*

### Government Action Threshold 🟢
The Fourth Amendment applies only to governmental — that is, state or federal — action. A private citizen's search, even if identical in nature to a police search, does not implicate the Fourth Amendment unless the private party acted as an instrument or agent of the government (e.g., at police direction or with police knowledge and acquiescence). Once a private party turns evidence over to the government, the government cannot constitutionally "replay" a search that exceeded what the private party did.

### Reasonable Expectation of Privacy — Standing 🔴
A defendant may challenge a search only if she had a personal, reasonable expectation of privacy (REP) in the place or thing searched. REP is assessed under a two-part test: the defendant must have (1) a subjective expectation of privacy and (2) an expectation that society recognizes as objectively reasonable. A person lacks REP in items or communications voluntarily shared with a third party (the third-party doctrine), in items in open fields, in the exterior of a car, or in contraband. A person has REP in her home, hotel room, the contents of sealed mail, and digital data (notably, cell phone contents after *Riley* and cell-site location information after *Carpenter*).

### Warrant Requirement — Probable Cause and Particularity 🟢
A valid search or arrest warrant requires: (1) issuance by a neutral and detached magistrate; (2) probable cause — a fair probability or substantial chance that evidence of a crime will be found in the place to be searched (for a search warrant) or that the person committed a crime (for an arrest warrant); and (3) particularity — the warrant must describe with reasonable specificity the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. A warrant that fails particularity is facially invalid; evidence obtained by executing it may be suppressed unless the good-faith exception applies. Probable cause may be established by informant tips evaluated under the *Gates* totality-of-the-circumstances test.

### Search Incident to Arrest (SITA) 🔴
A warrantless search is permissible as incident to a lawful custodial arrest. The permissible scope covers the arrestee's person (including containers on the person) and the area within the arrestee's immediate control at the time of arrest — the area from which the arrestee could grab a weapon or destroy evidence. For vehicle searches incident to arrest, *Gant* limits the exception: officers may search the passenger compartment and any containers therein only if the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of the vehicle at the time of the search, or if it is reasonable to believe evidence of the arrest offense will be found in the vehicle.

The automobile exception permits a warrantless search of a vehicle — including all containers within it — whenever officers have probable cause to believe the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime. The justification rests on the vehicle's inherent mobility and the reduced expectation of privacy in automobiles. Probable cause attaches to the entire vehicle and any container that might reasonably contain the evidence; if probable cause is limited to a specific container, only that container may be searched. ### Plain View 🟢
Officers who are lawfully in a position may seize an item in plain view without a warrant if (1) the officer is lawfully present at the location, (2) the incriminating nature of the item is immediately apparent (the officer must have probable cause to believe it is contraband or evidence), and (3) the officer has lawful access to the item. The plain-view doctrine does not justify the initial intrusion — the officer must already have a legal right to be where he is. Moving or manipulating an item to reveal its incriminating character (before probable cause) goes beyond plain view.

### Consent 🟢
A warrantless search is reasonable if the person with authority voluntarily consented. Consent must be freely and voluntarily given — it cannot be the product of duress, coercion, or submission to a claim of authority. The government bears the burden of proving voluntariness by a totality-of-the-circumstances analysis. Third-party consent is valid if the third party has actual authority (co-occupant, shared access) or apparent authority (officers reasonably believed the person had authority). A frisk does not justify searching for contraband — the officer may seize only items whose incriminating nature is immediately apparent through touch (the "plain feel" doctrine). ### Exigent Circumstances 🟡
Exigent circumstances permit a warrantless entry or search where obtaining a warrant is impracticable because of (1) hot pursuit of a fleeing felon into private premises; (2) a threat that evidence will be imminently destroyed; or (3) an emergency-aid exception where officers have a reasonable belief that a person inside is in need of immediate assistance. ### Inventory Search 🟢
Police may conduct a warrantless inventory search of a lawfully impounded vehicle (or arrestee's property) pursuant to standardized departmental procedures, without a warrant or individualized probable cause. The search must follow established, routine procedures — it may not be used as a pretext for a criminal investigation. Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth, Fifth, or Sixth Amendments is inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief under the exclusionary rule. ## I. Fifth Amendment

### Miranda — Custody and Interrogation 🔴
Before subjecting a suspect to custodial interrogation, law-enforcement officers must administer the Miranda warnings: the right to remain silent, that anything said may be used against the suspect in court, the right to have counsel present, and the right to appointed counsel if the suspect cannot afford one. A suspect may waive Miranda rights if the waiver is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary — made with full awareness of the right and the consequences of abandoning it, free of coercion. ## J. Sixth Amendment

### Right to Counsel — Attachment and Scope 🔴
The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches at the initiation of formal adversary judicial proceedings — indictment, information, arraignment, or preliminary hearing — whichever occurs first.
