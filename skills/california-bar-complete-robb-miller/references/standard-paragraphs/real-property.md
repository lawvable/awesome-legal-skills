## A. Present Possessory Estates

### Fee Simple Absolute 🟢
A fee simple absolute is the largest estate recognized at law, conferring complete and perpetual ownership on the holder. It is freely alienable during life, descendible at death, and devisable by will. No future interest accompanies it in any other party, and no condition can divest it. Words of limitation "and his heirs" are not required under modern law; a conveyance "to A" presumptively creates a fee simple absolute.
*Apply: [Grantor] conveyed "to [Grantee]" with no limiting language, so [Grantee] holds a fee simple absolute—absolute ownership with no accompanying future interest.*
> **CA divergence:** None material; CA follows the common-law presumption favoring fee simple absolute.

---

### Fee Simple Determinable 🔴
A fee simple determinable is a defeasible fee created when the grantor uses clear durational language tying continued ownership to a stated condition (classic signal words: "so long as," "during," "until," or "while"). If the condition is ever violated, the estate terminates automatically by operation of law—no further act by the grantor is required. The accompanying future interest in the grantor is the **possibility of reverter**, which arises the instant the durational language is used. The estate remains freely alienable, descendible, and devisable, but always subject to the stated condition. Mere words of motive, desire, or intention do not create a defeasible fee.
*Apply: The deed conveyed Blackacre "to [Grantee] so long as [condition]," which is classic durational language; if [Grantee] violates [condition], the estate automatically reverts to [Grantor] via the possibility of reverter.*
> **CA divergence:** CA recognizes the fee simple determinable but restricts possibilities of reverter and rights of entry to 30 years under Civil Code § 885.020 unless re-recorded; exam questions typically avoid this wrinkle but flag if facts show old deed.

---

### Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent 🔴
A fee simple subject to a condition subsequent arises when the grantor uses clear conditional language plus expressly reserves the right to re-enter and reclaim the estate (signal words: "but if," "provided however that," "on condition that," followed by language reserving reentry). Unlike the determinable fee, the estate does **not** end automatically; it continues until the grantor elects to exercise the **right of entry** (power of termination). Failure to timely exercise that right may result in waiver or, under some statutes, extinguishment.
*Apply: The deed conveyed to [Grantee] "but if [condition occurs], Grantor reserves the right to re-enter"; the estate is not automatically forfeited—[Grantor] must affirmatively exercise the right of entry to cut off [Grantee]'s estate.*

---

### Fee Simple Subject to Executory Limitation 🟡
A fee simple subject to an executory limitation resembles a fee simple determinable in that the estate automatically divests if the stated condition is breached—but here, upon breach, title shifts not back to the grantor but forward to a **third party** identified in the grant. The third party's future interest is a **shifting executory interest**. Because the interest shifts to a non-grantor, it is subject to the Rule Against Perpetuities.
*Apply: The grant to [Grantee] provides that if [condition], title passes automatically to [Third Party], whose shifting executory interest will be tested under the RAP.*

---

### Life Estate 🔴
A life estate grants ownership measured by a human life, either the holder's own ("to A for life") or another's ("to A for the life of B"—a life estate pur autre vie). The life tenant is entitled to all ordinary uses, rents, and profits from the property during the measuring life. A future interest accompanies every life estate: if the grantor retains it, the future interest is a **reversion**; if given to a third party, it is a **remainder**. The estate cannot be measured in years only—a conveyance "to A for 99 years" creates a term of years, not a life estate.
*Apply: [Grantor] conveyed "to [A] for life, then to [B]"; [A] holds a life estate, [B] holds a remainder, and [A] is entitled to all ordinary use and profits but must not commit waste.*

---

### Waste — Voluntary (Affirmative) 🔴
Voluntary waste consists of overt, affirmative conduct by the life tenant or tenant for years that causes a permanent decrease in the property's value. Classic examples include demolishing structures, strip-mining, or clear-cutting timber where prior use did not involve such exploitation. The life tenant may consume or exploit natural resources only under recognized exceptions: **PURGE** (Prior use of the land for that purpose; Use reasonably necessary for Repair or maintenance; Grant authorizing exploitation; Exploitation already commenced by prior owner).
*Apply: [Life tenant]'s act of [conduct] was an overt physical act that diminished property value without falling within the PURGE exceptions, constituting voluntary waste.*

---

### Waste — Permissive 🟢
Permissive waste occurs when the life tenant neglects the property, allowing it to fall into disrepair. The life tenant owes a duty to make ordinary repairs sufficient to maintain the premises in reasonably good condition, and a duty to pay all ordinary carrying costs—taxes and mortgage interest—out of income generated by the property (but only to the extent income is received). The life tenant is not obligated to make extraordinary or capital improvements.
*Apply: [Life tenant]'s failure to [repair/pay taxes] allowed the premises to deteriorate, and [life tenant] had income sufficient to fund ordinary maintenance, establishing permissive waste.*

---

### Waste — Ameliorative 🟡
Ameliorative waste consists of alterations by the life tenant that actually increase the market value of the property but nonetheless change its character. At common law, even value-enhancing changes were impermissible without the consent of all future interest holders. The modern approach allows ameliorative changes where the character of the neighborhood has changed and the alteration better adapts the property to its current surroundings—but only if all future interest holders are known and ascertained and all consent.
*Apply: [Life tenant]'s renovation increased property value but materially changed its character; unless all future interest holders—[remaindermen]—consented, this constitutes ameliorative waste.*

## B. Future Interests

### Reversion 🟢
A reversion is a future interest retained by the grantor whenever the grantor conveys a lesser estate than the grantor holds and does not provide for an alternative taker to hold the estate after the lesser estate ends. It arises automatically by operation of law—no express language is required. A reversion is always vested, so it is not subject to the Rule Against Perpetuities, and it is freely transferable.
*Apply: When [Grantor] conveyed "to [A] for life" without designating a remainderman, [Grantor] retained a reversion that becomes possessory upon [A]'s death.*

---

### Possibility of Reverter 🟢
A possibility of reverter is the future interest in the grantor that accompanies a fee simple determinable. It takes effect automatically—without any further action by the grantor—the moment the durational condition is violated. Unlike a right of entry, it does not require the grantor to elect to terminate the estate.
*Apply: Because [Grantor] used durational language, [Grantor] holds a possibility of reverter; the moment [condition] occurs, title returns to [Grantor] without any further act.*

---

### Right of Entry (Power of Termination) 🟢
A right of entry is the future interest retained by the grantor when a fee simple subject to condition subsequent is created. Unlike the possibility of reverter, the right of entry does not vest automatically on breach of the condition; the grantor must affirmatively elect to exercise it. Failure to timely exercise may constitute waiver, and in some jurisdictions statutes extinguish stale rights of entry.
*Apply: [Grantor] retained a right of entry; upon [condition occurring], [Grantor] must elect to re-enter and terminate [Grantee]'s estate—the breach alone does not automatically end the estate.*

---

### Vested Remainder — Indefeasibly Vested 🟢
A remainder is a future interest in a third party that is capable of becoming possessory at the natural expiration of a prior estate of known, fixed duration (typically a life estate or term of years). An indefeasibly vested remainder is created in an identified, ascertained person and is subject to no conditions precedent or subsequent; it will certainly become possessory at the end of the prior estate and cannot be divested. Example: "to A for life, then to B"—B's remainder is indefeasibly vested.
*Apply: [B]'s remainder was created in an identified person with no attached conditions, making it indefeasibly vested; it will become possessory on [A]'s death and is not subject to RAP.*

---

### Vested Remainder Subject to Divestment (Complete Defeasance) 🟡
A vested remainder subject to complete defeasance (also called subject to total divestment) is created in an ascertained person with no condition precedent, but a condition subsequent could cut off the remainder before it becomes possessory. Example: "to A for life, then to B, but if B dies before age 25, to C." B has a vested remainder, but B's right to possession can be defeated if B dies before 25.
*Apply: [B] holds a vested remainder subject to complete defeasance because no condition precedes the grant to [B], but the condition of [dying under 25] is a subsequent condition that could cut off [B]'s interest.*

---

### Vested Remainder Subject to Open (Class Gift) 🔴
A vested remainder subject to open arises when a remainder is given to a class (e.g., "children") and at least one class member is ascertained and qualified to take, but additional members may join the class, diluting each existing member's share. The class closes under the **rule of convenience** when any member can demand distribution—typically when the preceding estate ends. Unborn class members who are not yet in gestation at that time are excluded.
*Apply: "To A for life, then to A's children": at least one child exists, so the remainder is vested in that child subject to open; each additional child born before the class closes will share in the remainder.*
> **CA divergence:** CA follows the rule of convenience for class closing.

---

### Contingent Remainder 🔴
A contingent remainder is a future interest in a third party that is either (a) created in an unascertained person or (b) subject to a condition precedent—something that must occur before the interest can become possessory—or both. Because the remainder may never vest, the grantor retains a reversion as a safety net. A contingent remainder is subject to the Rule Against Perpetuities. At common law, a contingent remainder was destroyed if it remained contingent when the preceding estate terminated (Destructibility Rule); modern law (followed by most states including CA) abolishes this rule and instead converts the preceding estate into a springing executory interest until the condition is satisfied.
*Apply: "To A for life, then to B if B graduates law school": B's remainder is contingent (subject to a condition precedent); if B has not yet graduated when A dies, under modern law the interest survives as a springing executory interest.*

---

### Executory Interests — Shifting and Springing 🔴
An executory interest is a future interest in a third party that takes effect by cutting short some prior interest before its natural termination. A **shifting executory interest** cuts short another grantee's interest (e.g., "to A, but if B returns from abroad, to B"). A **springing executory interest** cuts short the grantor's own interest (e.g., "to A when A passes the bar"—the estate springs from the grantor to A upon occurrence of the condition). Both types are subject to the Rule Against Perpetuities.
*Apply: The grant to [A] "but if [condition], to [B]" creates a shifting executory interest in [B] that divests [A]'s fee; the RAP must be satisfied by establishing [B]'s interest will vest or fail within 21 years of a life in being.*

---

### Rule of Destructibility of Contingent Remainders 🟡
At common law, a contingent remainder was automatically destroyed if it remained contingent at the moment the preceding freehold estate terminated (e.g., the life tenant died before the condition precedent was satisfied). The contingent remainder simply ceased to exist, and the grantor's reversion became possessory. The **modern rule**, followed by California and most states, abolishes the Destructibility Rule: a contingent remainder that fails to vest in time does not perish—instead, the grantor holds possession subject to a springing executory interest until the condition is or is not met.
*Apply: [Life tenant A] died before [B] graduated; at common law, [B]'s contingent remainder was destroyed and [O] retook; under modern CA law, the interest survives as a springing executory interest and [B]'s interest vests if and when [B] satisfies the condition.*
> **CA divergence:** CA abolishes the Destructibility Rule by statute; contingent remainders that outlast the preceding estate convert to springing executory interests.

---

### Rule in Shelley's Case 🟡
Under the Rule in Shelley's Case, if a freehold estate is created in the same instrument that also creates a remainder in that grantee's heirs (or heirs of her body), and both estates are of the same quality (both legal or both equitable), the remainder in the heirs merges into the freehold estate in the grantee, giving the grantee a fee simple absolute. The classic formulation: "to A for life, then to A's heirs" — the rule collapses the life estate and contingent remainder into a fee simple in A. **The modern rule** (followed by most states including CA) abolishes Shelley's Case: under modern law, A would hold only a life estate and A's heirs would hold a contingent remainder.
*Apply: O conveyed "to A for life, then to A's heirs"; at common law (Shelley's Case jurisdictions), A holds a fee simple absolute by merger; under CA modern law, A holds a life estate and A's heirs hold a contingent remainder.*
> **CA divergence:** CA has abolished the Rule in Shelley's Case; the grantor's actual intent controls, and "to A's heirs" is treated as a contingent remainder in A's unidentified heirs.

---

### Doctrine of Worthier Title 🟡
The Doctrine of Worthier Title provides that a grantor cannot create a future interest in her own heirs: if a living grantor conveys a life estate (or other interest) "to A for life, then to grantor's heirs," the remainder in the grantor's heirs is void, and instead the grantor retains a reversion. The doctrine rests on the idea that an heir can take nothing from a living ancestor by deed—heirs inherit only at death. **The modern trend** treats Worthier Title as a mere rule of construction (not an absolute rule of law) controlled by the grantor's intent; a clearly expressed intent to benefit the grantor's heirs will be honored.
*Apply: O, still living, conveyed "to A for life, then to O's heirs"; under the Doctrine of Worthier Title, the remainder in O's heirs is void, and O retains a reversion—O's heirs take nothing under the deed.*
> **CA divergence:** CA treats the Doctrine of Worthier Title as a rebuttable presumption (rule of construction), not an inflexible rule of property; evidence of contrary intent may preserve the remainder in the grantor's heirs.

---

### Rule Against Perpetuities (RAP) 🔴
Under the common-law Rule Against Perpetuities, a future interest is void from inception if there is any possibility—however remote—that it might vest more than 21 years after the death of some measuring life in being at the time of the conveyance. The rule applies to contingent remainders, executory interests, and vested remainders subject to open (as to unborn class members). It does not apply to vested remainders, reversions, possibilities of reverter, or rights of entry. Gifts from one charity to another are exempt. The **wait-and-see** and **cy pres** reforms (adopted in many states) save interests that would otherwise violate the rule by waiting to see if the interest actually vests in time.
*Apply: [Future interest] in [party] is subject to RAP; the measuring life is [life in being]; because [interest] could potentially vest more than 21 years after [measuring life]'s death, it is [valid/void] under the common-law rule.*
> **CA divergence:** CA has abolished the common-law RAP and adopted the **Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities** (Prob. Code § 21200 et seq.), which provides a 90-year wait-and-see period. An interest is valid if it either satisfies the common-law RAP at creation or actually vests within 90 years.

## C. Concurrent Estates

### Joint Tenancy — Creation and Four Unities 🔴
A joint tenancy is a form of co-ownership in which two or more persons hold with the right of survivorship, meaning a deceased joint tenant's share passes automatically to the surviving joint tenants rather than through the decedent's estate. To create a joint tenancy, four unities must be present at creation—**TTIS**: the co-tenants must take at the **same Time**, under the **same Title** (same instrument), with **identical Interests** (equal shares), and with the **same right of possession** (right to possess the whole). In addition to the four unities, the grantor must expressly state the right of survivorship; without clear survivorship language, modern law presumes a tenancy in common.
*Apply: [Grantor] conveyed "to A and B as joint tenants with right of survivorship"; all four unities—time, title, interest, possession—were satisfied at the moment of conveyance, creating a valid joint tenancy.*
> **CA divergence:** CA requires express survivorship language or use of the statutory form; a mere conveyance "to A and B" creates a tenancy in common. CA also allows a sole owner to create a joint tenancy by conveying directly to themselves and another (no strawman required under Civil Code § 683).

---

### Joint Tenancy — Severance 🔴
A joint tenancy is severed—and converted into a tenancy in common—by any act that destroys one of the four unities during the joint tenant's lifetime. **SPAM** summarizes the common severance methods: **Sale** (a joint tenant's inter vivos conveyance of her interest to a third party severs the joint tenancy as to that interest, even without the other joint tenants' knowledge or consent); **Partition** (voluntary agreement, judicial partition in kind, or forced sale); **Agreement** (written contract to sever); and **Mortgage** (under the minority title theory only—under the majority lien theory, granting a mortgage does not sever). Additionally, under equitable conversion, a joint tenant's mere act of entering a binding contract to sell her interest severs the joint tenancy immediately, before the deed is transferred.
*Apply: [Joint tenant A] secretly conveyed her interest to [Third Party]; this inter vivos transfer destroyed the unity of time and title as to A's share, severing the joint tenancy and converting it into a tenancy in common between [Third Party] and [B].*
> **CA divergence:** CA follows the lien theory; granting a mortgage does not sever the joint tenancy. However, a recorded deed of trust by one joint tenant does not sever in CA (confirmed in *Harms v. Sprague* approach; see *Grothe v. Cortlandt*—CA courts hold mortgage/deed of trust does not sever).

---

### Tenancy in Common 🟢
A tenancy in common exists when two or more persons own undivided fractional interests in property, each with the right to possess the entire property. Each co-tenant's interest is independently alienable, devisable, and descendible; there is no right of survivorship. The law presumes a tenancy in common when two or more persons take title without clear survivorship language. Interests need not be equal.
*Apply: The conveyance "to A and B" without survivorship language creates a tenancy in common; A and B each hold an undivided interest with the right to possess the whole, and each may freely transfer her share.*

---

### Co-Tenant Rights and Duties 🔴
Each co-tenant is entitled to possess and enjoy the whole property; one co-tenant who wrongfully excludes another from any part commits an **ouster**, entitling the excluded co-tenant to a remedy including an accounting for rental value. A co-tenant in exclusive possession (absent ouster) owes no rent to the other co-tenants. A co-tenant who leases to a third party must account to co-tenants for their proportionate share of rental income. A co-tenant who makes necessary repairs is entitled to contribution from co-tenants in proportion to their interests, provided advance notice was given; there is no right to contribution for improvements (though the improving co-tenant receives credit for net value added at partition). Each co-tenant bears her proportionate share of carrying costs (taxes, mortgage interest).
*Apply: [Co-tenant A]'s leasing of the property to [Third Party] obligates A to account to [Co-tenant B] for B's [fraction] share of rent; B in turn is responsible for B's proportionate share of taxes and upkeep.*

---

### Partition 🟢
Any joint tenant or tenant in common has the right to bring an action for partition at any time; this right cannot be waived by agreement in most jurisdictions. Partition may be accomplished by (1) voluntary agreement among the co-tenants; (2) partition in kind, where a court physically divides the property; or (3) a forced sale, where the court orders the property sold and divides the proceeds proportionately (ordered when physical division is impractical or inequitable). Tenants by the entirety cannot compel partition without divorce or mutual agreement.
*Apply: Because A and B hold as tenants in common and cannot agree on use, A may seek judicial partition; if physical division is impractical, the court will order a forced sale and divide proceeds according to ownership percentages.*

---

### Tenancy by the Entirety 🟡
A tenancy by the entirety is a form of co-ownership available only to married couples (and in some states, registered domestic partners). Each spouse holds the whole estate; neither can unilaterally alienate, encumber, or sever the tenancy without the other's consent. The right of survivorship is indestructible by unilateral action. A creditor of only one spouse cannot reach property held in tenancy by the entirety; only a joint creditor of both spouses can.
*Apply: [H] and [W], as spouses, hold Blackacre by the entirety; [H]'s attempt to mortgage his "share" is ineffective—neither spouse may unilaterally encumber or transfer a tenancy by the entirety.*
> **CA divergence:** CA does not recognize tenancy by the entirety. Married couples in CA hold property as community property or as joint tenants. Community property rules (equal management rights, spouse's one-half interest) serve a similar protective function.

## D. Servitudes

### Easements — Definition and Appurtenant vs. In Gross 🟢
An easement is a nonpossessory interest that entitles the holder to use another's land (the **servient estate**) in a specified way. An easement **appurtenant** benefits the holder in the physical use of adjacent land (the **dominant estate**); it runs automatically with the dominant estate on transfer, even if not mentioned in the deed. An easement **in gross** confers a personal or commercial benefit on the holder unrelated to ownership of other land (e.g., a billboard right, utility line). Commercial easements in gross are transferable; personal easements in gross are not.
*Apply: The easement allowing [A] to cross [B]'s land to reach [A]'s parcel is appurtenant to [A]'s dominant estate; it passed automatically when [A] conveyed the dominant estate to [C], regardless of whether the deed mentioned the easement.*

---

### Express Easement — Creation by Grant or Reservation 🔴
An easement for a duration exceeding one year must be created in a writing signed by the grantor, satisfying the Statute of Frauds. An easement **by grant** transfers a new easement interest to the holder. An easement **by reservation** is retained by the grantor when conveying the underlying fee (e.g., grantor conveys Blackacre to A but reserves a right-of-way). At common law, a grantor could not reserve an easement in favor of a stranger to the deed; modern law and CA allow reservation in favor of a third party. A failed written easement—one that does not meet SOF requirements—creates only a revocable license.
*Apply: [Grantor]'s deed to [Grantee] reserved a right-of-way over the western boundary; because the reservation is in a signed writing, a valid express easement by reservation was created.*
> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code § 1104 permits reservation of easements in favor of third parties; the common-law stranger-to-the-deed rule is abolished.

---

### Easement Implied from Prior Use 🔴
An easement implied from prior use (also called implied by existing use) arises when: (1) at some point before division, the dominant and servient parcels were under common ownership; (2) during that common ownership, the owner used part of the land for the benefit of another part (quasi-easement); (3) the use was apparent (visible, discoverable upon reasonable inspection) at the time of severance; and (4) the easement is reasonably necessary to the enjoyment of the dominant parcel. "Reasonably necessary" does not require strict necessity—convenient and beneficial use suffices.
*Apply: [Owner] used a path across the back portion of Blackacre to access the garage on the front portion; when [Owner] sold the back portion, an easement implied from prior use arose because the use was apparent and reasonably necessary to the front parcel's enjoyment.*

---

### Easement by Necessity 🔴
An easement by necessity is implied by law when a grantor conveys a portion of land that is completely landlocked—the grantee has no access to a public road except over the grantor's remaining land. Two elements are required: (1) common ownership of the dominant and servient parcels at some prior time, and (2) strict necessity at the time of severance. The easement lasts only as long as the necessity continues; if an alternative access route later becomes available, the easement by necessity terminates. An easement created by express grant does not terminate simply because necessity ends.
*Apply: When [Grantor] sold the back parcel to [Grantee], the only access to a public road was across [Grantor]'s remaining land; an easement by necessity arose automatically at severance and continues until [Grantee] obtains an alternative means of access.*

---

### Easement by Prescription 🔴
A prescriptive easement is acquired through use of another's land that is **OACH**: **Open and notorious** (visible use that a reasonable landowner would be aware of); **Actual** (physical use, not merely a claim); **Continuous** for the statutory period (uninterrupted, consistent with the character of the easement); and **Hostile** (without the servient owner's permission—permission defeats hostility and the prescriptive period). Permission can be negated by a posted sign or by the servient owner's affirmative granting of a license. Tacking of successive prescriptive users is permitted if there is privity between them.
*Apply: [A] openly drove across [B]'s field for [X] years without [B]'s permission; the use was visible, continuous, and hostile; if the statutory period is met, [A] has acquired a prescriptive easement.*
> **CA divergence:** CA prescriptive easement period is 5 years. Unlike adverse possession, CA does not require payment of taxes for a prescriptive easement.

---

### Scope of an Easement 🟡
The scope of an express easement is determined by the language of the grant and the reasonable intent of the parties at the time of creation. An easement holder may not unilaterally expand the use beyond what was contemplated, even if the change would benefit the servient estate. For implied and prescriptive easements, the scope is fixed by the prior use or the prescriptive use from which the easement arose. Increased intensity of use that is a reasonable evolution of the original use is generally permissible; a fundamentally different use is not.
*Apply: The easement granted for "agricultural access" does not authorize [Holder] to use the path for commercial truck traffic—such use exceeds the reasonable scope of the original grant and burdens the servient estate beyond what the parties contemplated.*

---

### Termination of Easements 🔴
An easement may be terminated by: (1) **Release** — a written release from the easement holder to the servient owner; (2) **Merger** — when title to the dominant and servient estates becomes vested in the same person, the easement is extinguished (it does not automatically revive upon re-separation); (3) **Abandonment** — the easement holder must demonstrate by physical action an intent to never use the easement again (mere non-use is insufficient); (4) **Prescription** — the servient owner may extinguish the easement by using the land in a manner inconsistent with the easement for the full statutory prescriptive period; (5) **Estoppel** — the servient owner materially changes position in reasonable reliance on the easement holder's assurance that the easement will no longer be used; (6) **Condemnation** — government taking of the servient estate extinguishes the easement; (7) **End of necessity** — an easement by necessity terminates when the necessity ends.
*Apply: [Easement holder]'s statement to [Servient owner] that the path would no longer be used, followed by [Servient owner]'s construction of a structure over the path in reliance, terminates the easement by estoppel.*

---

### License 🟡
A license is a mere personal privilege to enter another's land for a specific purpose; it conveys no property interest. Licenses require no writing and are freely revocable by the licensor at any time, with or without cause—except where revocation would work a fraud on the licensee who has invested substantially in reliance on the license (easement by estoppel / irrevocable license). A license is also irrevocable when it is coupled with an interest (e.g., a right to enter land to retrieve personal property one owns). A failed attempt to create an easement for lack of a sufficient writing results in a license, not an easement.
*Apply: [A]'s oral permission to [B] to use the driveway is a revocable license; however, if [B] spent $[X] constructing improvements in reasonable reliance on [A]'s promise, the license may be irrevocable under estoppel principles.*

---

### Negative Easement (SALS) 🟡
A negative easement entitles the holder to **prevent** the servient landowner from doing something on the servient land that would otherwise be lawful. Negative easements are recognized only in four traditional categories (**SALS**): blocking **S**unlight (solar access); blocking **A**ir flow; blocking the **L**ight through windows; and interfering with an artificial **S**tream of water (artificial flow). Because negative easements restrict rather than permit, they can be created **only expressly**, by a written instrument signed by the grantor; there is no negative easement by implication, necessity, or prescription. Any restriction outside the four SALS categories must be enforced as a restrictive covenant or equitable servitude.
*Apply: [A]'s right to prevent [B] from constructing a structure that blocks [A]'s solar panels falls within the negative easement category (Sunlight/Air) only if created by an express written grant; absent such a grant, [A] must rely on a restrictive covenant or equitable servitude.*
> **CA divergence:** CA Solar Shade Control Act and related statutes may provide statutory solar access rights independent of easement law; flag this in questions involving solar energy disputes.

---

### Profit à Prendre 🟢
A profit à prendre entitles the holder to enter the servient land and sever and remove a specified natural resource—such as minerals, timber, oil, gravel, or game. A profit shares all the rules applicable to easements: it must satisfy the Statute of Frauds to be created expressly, may be appurtenant or in gross, may be acquired by prescription, and may be terminated by the same methods as easements. An owner of a profit may bring an action for interference with that profit.
*Apply: [A]'s profit allowed [A] to enter [B]'s land and remove timber; [B]'s fencing of the property constitutes interference with the profit, entitling [A] to an injunction or damages.*

---

### Real Covenants — Burden Running with the Land 🔴
A real covenant is a promise respecting the use of land contained in a deed. For the **burden** of a covenant to run to a successor of the promisor so as to bind that successor at law, five elements must be established (**WIT-PH**): (1) the covenant must be in **Writing**; (2) the original parties must have **Intended** the burden to run (express or implied); (3) the promise must **Touch and Concern** the land (affect the parties in their capacity as landowners, not merely in a personal capacity); (4) **Horizontal Privity** must exist at the time the covenant was made—the original parties must have been in a special relationship (grantor-grantee, landlord-tenant, or mortgagee-mortgagor); and (5) **Vertical Privity** must exist between the original promisor and the successor—the successor must hold the same estate or a portion of it.
*Apply: [Covenantor]'s promise not to build commercially was in a deed (writing); the parties intended it to run; it limits land use (touches and concerns); the parties were grantor and grantee (horizontal privity); and [Successor] holds the same fee estate (vertical privity). Thus, the burden binds [Successor].*

---

### Real Covenants — Benefit Running with the Land 🟡
For the **benefit** of a covenant to run to a successor of the promisee (so the successor may enforce it at law), the following elements must be met (**WIT-V**, no horizontal privity required): (1) **Writing**; (2) **Intent** that the benefit run; (3) the promise must **Touch and Concern** the land; and (4) **Vertical Privity** between the original promisee and the successor. Horizontal privity is not required for the benefit to run.
*Apply: [Promisee]'s successor in title [Plaintiff] may enforce the covenant at law because writing, intent, touch-and-concern, and vertical privity are all satisfied—no horizontal privity is needed for the running benefit.*

---

### Equitable Servitude — Burden and Enforcement 🔴
An equitable servitude is a promise respecting land use that equity will enforce against successors who had **notice** of the promise, even if the technical requirements for a running covenant at law are not met. The elements for the burden of an equitable servitude to bind a successor are (**WITNES**): (1) **Writing** satisfying the SOF; (2) **Intent** to bind successors; (3) the promise **Touches and Concerns** the land; (4) **Notice** — the successor must have had actual, inquiry, or record notice of the promise at the time of acquisition. No horizontal privity is required. The remedy for breach of an equitable servitude is an **injunction**; the remedy for breach of a real covenant is **damages**.
*Apply: [Successor] purchased with record notice of the restriction; even if the burden does not run at law, equity will enforce the servitude against [Successor] by injunction.*

---

### Implied Equitable Servitude — Common Scheme 🔴
When a developer subdivides land and sells lots pursuant to a **common scheme** of development (e.g., residential-only use), equitable servitudes may be implied as to lots sold without express restriction, binding those lot owners who had notice of the scheme. Two elements are required: (1) when the sales began, the developer had a general plan of uniform development that included the lot in question; and (2) the lot owner took with notice of the scheme—actual notice, inquiry notice (visible uniformity of the neighborhood), or record notice (recorded plat or prior deeds in the chain of title mentioning the restrictions).
*Apply: [Developer] sold the majority of lots with residential-only restrictions; the subdivision's uniform character placed [Lot Owner] on inquiry notice of the common scheme, and equity will imply an equitable servitude binding [Lot Owner]'s lot.*

---

### Termination of Covenants and Equitable Servitudes 🟡
Covenants and equitable servitudes may be terminated by: (1) **Written release** from the benefited party to the burdened party; (2) **Merger** of the benefited and burdened estates in the same owner; (3) **Condemnation** of the burdened property; and (4) for equitable servitudes, the equitable defense of **changed conditions**—where the character of the neighborhood has changed so dramatically since the restriction was imposed that enforcement would be inequitable (e.g., an all-residential area now surrounded by commercial development). Changed conditions is an equitable defense and does not extinguish the covenant at law.
*Apply: The once-residential neighborhood surrounding [Lot Owner]'s parcel has been rezoned and developed commercially; [Lot Owner] may assert changed conditions as an equitable defense to enforcement of the residential-only restriction.*

---

## E. Adverse Possession

### Adverse Possession — Elements 🔴
Adverse possession is the acquisition of title to real property by open, hostile, continuous, and actual possession for the full statutory period. The elements are **OACH**: (1) **Open and notorious** — possession must be visible and obvious, of the type a reasonable owner of such land would make, placing the true owner on constructive notice; (2) **Actual** — the claimant must physically occupy and use the land (clearing, fencing, cultivating, building—consistent with the land's nature); (3) **Continuous** — without significant interruption for the full statutory period (not necessarily constant; seasonal use of a summer cabin may be sufficient); (4) **Hostile** — without the true owner's permission; the claimant need not have a subjective belief of ownership under the objective standard most courts apply. Upon satisfaction of all elements for the full statutory period, title vests in the adverse possessor by operation of law, but a quiet title action is typically needed to perfect the record.
*Apply: [Claimant] fenced and farmed [Owner]'s back acre openly for [X] years, without permission; if [Claimant] satisfies all elements for the statutory period, title has vested.*
> **CA divergence:** CA adverse possession requires ALL elements PLUS **payment of all property taxes** assessed against the disputed parcel during the adverse possession period (CCP § 325). This is a hard requirement frequently tested on the CA bar. The CA statutory period is **5 years**.

---

### Adverse Possession — Tacking and Disabilities 🟡
A claimant may **tack** her period of possession onto a predecessor's period to satisfy the statutory period, provided there is a sufficient nexus ("privity") between successive possessors — established by a non-hostile transfer such as a deed (even a void one), a will, descent, or other voluntary connection. Tacking is not allowed where one adverse possessor ousted another. The statute of limitations for adverse possession does not begin to run against a true owner who is under a **disability** (minority, insanity, or imprisonment) at the inception of the adverse possession. If the owner becomes disabled after adverse possession commences, the disability is irrelevant; only disabilities existing at the moment adverse possession begins toll the statute.
*Apply: [Claimant] possessed for 3 years and then conveyed to [Successor] by deed; [Successor] may tack [Claimant]'s 3 years to [Successor]'s own possession because there is privity via the deed; if [Successor] has possessed for 2 more years, the combined 5-year period satisfies CA's statute.*

---

## F. Miscellaneous Land Rights

### Lateral and Subjacent Support 🟡
A landowner has an absolute right to have her land supported in its **natural state** by the soil of adjoining land (lateral support) and by the land beneath (subjacent support). If an adjacent landowner's excavation causes the plaintiff's unimproved land to cave in or subside, the excavating neighbor is **strictly liable**—no showing of negligence is required. If the land is improved with buildings, the owner of the improved land may recover damages only if she can show that the land would have collapsed even **without** the added weight of the improvements (i.e., the natural land itself was unsupported). If the improved land would not have subsided but for the added weight, the excavator is liable only in **negligence**, not strict liability.
*Apply: [Neighbor]'s excavation caused [Owner]'s lot to cave in; because [Owner]'s lot bore a house, [Owner] must show that the unimproved land would have collapsed too to recover under strict liability—otherwise [Owner] must prove [Neighbor]'s negligence.*
> **CA divergence:** CA follows the general common-law rule on lateral support; CA Civil Code § 832 codifies the right to lateral support and provides that an excavating landowner must give reasonable notice and take precautions to avoid damage.

---

### Water Rights — Riparian and Prior Appropriation 🟡
Under the **riparian doctrine** (majority of eastern states), the right to use water belongs to landowners whose property borders the watercourse; each riparian owner may make reasonable use of the water, but use must not unreasonably interfere with other riparian owners' rights. Under the **prior appropriation doctrine** (used in arid western states), water rights are based on priority of beneficial use—the first person to actually divert and beneficially use the water acquires a senior water right regardless of whether she owns riparian land ("first in time, first in right"). **Groundwater**: surface owners may make reasonable use of percolating groundwater, but waste is prohibited. **Surface water (diffuse)**: under the **common enemy rule**, each landowner may freely alter drainage or divert surface water runoff; under the **natural flow rule** (minority), a landowner may not significantly alter natural drainage patterns to the detriment of neighbors.
*Apply: [State] follows the prior appropriation doctrine; [Farmer A], who diverted water from the creek in 1990, holds a senior right superior to [Farmer B], who began diverting in 2000—even if [Farmer B]'s land is closer to the creek.*
> **CA divergence:** CA applies a **hybrid** system — CA recognizes both riparian rights (for riparian landowners) and appropriative rights (under a state permit system). In conflicts, appropriative rights with a permit generally prevail over unexercised riparian rights; CA's State Water Resources Control Board administers permits.

---

### Trespass 🟢
A trespass to land consists of an unauthorized, intentional (or in some formulations, negligent) physical invasion of the plaintiff's real property by a person or by a tangible physical object the defendant set in motion. The plaintiff need not show actual damages — nominal damages are available for any trespass. The trespass must be by a physical intrusion; purely intangible invasions (light, noise, odors) are governed by nuisance law, not trespass. The appropriate remedy to remove a trespassory person or object from the land is an action in **ejectment**; the plaintiff may also seek compensatory and, where conduct is willful, punitive damages.
*Apply: [D]'s construction crew poured concrete onto [P]'s adjoining parcel without permission; the physical intrusion constitutes trespass, and [P] may seek ejectment, damages for any injury to the land, and nominal damages even without proof of harm.*

---

### Private Nuisance 🔴
A private nuisance is a **substantial and unreasonable interference** with another person's use and enjoyment of her land. The interference must be substantial (not merely trivial or fleeting) and unreasonable (the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of the defendant's conduct under a balancing test, or the harm is severe and the plaintiff should not have to bear it without compensation). Unlike trespass, nuisance does not require a physical tangible invasion — noise, light pollution, odors, and vibrations can all qualify. A nuisance claim fails if the interference results solely from the plaintiff's **hypersensitivity** or specialized use of land beyond what is normal for the area. Remedies include injunction and/or damages; a court may also award permanent damages in lieu of injunction where an injunction would be unduly burdensome relative to the public benefit.
*Apply: [D]'s industrial plant emits noxious fumes that substantially interfere with [P]'s ability to use and enjoy her adjacent residence; if the harm is unreasonable under the balancing test, [P] may obtain injunctive relief or damages in nuisance—even without a physical trespass.*

---

## G. Landlord-Tenant

### Tenancy for Years 🟢
A tenancy for years (also called an estate for years or term of years) is a leasehold for a fixed, ascertainable period of time, regardless of how short or long. It terminates automatically at the end of the stated term without any notice from either party. Any term exceeding one year must be created in a writing signed by the party to be charged, to satisfy the Statute of Frauds.
*Apply: The lease providing "[Tenant] shall occupy the premises from January 1 to December 31" is a tenancy for years; it terminates automatically on December 31 without notice.*

---

### Periodic Tenancy 🟢
A periodic tenancy is a leasehold that continues for successive, recurring intervals until either party gives proper notice of termination. It may be created expressly (e.g., "month-to-month") or by implication (e.g., where a lease omits duration but specifies rent intervals, or where a tenant holds over under a residential lease and the landlord accepts rent). To terminate, the party must give advance notice at least equal to the length of the period (e.g., one month's notice for a month-to-month tenancy), except a year-to-year tenancy requires only six months' notice. Termination must take effect at the end of a natural period.
*Apply: [Landlord] accepted [Tenant]'s continued rent payment after lease expiration, creating an implied month-to-month periodic tenancy; to terminate, either party must give at least one month's advance written notice effective at month's end.*

---

### Tenancy at Will and Tenancy at Sufferance 🟢
A **tenancy at will** has no fixed duration and may be terminated by either party at any time, though reasonable notice is generally required. A **tenancy at sufferance** arises when a tenant remains in possession (holds over) after the lawful tenancy expires without the landlord's consent; it lasts only until the landlord elects either to evict the tenant or to bind the tenant to a new tenancy. If the landlord elects to hold the tenant to a new term, the new periodic tenancy is typically measured by the original lease terms (month-to-month for monthly rent; up to one year if rent was annual).
*Apply: [Tenant]'s failure to vacate at lease expiration created a tenancy at sufferance; [Landlord] may elect to evict and recover possession or, by accepting rent, bind [Tenant] to a new periodic tenancy.*

---

### Implied Warranty of Habitability 🔴
In residential leases, every landlord impliedly warrants that the premises are fit for basic human habitation and will remain so throughout the tenancy. The standard is whether the premises are suitable for basic living needs (e.g., functioning heat, plumbing, weatherproofing, and structural safety). This warranty is **non-waivable**—any lease provision purporting to waive it is void. Upon breach, the tenant may (1) terminate the lease and vacate; (2) repair the defect and deduct the reasonable cost from rent (where permitted by statute); (3) withhold rent or reduce rent to the premises' fair rental value; or (4) remain in possession and seek damages.
*Apply: [Landlord]'s failure to repair the broken heating system from November through February rendered the premises unfit for basic habitation; [Tenant] may withhold rent, repair-and-deduct, or terminate the lease.*
> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code §§ 1941-1942 codify the implied warranty of habitability for residential units; tenants may use repair-and-deduct (up to one month's rent, no more than twice per year) and rent withholding remedies.

---

### Quiet Enjoyment and Constructive Eviction 🔴
Every lease contains an implied covenant of quiet enjoyment—the tenant's right to undisturbed use and possession of the premises, free from interference by the landlord or anyone claiming through the landlord. Breach occurs by **actual eviction** (physical exclusion) or **constructive eviction**. For constructive eviction, the tenant must establish **SING**: (1) **Substantial interference** with possession attributable to the landlord's act or failure to act (a chronic, ongoing problem, not a minor inconvenience); (2) **Notice** to the landlord giving the landlord a reasonable opportunity to cure; (3) the landlord fails to cure; and (4) the tenant must **Go**—vacate the premises within a reasonable time. A tenant who stays in possession cannot claim constructive eviction.
*Apply: [Landlord]'s failure to repair the chronic roof leak after [Tenant]'s notice substantially interfered with possession; [Tenant] vacated within a month, satisfying all SING elements; thus, constructive eviction occurred and [Tenant] is relieved of further rent obligations.*

---

### Landlord's Duty to Deliver Possession 🟡
Under the **majority (English) rule**, the landlord has an implied duty to deliver actual physical possession of the premises to the tenant at the beginning of the tenancy. If a prior tenant wrongfully holds over, the landlord—not the new tenant—must take action to remove the holdover; if the landlord fails, the landlord is in breach and the new tenant is entitled to damages and may terminate. Under the **minority (American) rule**, the landlord need only deliver legal possession (the right to occupy), not actual physical possession; the new tenant must take action against the holdover herself.
*Apply: Because [Jurisdiction] follows the majority rule, [Landlord]'s failure to remove the holdover tenant before [New Tenant]'s lease commenced is a breach of the duty to deliver possession.*

---

### Assignment vs. Sublease 🔴
A tenant's transfer of the **entire remaining leasehold** with no reversion retained is an **assignment**; the assignee steps into privity of estate with the landlord and is directly liable for all covenants in the original lease that run with the land (including rent), but the original tenant remains secondarily liable under privity of contract. A transfer that retains any portion of the remaining term (even one day) is a **sublease**; the subtenant has privity only with the original tenant, not with the landlord—the landlord cannot sue the subtenant directly for rent or covenants, and the landlord's remedies run only against the original tenant. If the lease is silent on transfers, the tenant may freely assign or sublease; anti-transfer clauses are strictly construed against the landlord.
*Apply: [Tenant] transferred the lease to [Transferee] with three months remaining and retained no interest; this is an assignment—[Transferee] is in privity of estate with [Landlord] and directly liable for rent, while [Tenant] remains secondarily liable.*
> **CA divergence:** CA requires landlords not to unreasonably withhold consent to assignment when the lease requires consent; if the landlord unreasonably withholds, the tenant may assign despite the clause (Civil Code § 1995.310).

---

### Tenant's Duty to Pay Rent — Breach and Landlord's Remedies 🔴
When a tenant breaches the duty to pay rent while remaining in possession, the landlord's remedies are limited to: (1) sue for each installment of rent as it comes due, or (2) evict the tenant through proper judicial process and sue for unpaid rent accrued. Self-help eviction (changing locks, removing belongings, shutting off utilities) is unlawful everywhere. When a tenant abandons the premises and stops paying rent, the landlord may (1) **accept surrender** — treat the abandonment as a mutual rescission of the lease, terminating the tenant's obligations; (2) under the **minority view**, ignore the abandonment and hold the tenant liable for rent as it accrues; or (3) under the **majority view** (and the modern approach), re-let the premises on the tenant's behalf and hold the tenant liable for any deficiency, after the landlord's duty to mitigate is satisfied.
*Apply: [Tenant] vacated mid-lease without notice; [Landlord] cannot sit idle and demand full remaining rent—[Landlord] must make reasonable efforts to re-let, and [Tenant] is liable only for the deficiency after mitigation.*
> **CA divergence:** CA imposes a statutory duty to mitigate on residential landlords (Civil Code § 1951.2).

---

### Retaliatory Eviction 🟡
A landlord may not evict, threaten to evict, raise rent, or reduce services in retaliation against a tenant who has (1) reported housing code violations to a government authority; (2) requested repairs of habitability defects; or (3) exercised any other legally protected right. If the landlord's adverse action occurs within a defined period after the tenant's protected activity (typically 90 to 180 days, depending on jurisdiction), retaliatory motive is presumed and the burden shifts to the landlord to show a legitimate non-retaliatory reason.
*Apply: [Landlord] served a notice to vacate two weeks after [Tenant] reported code violations to the housing authority; retaliatory motive is presumed, and [Landlord] must establish a legitimate independent basis for the eviction.*
> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code § 1942.5 creates a rebuttable presumption of retaliation for 180 days after the tenant's protected activity and permits the tenant to remain in possession plus obtain damages and attorneys' fees.

---

### Landlord's Tort Liability (CLAPS) 🔴
At common law, a landlord owed no general duty to tenants or third parties to maintain the premises in a safe condition ("caveat lessee"). However, the landlord is liable in tort under five exceptions captured by **CLAPS**: (1) **Common areas** — the landlord must exercise reasonable care to maintain hallways, stairwells, lobbies, and other common areas; (2) **Latent (hidden) defects** — the landlord must disclose known defects that are not discoverable by a reasonable inspection; (3) **Assumption of repairs** — if the landlord voluntarily undertakes repairs, the landlord must complete them non-negligently; (4) **Public use** — a landlord who leases premises for public use (e.g., an auditorium) and who knows or should know that the tenant will not repair is liable for defects; (5) **Short-term furnished dwelling** — in short-term leases of furnished premises, the landlord impliedly warrants the premises are safe, and is liable for defects that injure the tenant.
*Apply: [Landlord] knew of the broken stairwell railing in the common hallway and failed to repair it; under the common-areas exception, [Landlord] is liable to [Injured Visitor] for negligence.*

---

## H. Land Sale Contracts

### Statute of Frauds — Land Sale Contracts 🔴
A contract for the sale of an interest in real property must be evidenced by a writing signed by the party to be charged (the defendant), describing the land and stating some consideration. Emails and electronic records generally satisfy the writing requirement under modern e-signature statutes. The **part performance** doctrine allows equity to enforce an oral land contract if the buyer has taken possession **and** paid all or part of the purchase price, or **and** made substantial improvements to the property—acts that are unequivocally referable to the existence of a contract. Part performance is available only in equity (specific performance), not at law (damages).
*Apply: The oral agreement to sell Blackacre is unenforceable at law; however, [Buyer]'s taking possession and making $[X] in improvements constitutes part performance, entitling [Buyer] to equitable enforcement.*

---

### Marketable Title 🔴
Every land sale contract contains an implied covenant that the seller will deliver **marketable title** at closing—title reasonably free from doubt, lawsuit, and the threat of litigation that a reasonable, prudent buyer would be unwilling to accept. Title is rendered unmarketable by: (1) a defect in the chain of title (e.g., a gap, forgery, or title acquired via adverse possession even of a portion); (2) outstanding encumbrances (mortgages, liens, easements, or restrictive covenants) that the buyer did not agree to accept—though a seller may satisfy an outstanding mortgage at closing with proceeds of the sale; and (3) zoning violations (not mere zoning restrictions—only actual violations make title unmarketable). The seller need not deliver marketable title until closing; the buyer's remedy upon breach is rescission and return of the deposit, or specific performance with a price reduction.
*Apply: [Seller]'s title shows an undisclosed easement across the southeast corner; this encumbrance renders title unmarketable; [Buyer] may rescind the contract or demand specific performance with a proportionate price reduction.*

---

### Equitable Conversion and Risk of Loss 🔴
Under the doctrine of equitable conversion, once a specifically enforceable land sale contract is signed, equity treats the buyer as the equitable owner of the land and the seller as the holder of legal title in trust for the buyer. As a consequence, if the property is **destroyed or damaged** between contract and closing through no fault of either party, the **risk of loss falls on the buyer** under the majority rule—the buyer must still complete the purchase (though insurance proceeds held by the seller inure to the buyer's benefit). Many states have modified this by statute to keep the risk on the seller until closing; the minority rule also keeps risk on the seller. Most modern contracts expressly allocate risk.
*Apply: Blackacre burned down after the contract was signed but before closing; under the majority equitable conversion rule, [Buyer] bears the risk of loss and must still pay the contract price—though [Buyer] is entitled to any insurance proceeds.*
> **CA divergence:** CA follows the minority rule by statute — risk of loss remains on the **seller** until legal title or possession passes to the buyer (CA Civil Code § 1662).

---

### Duty to Disclose Latent Material Defects 🔴
A seller of real property has an affirmative duty to disclose known latent material defects that are not discoverable by a reasonable inspection and that materially affect the value or desirability of the property. This duty applies to both physical defects and certain off-site conditions. The traditional caveat emptor rule shielded sellers from undisclosed defects but has been substantially abrogated; builder-sellers additionally warrant fitness and workmanlike construction on new homes, and their successors may also benefit from implied warranties under some theories.
*Apply: [Seller] knew of the cracked foundation hidden behind paneling but did not disclose it; the defect was latent, material, and not discoverable on reasonable inspection—[Seller] is liable to [Buyer] for fraud and breach of the implied duty to disclose.*
> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code § 1102 et seq. requires broad seller disclosures via a statutory Transfer Disclosure Statement for most residential transactions; failure to provide the TDS gives the buyer rescission rights.

---

### Implied Warranty of Fitness — Builder-Seller 🔴
As a general rule, real property is sold under the doctrine of **caveat emptor** ("buyer beware"), meaning the seller makes no implied warranty about the condition or fitness of the property. However, an important exception applies to **builder-sellers**: when a seller is also the builder of a new residential structure, the seller impliedly warrants that the property was constructed in a **workmanlike manner** and is **fit for its intended use** as a residence. This warranty runs with the property and may extend to subsequent purchasers who discover latent construction defects within the warranty period. The implied warranty covers latent defects—those not discoverable upon a reasonable inspection—and is breached when the construction is defective in materials, design, or workmanship.
*Apply: [Builder-Seller] constructed and sold the home to [Buyer]; the foundation cracks concealed beneath flooring constitute a latent structural defect; [Buyer] may sue [Builder-Seller] for breach of the implied warranty of fitness and workmanlike construction.*
> **CA divergence:** CA's Right to Repair Act (Civil Code § 895 et seq.) provides specific statutory construction defect standards and a mandatory pre-litigation process for most new residential construction claims; this supplements (and in some respects supplants) common-law implied warranty claims.

---

### Merger Doctrine 🟡
Under the merger doctrine, when the sale closes and the deed is delivered, the deed supersedes (merges into itself) all prior agreements, including the land sale contract; thereafter, the buyer's rights are governed solely by the deed, not by the contract. Thus, contract warranties not carried into the deed are extinguished. The merger doctrine does not apply to collateral agreements (provisions intended by the parties to survive closing, such as post-closing repair obligations or indemnities) or to fraud claims.
*Apply: [Buyer]'s contract contained a promise by [Seller] to repair the roof before closing; because that promise was not carried into the deed and [Buyer] accepted the deed without reservation, the promise merged into the deed and is extinguished.*

---

## I. Deeds

### Deed Requirements — Lawful Execution 🟢
A valid deed must be (1) in **writing** signed by the grantor; (2) contain a sufficient **description** of the property (need not be perfect, but must identify the land with reasonable certainty—a metes-and-bounds description, a reference to a recorded plat, or a commonly known name suffices); (3) name the **grantee** with sufficient certainty; and (4) include **words of conveyance** (granting language). A deed need not recite consideration, be witnessed, be notarized, or be recorded to be legally effective between the parties—though recording provides constructive notice to the world and protects against subsequent purchasers.
*Apply: [Grantor]'s handwritten and signed document conveying "the house at 123 Main Street, Springfield" to [Grantee] is a valid deed—the writing, signature, description, grantee identification, and conveyance language are all present.*

---

### Deed Delivery 🔴
A deed is not effective until **delivered** with present intent to transfer title. Delivery is a question of the grantor's **present intent** to be immediately bound—not the physical act of handing over the paper. A deed physically handed to the grantee raises a strong presumption of delivery. A deed handed to the grantee with an oral condition is nevertheless effective immediately—the oral condition drops out and title passes. A deed retained by the grantor with no present intent is not delivered. **Delivery by escrow** is valid: the grantor deposits the deed with a neutral third party to be released upon occurrence of a condition (typically payment of the purchase price); title passes to the grantee upon satisfaction of the condition, even if the grantor dies before that moment. A grantee's **express rejection** of a deed defeats delivery.
*Apply: [Grantor] handed the deed to [Grantee] "but only if [Grantee] pays taxes by year-end"—the oral condition drops out; delivery occurred upon physical transfer, and title passed to [Grantee] immediately.*

---

### Types of Deeds — Warranty Covenants 🔴
Deeds are categorized by the covenants of title they contain. A **quitclaim deed** conveys whatever interest the grantor has (if any) with no warranties—the grantor makes no representations about the quality of title. A **general warranty deed** contains six covenants: three **present** (seisin — the grantor owns the estate; right to convey — the grantor has authority to transfer; no encumbrances — no undisclosed encumbrances exist) and three **future** (quiet enjoyment — the grantee will not be disturbed by third-party claims; warranty — the grantor will defend title against lawful claims; further assurances — the grantor will take additional steps to perfect title if needed). Present covenants are breached, if at all, at the moment of delivery; future covenants run with the land and may be breached when the grantee is later disturbed. A **statutory special warranty deed** contains only the grantor's own-act covenants: the grantor has not previously conveyed the property to anyone else and has not created any undisclosed encumbrances.
*Apply: [Grantor]'s general warranty deed covenanted against all encumbrances; the undisclosed easement existing at delivery breaches the present covenant against encumbrances, and [Buyer] may sue [Grantor] immediately.*

---

### Estoppel by Deed 🟡
Under the doctrine of estoppel by deed (after-acquired title), a grantor who purports to convey real property in which she has no interest is estopped from denying the validity of that conveyance once she subsequently acquires the title she previously purported to transfer. Title "feeds" to the grantee automatically upon the grantor's acquisition. This doctrine applies principally where the grantor conveyed by a warranty deed (creating an expectation of good title); it may not apply to quitclaim deeds, depending on jurisdiction.
*Apply: [Grantor] conveyed Blackacre by warranty deed before acquiring title; when [Grantor] later obtained title, it automatically passed to [Grantee] by estoppel by deed—[Grantor] is estopped from claiming ownership.*

---

## J. Recording Acts

### The Recording System — BFP Defined 🔴
Recording statutes protect **bona fide purchasers (BFPs)**—parties who (1) give **value** (substantial pecuniary consideration; not a donee, heir, or devisee), and (2) take **without notice** of a prior unrecorded interest. A bargain price qualifies as value; nominal consideration (a penny) does not. Notice may be **actual** (direct knowledge of the prior transfer), **inquiry** (facts that would put a reasonable person on notice to investigate—including visible possession by a third party), or **record** (constructive notice from a properly recorded instrument within the chain of title). A donee who takes without paying value receives no protection under any recording statute; the **shelter rule** provides the only exception.
*Apply: [Buyer] paid $[X] in substantial consideration and had no actual, inquiry, or record notice of [Prior Purchaser]'s interest at closing—[Buyer] qualifies as a BFP.*

---

### Race-Notice Recording Statute 🔴
Under a **race-notice statute** (the majority form and CA's system), a subsequent purchaser is protected against a prior unrecorded interest only if she (1) takes **without notice** of the prior interest at the time of her purchase AND (2) **records first**. Both conditions must be satisfied simultaneously. A subsequent purchaser who has notice cannot win even if she records first. A subsequent purchaser without notice who fails to record first also loses.
*Apply: [B] purchased Blackacre after [A] but without notice of [A]'s prior deed, and [B] recorded before [A]; under the race-notice statute, [B] prevails over [A] because [B] was a BFP who recorded first.*
> **CA divergence:** CA Civil Code § 1214 is a **race-notice** statute. To prevail, a subsequent purchaser must record before the prior instrument and must have taken without notice. This is among the highest-tested CA distinctions.

---

### Notice Recording Statute 🟡
Under a **notice statute**, a subsequent BFP is protected against a prior unrecorded interest simply by taking without notice at the time of purchase—regardless of whether the subsequent purchaser ever records. The subsequent purchaser's protection vests at the moment she takes without notice; subsequent recording by the prior claimant does not defeat her. The subsequent purchaser need not be the first to record.
*Apply: Under a notice statute, [B]'s purchase without notice of [A]'s prior unrecorded interest protects [B] against [A]—even if [A] records before [B], [B]'s interest is superior.*

---

### Shelter Rule 🔴
Under the shelter rule, a person who takes from a BFP acquires the same priority that the BFP enjoyed, even if the new taker would not otherwise qualify as a BFP (e.g., because the new taker has notice of the prior interest or paid no consideration). The rule ensures the free transferability of land by allowing BFPs to pass their protected status to successors. However, the shelter rule cannot be used to launder a transaction—a person cannot convey to a BFP and then reacquire from the BFP to bootstrap priority.
*Apply: [C] acquired from [BFP B], who had prevailed over [A]; even though [C] had actual notice of [A]'s claim, [C] shelters under [B]'s BFP status and prevails over [A].*

---

### Chain of Title Problems — Wild Deed and Out-of-Chain Recording 🟡
A deed is a **wild deed** if it is recorded but cannot be located by searching the chain of title because it is not indexed to or traceable from the name of any grantor in the chain (e.g., because a prior deed in the chain was never recorded). A wild deed provides no constructive (record) notice to a subsequent purchaser. Similarly, a deed recorded **before the grantor acquired title** (early recording) is generally outside the chain of title and does not give constructive notice, though some jurisdictions disagree. A deed recorded **after the grantor conveyed** (late recording) is also outside the chain of title and provides no record notice to subsequent buyers of other parcels from the same grantor.
*Apply: [A]'s deed was recorded, but because [A]'s grantor's deed was never recorded, [A]'s deed appears as a wild deed—[B]'s search of the chain of title under [Grantor]'s name would not reveal [A]'s deed, so [B] takes without record notice.*

## K. Mortgages

### Mortgage — Creation and Lien vs. Title Theory 🔴
A mortgage is a security interest in real property given by a debtor (mortgagor) to a creditor (mortgagee) to secure repayment of a debt. To be enforceable, a mortgage typically must be in writing (satisfying the Statute of Frauds), signed by the mortgagor, describe the debt and property, and be delivered. Under the **lien theory** (majority, including CA), the mortgagor retains legal title and possession throughout; the mortgagee holds only a lien on the property, which becomes enforceable only on default. Under the **title theory** (minority), the mortgagee holds legal title subject to the mortgagor's right to redeem. In CA, the mechanism is a **deed of trust** (trustor, trustee, beneficiary), not a traditional mortgage, but courts apply substantially the same rules.
*Apply: [Borrower] gave [Lender] a mortgage on Blackacre; under CA's lien theory, [Borrower] retains title and possession, and [Lender] holds only a security interest enforceable upon default.*
> **CA divergence:** CA is a **lien-theory state** and commonly uses deeds of trust. The trustee holds bare legal title; the trustor (borrower) has equitable title and full use of the property until foreclosure.

---

### Equitable Mortgage 🟡
An equitable mortgage arises when a transaction that is technically structured as an absolute deed or some other conveyance is, in substance, intended by the parties to serve as security for a debt. Courts look to the parties' **actual intent**, and parol evidence is freely admissible to show that what appears on its face to be an outright conveyance was intended as security for repayment. A classic scenario: the borrower conveys an absolute deed to the lender as collateral for a loan, intending to re-convey once the debt is paid. Equity treats the grantee as a mortgagee, not an owner; the grantor retains an equity of redemption. If the lender-grantee conveys to a bona fide purchaser for value and without notice of the equitable mortgage, the BFP takes free and clear, and the original borrower's only remedy is against the lender for fraud and proceeds of the sale.
*Apply: [O] delivered an absolute deed to [Creditor] as security for a $[X] loan; despite the deed's facial appearance, equity treats the arrangement as a mortgage—[O] retains the equity of redemption and may redeem by tendering the full debt.*
> **CA divergence:** CA courts apply a strong presumption against equitable mortgages in favor of the deed's plain meaning; the burden of proof on the party claiming an equitable mortgage is high, and clear and convincing evidence of intent to create security is typically required.

---

### Mortgage Transfer — Note and Assignment 🟡
A mortgage follows the note—whoever holds the properly endorsed and delivered promissory note also holds the mortgage, even without a separate recorded assignment. A mortgagee (creditor) may transfer the mortgage by (1) endorsing and delivering the note to the transferee, or (2) executing a separate written assignment. A transferee who takes the note as a **holder in due course (HDC)** takes free of personal defenses the mortgagor might assert against the original mortgagee (e.g., failure of consideration, fraud in the inducement) but is still subject to real defenses (fraud in the factum, material alteration, duress, incapacity, illegality, infancy, insolvency/discharge in bankruptcy — **MADIFII** or "real defense" mnemonic).
*Apply: [Lender] endorsed and delivered the note to [Transferee]; [Transferee] is a HDC and takes free of [Borrower]'s defense that [Lender] failed to make promised improvements—that is a personal defense.*

---

### Assumption vs. Subject-To — Transfer by Mortgagor 🟡
When a mortgagor sells encumbered property, the lien follows the land (assuming the mortgage was properly recorded) regardless of who now owns the property. If the buyer **assumes** the mortgage, both the original mortgagor and the assuming buyer are personally liable on the debt (buyer primarily, original mortgagor secondarily as surety). If the buyer takes **subject to** the mortgage, the buyer accepts no personal liability—the original mortgagor remains personally liable—but the land remains encumbered, and the lender may foreclose if the debt is not paid.
*Apply: [Buyer] assumed the mortgage; upon [Buyer]'s default, [Lender] may sue [Buyer] primarily on the debt and [Seller] secondarily—both are personally liable.*

---

### Foreclosure — Judicial and Nonjudicial 🔴
If the mortgagor defaults, the mortgagee must foreclose to cut off the mortgagor's equity of redemption. **Judicial foreclosure** involves a court-supervised sale; all junior lienholders must be joined or their liens survive. **Nonjudicial (power-of-sale) foreclosure** proceeds without court oversight pursuant to a power-of-sale clause in the deed of trust; the trustee (in a deed of trust) gives proper statutory notice and conducts a trustee's sale. Foreclosure terminates all interests **junior** to the foreclosed mortgage; senior interests survive, and the buyer at sale takes subject to them. If the mortgagee fails to join a necessary party (junior lienholder), that party's interest survives the foreclosure.
*Apply: [Lender-1]'s foreclosure (recorded first) terminates [Lender-2]'s junior mortgage; [Buyer at Sale] takes free of [Lender-2]'s lien but subject to [Senior Lender-0]'s mortgage.*
> **CA divergence:** CA primarily uses **nonjudicial foreclosure** (trustee's sale) under Civil Code §§ 2924 et seq. The one-action rule (CCP § 726) generally requires the lender to first foreclose on the real property before suing personally on the debt.

---

### Equity of Redemption and Statutory Redemption 🔴
The **equity of redemption** is the mortgagor's common-law right to redeem (pay off) the mortgage and free the property from the lien at any time before the foreclosure sale is completed. To redeem, the mortgagor must pay the full amount owed plus interest and costs; if an acceleration clause exists, the full accelerated balance is due. The mortgagor may not contractually waive the equity of redemption (a "clog" on the equity of redemption is void). **Statutory redemption** is a post-sale right in some jurisdictions allowing the mortgagor (and sometimes junior lienholders) to redeem the property for a fixed period after the foreclosure sale by paying the sale price. Statutory redemption is a separate right from the equity of redemption.
*Apply: Before the trustee's sale, [Mortgagor] has the equitable right to redeem by paying the full accelerated debt; once the sale occurs, the equity of redemption is cut off—[Mortgagor] can only redeem under any applicable statutory right.*
> **CA divergence:** CA has **abolished statutory redemption** in most nonjudicial foreclosure contexts—the one-action and antideficiency rules are the primary post-foreclosure protections.

---

### Antideficiency and One-Action Rules 🔴
After a foreclosure sale, if the sale proceeds are insufficient to cover the full debt, the mortgagee may seek a **deficiency judgment** against the mortgagor for the shortfall—unless state law restricts deficiency judgments. The excess proceeds after satisfying the foreclosed debt are distributed to junior lienholders in order of priority, then to the mortgagor.
*Apply: [Lender] foreclosed and the sale produced $[X]; the remaining $[Y] balance may be the subject of a deficiency action against [Borrower] unless barred by antideficiency statute.*
> **CA divergence:** CA has robust **antideficiency protections** (CCP §§ 580a–580d): (1) No deficiency judgment following a **nonjudicial trustee's sale** (CCP § 580d—the most heavily tested rule); (2) No deficiency on a **purchase money mortgage** (loan used to purchase the property) regardless of foreclosure method (CCP § 580b); (3) Even in judicial foreclosure, deficiency is limited to the greater of the debt minus the fair market value or the debt minus the sale price (fair value limitation). The **one-action rule** (CCP § 726) requires the secured creditor to exhaust the real property security before pursuing a personal money judgment.

---

### Mortgage Priorities — PMM and Recording 🔴
As among competing liens, priority is generally determined by the recording act — first to record (under race-notice) in good faith and for value prevails. A **purchase money mortgage (PMM)** — a mortgage given to the seller or a third-party lender to finance the purchase of the property itself — has **superpriority** over other encumbrances that arise at the same time as the PMM (e.g., a judgment lien attaching to the buyer's newly acquired property on the closing date). The seller's PMM takes priority over a third-party lender's PMM when both arise simultaneously. Parties may contractually alter priority through **subordination agreements**, by which a senior lienholder agrees to stand behind a junior lienholder.
*Apply: [Seller] took back a PMM at closing on the same day [Judgment Creditor]'s lien attached to [Buyer]'s new property; [Seller]'s PMM has superpriority over the judgment lien.*

## L. Fixtures

### Fixtures 🔴
A fixture is a once-movable chattel that has been so permanently affixed to real property that it becomes part of the realty. The test is whether the annexor's **objective intention** — judged by the nature of the article, the manner of annexation, and the relationship of the parties — was to make the chattel a permanent improvement to the realty. Classic fixtures include HVAC systems, built-in cabinetry, and plumbing; classic non-fixtures (trade fixtures) include manufacturing equipment and storefront signs. Fixtures pass with the real estate on conveyance unless expressly reserved. A **tenant** who installs a chattel may remove it before or at the end of the lease, provided removal does not cause substantial damage to the premises; failure to remove before lease expiration may constitute abandonment. Between mortgagor and mortgagee, fixtures acquired after the mortgage attach to the mortgage lien.
*Apply: [Tenant]'s installation of custom shelving units bolted to the walls evidences permanent improvement intent, making them fixtures; [Tenant] must remove them before lease expiration without causing substantial damage, or they pass to [Landlord].*

## M. Zoning and Takings

### Zoning — Variance and Nonconforming Use 🟡
Government may regulate land use pursuant to its police power to protect health, safety, and welfare. A **variance** allows a landowner to deviate from the zoning ordinance upon showing (1) unique undue hardship from strict application and (2) that the variance will not adversely affect surrounding property values or the character of the neighborhood. A **nonconforming use** is a use that was lawful when established but becomes nonconforming under a subsequently enacted zoning ordinance; such uses may continue but generally may not be expanded or rebuilt if destroyed. Immediate elimination of a nonconforming use without compensation constitutes a taking. **Unconstitutional exactions** — conditions imposed on development permits — must be related in nature to the impact of the proposed development (the *Nollan/Dolan* nexus and proportionality tests); otherwise they are unconstitutional conditions on the permit.
*Apply: [Owner]'s pre-existing lumber yard was a nonconforming use when the residential zoning ordinance was enacted; the city may require phase-out over a reasonable amortization period but may not eliminate the use overnight without compensation.*

---

### Regulatory Takings and Just Compensation 🔴
The Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause requires the government to pay just compensation when it takes private property for public use. An **explicit (physical) taking** — permanent physical invasion of property, even minor — is a per se taking requiring full compensation. A **regulatory taking** occurs when government regulation goes too far: under *Penn Central*, courts balance the economic impact on the owner, the extent of interference with investment-backed expectations, and the character of the government action. A **categorical regulatory taking** occurs when regulation deprives the owner of all economically beneficial use of the land (*Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*), requiring compensation unless the regulation merely codifies existing background principles of state property law. **Temporary takings** also require compensation for the period of the deprivation.
*Apply: The ordinance prohibiting any development on [Owner]'s parcel eliminates all economically beneficial use; this is a categorical regulatory taking under *Lucas*, requiring just compensation unless the restriction reflects background principles of CA nuisance or property law.*
