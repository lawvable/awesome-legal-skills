## A. Foundational Framework

### General Community Property Presumption 🔴
Under California law, all property acquired by either spouse during the marriage is presumptively community property. The party asserting that an asset is separate property bears the burden of overcoming this presumption with clear and convincing evidence. This presumption applies equally to registered domestic partners (RDPs), including same-sex couples and qualifying elderly opposite-sex couples receiving Social Security benefits.
*Apply: [Asset] was acquired [during/before/after] marriage, so the threshold question is whether the [spouse] has met the burden of rebutting the community presumption.*

Thus, absent rebuttal evidence, any asset acquired during the marital economic community is CP.

---

### Marital Economic Community — Timeline & Date of Separation 🔴
The marital economic community begins at marriage and ends at the permanent separation of the spouses. Separation requires both a physical parting and an unambiguous, subjective intent by at least one spouse not to resume the marital relationship, objectively evidenced by conduct. Post-separation earnings and acquisitions are the separate property of the earning or acquiring spouse. The precise date of separation therefore controls characterization of wages, purchases, and debt accrued during any disputed period.
*Apply: [Spouse] [moved out/filed/communicated intent] on [date], and thereafter [earned wages/acquired asset/incurred debt]; the question is whether that date constitutes legal separation so that the [asset/liability] is [SP/CP].*

Thus, the date separation became permanent determines which assets fall inside or outside the community.

---

### Separate Property — Definition 🔴
Separate property (SP) includes: (1) property owned by a spouse before marriage; (2) property acquired during marriage by gift, bequest, devise, or descent; (3) property purchased with separate funds during marriage; and (4) rents, issues, and profits derived from separate property. The SP character does not change merely because the asset is held or managed jointly, but the proponent must trace the SP origin to overcome the community presumption.
*Apply: [Spouse] claims [asset] is SP because it was [owned before marriage / received as a gift or inheritance / purchased with traced SP funds]; the court must determine whether that origin has been established.*

Thus, if the SP origin is traced and proven, the asset retains its separate character.

---

## B. Characterization & Tracing

### Characterization — General Tracing Principle 🔴
The character of property follows its source: an asset takes the character of the funds used to acquire it. When property has been purchased with a mix of community and separate funds, the party claiming separate character must trace each dollar of SP contribution to the acquisition. The court looks to the time of acquisition, not later payment patterns, to determine initial character, though subsequent events like community paydown of an SP mortgage can create a community interest via the proration rule.
*Apply: [Spouse] contends [asset] is SP because it was purchased with [inheritance/pre-marital savings]; the analysis requires tracing those funds from [source account] to [acquisition date].*

Thus, absent successful tracing, the community presumption governs.

---

### Commingling — Exhaustion Method 🟡
When SP funds are deposited into an account containing CP funds, the SP is not automatically converted to CP, but the commingling makes tracing the burden of the party asserting SP. Under the exhaustion method, the claimant demonstrates that, at the moment the disputed asset was purchased, community funds in the account had already been fully expended on family expenses, so the purchase necessarily came from the remaining separate funds. California presumes that family living expenses are paid from community funds, which aids this analysis.
*Apply: [Spouse] deposited [SP amount] into a joint account; at the time [asset] was purchased on [date], the community balance had been reduced to [amount] by [family expenses], leaving only SP funds available.*

Thus, if exhaustion is shown, the asset is SP despite the commingled account.

---

### Commingling — Direct Tracing Method 🟡
Under the direct tracing method, the claimant proves SP character by showing two things: (1) at the time of the purchase, there were identifiable separate funds available in the account, and (2) the purchasing spouse actually intended to use those separate funds — not the community funds also present — for that specific acquisition. Intent can be shown through records, testimony, or contemporaneous documentation.
*Apply: [Spouse] had [SP amount] in the account alongside community funds on [date]; [spouse] testified / records show intent to use the SP portion to purchase [asset].*

Thus, direct tracing succeeds when both sufficiency of SP funds and intent to use them are established.

---

### Transmutation — Pre-1985 Oral Agreements 🟡
Before January 1, 1985, California permitted transmutations — changes in the character of property between spouses — by oral agreement, either express or implied from conduct. A spouse could orally agree to convert CP to SP, SP to CP, or one spouse's SP to the other's SP, and such an agreement was valid and enforceable without a writing.
*Apply: The alleged transmutation occurred [before 1985]; [spouse] claims [asset] was changed from [CP/SP] to [SP/CP] based on an [oral statement / course of conduct].*

Thus, pre-1985 oral transmutations are recognized if proven by competent evidence of agreement.

---

### Transmutation — Post-1984 Writing Requirement 🔴
Effective January 1, 1985, California requires any transmutation of property to be made in writing, signed by the spouse whose interest is adversely affected, and must contain an express declaration that a change in ownership is being made. A general reference to the property, a statement in a will, or a deed that merely recites title are insufficient — the writing must unambiguously state that the characterization of the property is being altered. An exception exists for gifts of tangible personal property of a personal nature (clothing, jewelry, personal electronics) that are not substantial in value relative to the circumstances of the marriage; this exception does not apply to birthday or anniversary gifts.
*Apply: [Spouse] claims [asset] was transmuted from [CP/SP] to [SP/CP] by [document/statement]; the document must be examined to determine if it expressly declares a change in ownership, was signed by the adversely affected spouse, and post-dates January 1, 1985.*

Thus, absent a compliant writing, a post-1984 transmutation is invalid.

## C. Title Rules

### Joint Title at Divorce — Anti-Lucas Rule (Post-1984) 🔴
When spouses acquire property in joint title on or after January 1, 1984, the property is presumed to be community property at divorce or legal separation — regardless of whether one spouse contributed separate funds to the purchase. This "anti-Lucas" rule means a spouse who made an SP down payment on jointly titled real property does not automatically receive credit; however, that spouse is entitled to reimbursement (without interest) for separate property contributions to the down payment, improvements, and principal reduction on the mortgage (the "DIP" rule), unless the contributing spouse waived that right in writing.
*Apply: The home was purchased on [date after 1/1/1984] and titled in both spouses' names; [spouse] contributed [SP amount] toward [down payment/mortgage principal/improvements] and seeks either reimbursement or a proportionate ownership share.*

Thus, at divorce, jointly titled post-1984 property is CP, but the contributing spouse may claim DIP reimbursement.

---

### Joint Title at Death — Lucas Rule 🟡
When jointly titled property is distributed at death, the Lucas rule governs: property held in both spouses' names is presumptively CP, and any SP contribution to its acquisition is presumptively a gift to the community. The contributing spouse has no claim for reimbursement unless there is a written agreement to that effect executed before or at the time of the acquisition.
*Apply: [Spouse] died while the property was jointly titled; [surviving spouse/heir] claims [SP contribution] entitles the estate to a reimbursement or larger share; the court will apply the Lucas presumption of gift absent a written agreement.*

Thus, at death, SP contributions to jointly titled property are treated as gifts to the community unless a contrary written agreement exists.

---

### Married Woman's Special Presumption (Pre-1975) 🟡
Prior to 1975, California recognized a special exception to the general community presumption: if community funds were used to take written title in a married woman's name alone, in a form that did not indicate CP or joint tenancy, the property was presumptively the wife's separate property. This reflected the historical context in which wives rarely held title independently. The husband could rebut this presumption by establishing that he did not intend to make a gift to the wife but had a different reason for the titling arrangement (e.g., tax planning).
*Apply: Property was acquired before 1975 and titled solely in [wife's] name using community funds; [husband's estate/husband] seeks to rebut the married-woman's presumption by showing [no gift intent / other reason for titling].*

Thus, pre-1975 sole titling in the wife's name with CP funds raises an SP presumption rebuttable only by evidence of a non-gift purpose.

## D. Special Assets

### Business Appreciation — Pereira Formula 🔴
When a spouse brings a separate property business into the marriage and its value grows during the marriage, California uses two competing formulas to allocate the increase between the community and the SP estate. Under Pereira, the SP business owner is credited with the original SP value of the business at the date of marriage, plus a fair rate of return on that capital (10% per year). The remainder of the business's current value is characterized as community property attributable to the community labor — the spouse's efforts, skill, and time devoted during marriage, all of which are community assets. Pereira favors the community and is applied when the increase in value is primarily attributable to the spouse's personal efforts rather than the inherent nature of the business.
*Apply: [Spouse]'s business was worth [$X] at marriage; [spouse] worked in the business throughout the marriage; today it is worth [$Y]; under Pereira, SP = [$X + (10% × $X × years)], and the remainder [$ Y minus that amount] is CP.*

Thus, Pereira allocates a reasonable capital return to SP and attributes the residual appreciation to community labor.

---

### Business Appreciation — Van Camp Formula 🔴
Under the Van Camp formula, the community's share is limited to the reasonable market-rate compensation for the spouse's services during the marriage, reduced by the amount of family expenses already paid from business earnings. The SP estate retains the remainder. Van Camp favors the SP estate and is applied when the business's growth is driven primarily by the nature of the enterprise, fortuitous market forces, or capital investment rather than by the spouse's personal labor. Courts should discuss both formulas and select the one that most equitably reflects the true source of the appreciation.
*Apply: [Spouse]'s business grew substantially due to [market conditions / self-perpetuating goodwill]; under Van Camp, CP = [market-rate salary for comparable executives × years] minus [family expenses paid from business earnings]; the rest is SP.*

Thus, Van Camp limits the community's interest to the fair value of the spouse's labor, leaving business-driven appreciation as SP.

---

### Pension Benefits — Time Rule 🔴
Retirement and pension benefits accumulated during the marriage are community property to the extent they were earned during the marriage, regardless of whether the plan has vested at the time of divorce. California applies the time rule to apportion the community interest: the community fraction equals the number of years of service rendered during the marriage divided by the total years of service from hire to the date of retirement or separation. If the pension has not yet vested at divorce, a court may award the non-employee spouse an "if and when" order (the spouse receives her share upon actual distribution) or may award other CP assets of equivalent present value as a cash-out. Once vested, the non-employee spouse has a present enforceable interest even if the employee spouse has not yet retired.
*Apply: [Employee spouse] worked [X years before marriage] and [Y years during marriage]; at [divorce/retirement] the pension was worth [$Z]; the community fraction is [Y / (X + Y)], and the non-employee spouse is entitled to half of that fraction.*

Thus, the time rule allocates pension value to the community in proportion to the years of marriage during which the benefit was earned.

---

### Disability Pay, Workers' Compensation & Severance 🟡
Disability retirement payments and workers' compensation benefits are treated as wage replacements and are characterized according to when they are received, not when they were earned — post-separation receipts are SP. However, if a spouse elects disability retirement in lieu of an ordinary old-age retirement pension, the disability pay may be characterized as CP to the extent it replaces a pension interest that was community property; a spouse cannot defeat the other's CP rights by electing a disability option. Severance pay presents a split of authority: one view treats it as SP because it replaces future post-separation earnings, while the competing view treats it as CP because it arises from employment during the marriage and accrued under collective bargaining agreements.
*Apply: [Spouse] received [disability pay / workers' comp / severance] [before/after] the date of separation; characterization turns on whether the payments replace wages earned [during/after] the community period and whether the election was designed to defeat [other spouse]'s CP rights.*

Thus, disability and wage-replacement benefits follow the timing-of-receipt rule, subject to the anti-defeat election doctrine.

---

### Pension Benefits — QDRO & Federal Preemption 🟡
In a qualified pension or retirement plan governed by federal law (ERISA), the non-participant spouse's community property interest is recognized and may be enforced through a Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO) — a court order that directs the plan administrator to pay the non-participant spouse her proportionate share directly from the plan. Federal law, however, also limits what the non-participant spouse can do with that interest: if the non-participant spouse predeceases the participating spouse, she cannot devise her CP interest in the plan by will or trust, because federal law preempts California's rule that each spouse may bequeath one half of CP; the interest simply lapses.
*Apply: [Non-employee spouse] seeks to enforce her CP share in [spouse]'s 401(k)/pension through a QDRO; alternatively, [non-employee spouse] died first and attempted to bequeath her CP share in the plan — federal preemption prevents that bequest from being honored regardless of California CP rules.*

Thus, a QDRO is the vehicle for enforcing the non-participant spouse's CP share in a qualified plan, but federal preemption bars her from devising that interest if she dies first.

---

### Military Retirement Benefits 🟡
Spouses of military personnel have community property rights in a military retirement plan to the extent the retirement benefit was earned during the marriage. Federal law (the Uniformed Services Former Spouses' Protection Act) expressly authorizes states to treat military retired pay as marital property subject to division, and California does so under ordinary CP time-rule principles. The community fraction of the benefit is determined by comparing the years of service that fell within the marriage to the total years of service at retirement.
*Apply: [Spouse] served in the military for [X years], [Y of which] were during the marriage; at divorce, the military retirement benefit is subject to California CP division, and the non-military spouse is entitled to [Y / total service years] × ½ of the monthly retired pay.*

Thus, military retirement benefits accumulated during marriage are divisible as CP under both California law and federal authorization.

---

### Stock Options 🟡
Stock options granted to a spouse during the marriage are community property to the extent they were awarded in exchange for marital services. Where options are granted during the marriage but do not become exercisable until after the end of the community, courts apply a time-based apportionment formula: the community fraction equals the period from the date the option was awarded to the date the marital economic community ended, divided by the total period from the date of award to the date the option becomes exercisable. All forms of incentive compensation — bonuses, restricted stock units, deferred compensation — earned during the marriage are similarly CP.
*Apply: Options were awarded on [date] and become exercisable on [date]; the community ended on [date of separation]; the community fraction is [(community end date − award date) / (exercise date − award date)].*

Thus, stock options are apportioned between CP and SP based on the proportion of the vesting period that fell within the marital community.

---

### Goodwill of a Professional Practice 🟡
Goodwill of a professional practice is community property to the extent it was developed or enhanced during the marriage. Goodwill is the value of the practice beyond the fair compensation for the professional's personal labor and a reasonable return on tangible capital assets — it is the expectation of continued patronage attributable to the reputation and going-concern of the business. Goodwill may be valued by expert testimony or by capitalizing the "excess earnings" of the practice above those returns. A buy-sell agreement between partners that caps goodwill at a nominal value is not conclusive and does not bind the court.
*Apply: [Spouse]'s [law/medical/accounting] practice was established [before/during] marriage; the court must determine whether goodwill value attributable to the [marital period / pre-marital reputation] is CP or SP, and must independently value it rather than deferring entirely to any partnership agreement.*

Thus, professionally generated goodwill earned during the marriage is CP subject to valuation and division.

---

### Education, Degrees & Reimbursement 🟡
A professional degree or educational credential obtained during the marriage is not itself treated as community property subject to equal division, because it is personal to the holder and not transferable. However, if community funds were expended on the education and that education substantially enhanced the educated spouse's earning capacity, the community is entitled to reimbursement. The reimbursement claim is presumed to have been satisfied if more than ten years have elapsed since the degree was awarded, because the community is presumed to have already benefited from the enhanced earnings. Reimbursement is further unavailable if the other spouse also received a comparable CP-funded education. Educational debts are assigned solely to the spouse who incurred them and do not follow the equal-division default.
*Apply: The community paid [$X] in tuition/fees for [spouse]'s [degree]; [spouse] graduated [Y years ago]; [other spouse] seeks reimbursement [within/outside] the 10-year window; [did/did not] the other spouse also receive a CP-funded education.*

Thus, CP-funded education yields a reimbursement right that is presumed satisfied after ten years of marital benefit.

---

### Personal Injury Recovery 🔴
The character of a personal injury award depends on who inflicted the injury. When a third party tortfeasor injures a spouse, the recovery is classified as community property during marriage. However, at divorce the award is allocated entirely to the injured spouse unless the funds have already been expended or so commingled with other community assets that they cannot be identified, or unless the interests of justice — including the economic needs of both spouses — require a different allocation. If the tortfeasor is the other spouse, the recovery is the injured spouse's separate property, reflecting California's policy against insurance fraud through interspousal litigation. At death, personal injury proceeds that have not been segregated are treated as ordinary CP.
*Apply: [Spouse] received [$X] in personal injury damages from [third party / other spouse]; the funds are [still segregated / commingled]; the court must determine whether the third-party exception, the at-death rule, or the interspousal SP rule applies.*

Thus, third-party PI recoveries are CP during marriage but awarded to the injured spouse at divorce absent countervailing equities.

---

### Life Insurance — Term vs. Whole Life 🟡
The community's interest in a life insurance policy depends on the type of policy and when premiums were paid. For whole life and other policies with cash surrender value, the community acquires a proportionate ownership interest based on the proration of premiums paid from community funds versus separate funds. For term life insurance — which provides pure coverage with no investment component and no cash surrender value — the character of the policy is determined by the last premium payment: if the last premium was paid with community funds, the death benefit is CP; if paid with SP, the benefit is SP. The community's right to any wrongfully designated beneficiary payment can be vindicated post-death by recovering one half from the donee or the estate.
*Apply: [Spouse] held a [term / whole life] policy; premiums were paid from [community / separate / mixed] funds; at death, the court must apply the [last-premium rule / proration formula] to characterize the death benefit or cash value.*

Thus, the character of life insurance turns on policy type — last-premium controls for term, proration controls for whole life.

---

### Improvements — CP Funds Used on SP 🟡
When a spouse expends community funds to improve his or her own separate property, the community does not acquire a proportionate ownership interest in the real property (because improvements become fixtures and merge into the land). Instead, the non-improving spouse is entitled to the greater of: (1) the cost of the improvements, or (2) the enhanced value added to the SP by those improvements. This rule prevents a spouse from enriching his or her own SP nest at the community's expense. When community funds are spent improving the other spouse's separate property, some courts presume a gift to that spouse's SP, rebuttable by evidence of an agreement to reimburse; others reject the gift presumption and allow direct reimbursement — both views should be discussed.
*Apply: [Spouse] used [$X in CP funds] to [renovate/improve] the [home / rental property] that is [his/her] SP; the community seeks the greater of [cost of improvements =$X] or [enhanced value = $Y].*

Thus, CP spent on SP generates a community right to the greater of cost or enhanced value, not an ownership share.

---

### Installment Purchases & Proration Rule 🟡
When an installment purchase begins before marriage (or before the marital community closes) and mortgage principal is paid down with community funds during the marriage, the community acquires a proportionate ownership interest in the property under the proration (or buy-in) rule. The community fraction equals the portion of the total principal debt extinguished by community payments; only principal reductions count — interest, insurance, and property taxes do not build community equity. If a spouse inherits property subject to a mortgage and pays it down with CP funds, the same proration applies. The proration rule also applies to life insurance policies paid in part with pre-marital and in part with marital funds.
*Apply: [Spouse] purchased [property] before marriage for [$X] with a mortgage balance of [$Y]; during the marriage, community funds paid [$Z] of principal; the community's ownership interest is [Z/Y] × total current value.*

Thus, community principal payments on a pre-marital installment purchase earn the community a proportionate equity stake.

## E. Credit Acquisitions

### Acquisitions on Credit During Marriage 🟡
Property purchased on credit during the marriage is presumptively acquired on community credit and is therefore CP. The ultimate characterization, however, depends on the primary intent of the lender at the time of the loan: if the lender looked primarily to the separate credit, assets, or estate of one spouse, the asset is SP. If the lender looked to the community estate, the asset is CP. The analysis is fixed at the moment of acquisition; subsequent repayment with SP or CP funds may affect reimbursement rights but does not retroactively change the initial character of the asset.
*Apply: [Spouse] borrowed [$X] during marriage to purchase [asset]; the lender [extended credit based on / required / looked primarily to] [community income / [spouse]'s pre-marital assets / [spouse]'s separate guarantee]; this determines whether the [asset] is CP or SP from inception.*

Thus, credit acquisitions during marriage are CP unless the lender's primary reliance was on one spouse's separate estate.

## F. Management, Control & Fiduciary Duty

### Management & Control — SP and CP Rules 🟢
Each spouse has exclusive management and control over his or her own separate property. As to community property, both spouses have equal management authority and may independently buy, sell, encumber, or contract on behalf of the community without the other's joinder — subject to three major exceptions: (1) the operating-spouse with primary management of a CP business has predominant management authority over that business; (2) neither spouse may sell or encumber household furnishings or clothing without the other's written consent, and any such transaction is voidable; and (3) both spouses must join in any instrument conveying, selling, or leasing community real property for more than one year — a unilateral conveyance by a spouse who misrepresents marital status gives the non-consenting spouse one year to void the transfer, but that limitation does not apply if the buyer knew the seller was married (no BFP protection).
*Apply: [Spouse] [sold / encumbered / leased] [CP asset — furniture / real property / business] without [other spouse]'s [joinder / written consent]; the court must determine whether the transaction falls within one of the three exceptions and whether it is void or merely voidable.*

Thus, equal CP management has critical exceptions for the family home, personal property, and real estate that require joinder or consent.

---

### Fiduciary Duty Between Spouses 🟡
Spouses stand in a confidential relationship with one another and owe each other the highest duty of good faith and fair dealing with respect to all transactions affecting community property. When one spouse gains an advantage from a transaction affecting CP — whether an interspousal transfer, investment decision, or gratuitous disposition — a rebuttable presumption of undue influence arises, placing the burden on the advantaged spouse to show the transaction was fair. Under a 2002 statute, grossly negligent or reckless management of community funds constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty independently of any advantage gained.
*Apply: [Spouse] [transferred CP to a third party / made a risky investment with community funds / gifted CP without consent]; [other spouse] claims [undue influence / breach of fiduciary duty]; the advantaged spouse must prove the transaction was fair and not the product of overreach.*

Thus, the fiduciary framework polices interspousal dealings over CP and shifts the burden to the spouse who profited from the transaction.

---

### Unauthorized Gifts of CP 🟢
Neither spouse may make a lifetime gift of community property without the written consent of the other. An unauthorized gift is treated as a unilateral disposition of property that one spouse does not own — the giving spouse holds only a one-half interest. The non-consenting spouse may: (1) set the entire gift aside and restore the asset to the community; (2) recover offsetting CP assets of equal value at divorce; or (3) if the gift was not discovered until after the giving spouse's death, recover one half of the gift from either the donee or the decedent's estate. Federal preemption is an exception — CP used to purchase U.S. Savings Bonds cannot be recovered because federal law supersedes California CP rules.
*Apply: [Spouse] gifted [CP asset] to [third party] without [other spouse]'s consent; [other spouse] seeks to [void the gift / recover half / offset against other CP]; the court must determine which remedy is available given the status of the asset.*

Thus, unauthorized CP gifts are voidable by the non-consenting spouse with multiple remedial options, subject to the federal preemption carve-out.

## G. Division at Divorce & Death

### Equal Division at Divorce 🔴
At dissolution of marriage, California requires equal division of all community property — each and every community asset must be divided 50/50 unless the parties agree otherwise or a statutory exception applies. Economic disparity in earning power between spouses is not a basis for unequal division of CP; it may be addressed only through spousal or child support. A court may make a non-pro-rata division that awards one spouse a particular asset while awarding the other equivalent value (e.g., one spouse receives the house while the other receives stock of equal value). Exceptions permitting an unequal split include: (1) misappropriation of CP by one spouse; (2) personal injury awards, which go to the injured spouse; (3) community liabilities exceeding assets; (4) separately incurred educational debts; and (5) tort liability incurred for a non-community purpose.
*Apply: [Spouse] claims entitlement to more than half of [asset] based on [misappropriation / PI award / separate educational debt]; the court must determine whether any statutory exception to equal division applies.*

Thus, equal division governs by default at divorce, with named statutory exceptions permitting — but not requiring — a larger share to one spouse.

---

### Testamentary Power Over CP at Death 🟢
At death, each spouse has full testamentary power over all of his or her separate property and over one half of the community property. The other one half of the CP belongs absolutely to the surviving spouse and cannot be devised away by the decedent. If a will attempts to bequeath the surviving spouse's CP share to a third party, the survivor must elect between taking under the will (accepting the bequest and allowing the entire CP to pass as directed) or asserting his or her CP rights (rejecting the will and retaining the one-half CP interest, which may require returning any bequest). Under intestacy, the surviving spouse receives the entire community estate if the decedent leaves no issue, or one-half of the community estate alongside the decedent's descendants.
*Apply: [Decedent spouse] attempted to bequeath [entire CP / more than ½ CP] to [heir]; [surviving spouse] must elect between [taking under the will] and [asserting CP rights]; if no will, intestacy rules determine the share.*

Thus, each spouse may will only one half of CP, and the surviving spouse's half is not subject to the decedent's testamentary power.

## H. Quasi-Community Property & Putative Spouse

### Quasi-Community Property 🟡
Quasi-community property (QCP) is property acquired by either spouse while the couple was domiciled outside California that would have been classified as community property had the acquisition occurred in California. At divorce, QCP is treated identically to true CP and divided equally. At death, the surviving spouse has a vested one-half interest in the decedent's QCP — mirroring the true CP rule — but the decedent has no rights in the survivor's QCP. For out-of-state real property at death, the situs rule applies and the other state's law controls, though a surviving spouse may seek a resulting or constructive trust in California if the bequest of the situs property violated her QCP rights. QCP is subject to the same creditors' claims as true CP.
*Apply: The couple was domiciled in [other state] when [spouse] earned [salary/acquired asset]; now domiciled in California at [divorce/death]; the court must determine if the property would have been CP had it been acquired in California.*

Thus, QCP protects the non-acquiring spouse's expectations when the couple relocates to California from a non-CP jurisdiction.

---

### Putative Spouse / Quasi-Marital Property 🟡
A putative spouse is a person who participated in a marriage that is void or voidable but who had an objectively reasonable, good-faith belief that the marriage was valid, based on the totality of the circumstances. The putative spouse doctrine protects good-faith participants in bigamous, underage, or otherwise invalid marriages. Property accumulated by a putative spouse during the putative marriage is characterized as quasi-marital property and is generally divided 50/50, just as CP would be at dissolution, provided the claiming party can establish both the subjective good faith and the objective reasonableness of that belief. A party who knew the marriage was invalid at its inception cannot invoke putative spouse status.
*Apply: [Party] entered a marriage that was [void / voidable] because [bigamy / lack of capacity / prior undissolved marriage]; [party] claims good-faith belief in validity based on [facts]; the court must weigh whether that belief was objectively reasonable under the totality of circumstances.*

Thus, an objectively reasonable good-faith belief in the validity of a defective marriage entitles the putative spouse to a CP-equivalent division of quasi-marital property.

---

### Common Law Marriage & Marvin Claims 🟢
California does not recognize common-law marriage; couples who cohabit and hold themselves out as married without a valid ceremony or license acquire no CP rights based on that relationship. However, under the Marvin doctrine, a non-marital cohabitant may enforce express or implied contractual rights against the other party. An express contract — oral or written — describing property-sharing arrangements is fully enforceable. An implied contract may be inferred from the parties' conduct, course of dealing, or the nature of their joint enterprise, and the relationship may be treated as a partnership or joint venture for purposes of property division. Marriage alone does not constitute performance sufficient to validate an otherwise incomplete agreement.
*Apply: [Partners] lived together for [X years] without legal marriage; [party] claims [express agreement / implied contract from conduct] to share [property / earnings]; the court must determine the existence and terms of any enforceable non-marital agreement.*

Thus, cohabitation without valid marriage creates no CP rights but may give rise to contractual or partnership remedies under Marvin.

## I. Premarital Agreements

### Premarital Agreements — Formation & Scope (CA UPAA) 🔴
Under California's version of the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, a premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties before marriage to be enforceable; oral premarital agreements are unenforceable unless fully performed before marriage (and marriage itself does not constitute performance) or unless enforcement is compelled by estoppel based on detrimental reliance. The parties may contract about virtually any property right — characterization of existing or future property, spousal support, inheritance rights, management of assets — with the sole exception that the agreement may not adversely limit or waive child support obligations.
*Apply: [Parties] entered a [written / oral] agreement [before marriage] purporting to [characterize earnings as SP / waive spousal support / alter inheritance rights]; the court must determine whether the agreement satisfies the UPAA writing requirement and whether its subject matter is permissible.*

Thus, a valid premarital agreement can override California's default CP rules for virtually every property right except child support.

---

### Premarital Agreements — Voluntariness & the 7-Day Rule 🔴
A premarital agreement is presumed involuntary and therefore unenforceable unless the party seeking to enforce it can show that: (1) the challenging party was presented with the agreement at least seven calendar days before the date of signing; and (2) the challenging party was either represented by independent legal counsel at the time of signing, or was fully and accurately informed in writing about the terms and basic effect of the agreement and the rights being waived and signed a separate acknowledgment of receipt of that disclosure. The seven-day rule is a floor, not a ceiling; egregious time pressure combined with other circumstances may still render an agreement involuntary even if technically signed seven days in advance.
*Apply: [Party] received the draft agreement [X days] before signing; [party] [was / was not] represented by independent counsel; [party] [did / did not] receive written disclosure of terms and sign an acknowledgment; these facts determine whether the voluntariness presumption has been overcome.*

Thus, the UPAA's 7-day rule and independent counsel or written disclosure requirement protect against uninformed waivers of CP rights.

### Premarital Agreements — Unconscionability & Spousal Support Waivers 🟡
Even a voluntary premarital agreement may be unenforceable if it is unconscionable. For provisions other than spousal support, unconscionability must exist at the time of execution, and the challenger must also show: (1) no full and fair disclosure of the other party's property or financial obligations; (2) no written waiver of the right to disclosure; and (3) the challenger lacked adequate independent knowledge of the other party's finances. For spousal support waiver provisions specifically, post-2002 law adds a separate enforcement bar: a spousal support waiver is unenforceable if the challenger was not represented by independent legal counsel at signing, or — even if represented — if enforcement would be unconscionable at the time of enforcement (not merely at execution). Unconscionability as to any provision is ultimately a question of law for the court.
*Apply: [Spouse] challenges the spousal support waiver on the ground that it is unconscionable [at execution / at time of enforcement]; the court must separately assess [whether independent counsel represented [spouse]] and [whether enforcement is conscionable given current circumstances].*

Thus, spousal support waivers face a dual-unconscionability check — at signing and again at enforcement — unique among premarital agreement provisions.

## J. Creditors & Liability

### Tort Liability — Spouse as Tortfeasor 🟡
When a spouse commits a tort against a third party, the priority of satisfaction depends on whether the act was performed for the benefit of the community or not. If the tortious act was performed for a community purpose, liability is satisfied first from community property, then from the tortfeasor spouse's separate property. If the tortious act was not for a community purpose, liability is satisfied first from the tortfeasor's separate property, and community property is reached only if the SP is insufficient; the non-tortfeasor spouse's separate property is never reachable.
*Apply: [Spouse] committed [tort] while [acting for the community, e.g., driving to work / acting for a personal purpose, e.g., recreational activity]; the third party judgment creditor may first look to [CP / tortfeasor SP] then [SP / CP], but not to [non-tortfeasor]'s SP.*

Thus, the order of liability exposure tracks whether the tort arose from a community or personal enterprise.

---

### Tort Liability — Spouse Injured by Third Party 🟡
A personal injury award received by a spouse from a third-party tortfeasor is community property while the marriage continues. At divorce, however, the award is assigned entirely to the injured spouse unless it has been spent or commingled to the point of non-traceability, or unless the interests of justice — including the economic needs of both spouses and the available assets of the community — otherwise require. At death, any unspent, identifiable PI recovery is treated as ordinary CP subject to the testamentary and intestacy rules. If the tortfeasor is the other spouse, the recovery is the injured spouse's separate property.
*Apply: [Spouse] received [$X] personal injury settlement from [third party]; at divorce, [other spouse] claims a share; the court must determine if the funds have been [segregated / commingled] and whether any equitable circumstances require deviation from full allocation to the injured spouse.*

Thus, interspousal PI awards are CP during marriage but allocated to the injured spouse at divorce barring commingling or special equities.

---

### Creditors — Community & Separate Debts 🟢
Both community property and the debtor spouse's separate property are available to satisfy any debt incurred by either spouse before or after marriage, except for one rule protecting the non-debtor spouse's pre-marital earnings: a non-debtor spouse's earnings are shielded from the other spouse's pre-marital debts if those earnings are held in a separate account in which the debtor spouse has no right of withdrawal and are not commingled with other community funds. The non-debtor spouse's separate property is never liable for the other spouse's non-necessaries debts; it may be reached only for the other spouse's contracts for necessaries (e.g., medical care) — and if community funds were available to pay that obligation, the non-debtor spouse may seek reimbursement. After divorce, a creditor may only reach the CP awarded to a spouse if that spouse incurred the debt or was assigned it by court order.
*Apply: [Creditor] seeks to satisfy [pre-marital / community] debt from [non-debtor spouse]'s [separate account / SP]; determine whether the debt is a community obligation, a necessaries obligation, or a pre-marital debt subject to the earnings-shield rule.*

Thus, the non-debtor spouse's SP is sheltered from the other's debts except in the necessaries context, and the earnings-shield protects separately held wages from pre-marital creditors.
