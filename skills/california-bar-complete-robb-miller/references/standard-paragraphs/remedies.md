## A. Tort Remedies — Legal: Damages

### Compensatory Damages (Tort) 🔴
To recover compensatory tort damages, a plaintiff must satisfy four requirements: (1) actual causation — the defendant's conduct was a but-for cause of the harm; (2) foreseeability — the type of injury suffered was a proximate, foreseeable consequence of the tortious act at the time it occurred; (3) certainty — damages must be established with sufficient definiteness and cannot be purely speculative; and (4) unavoidability — the plaintiff must take reasonable steps to mitigate, and cannot recover for losses that could have been avoided through reasonable effort. The goal is to restore the plaintiff to the position they would have occupied absent the tort.
*Apply: [Defendant's conduct] was the but-for cause of [plaintiff's injury]; the harm was foreseeable because [facts]; damages are certain/speculative because [evidence]; plaintiff [did/did not] mitigate by [actions].*

Thus, plaintiff [can/cannot] recover compensatory damages in the amount of [X].

---

### Certainty — Past vs. Future Damages (Tort) 🟡
For past economic losses in personal injury cases, the certainty requirement is satisfied by analogous historical evidence, such as prior earnings records or comparable business data. For future economic losses, plaintiff must demonstrate that they are more likely than not to occur. Non-economic losses — such as pain and suffering — are not subject to strict certainty rules; a jury may award any reasonable amount under proper instructions. Any future damages award must be discounted to present value, and under the traditional approach inflation is not factored in (the modern view does account for inflation).
*Apply: [Plaintiff's past losses] are supported by [historical evidence]; future losses are [more likely than not/speculative] because [facts]; non-economic losses include [pain/suffering/emotional distress].*

Thus, the jury [may/may not] award [past/future/non-economic] damages in the amount of [X].

---

### Nominal Damages (Tort) 🟢
Nominal damages are a small, token award — typically one dollar — granted when a plaintiff proves a legally cognizable tort but suffers no actual, measurable injury. Their purpose is to vindicate the plaintiff's rights and establish that a legal wrong occurred, not to compensate for a loss.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] suffered no quantifiable harm from [defendant's conduct], but the [tort] was technically complete; nominal damages would serve to recognize the violation.*

Thus, plaintiff is entitled to nominal damages even absent proof of actual injury.

---

### Punitive Damages (Tort) 🔴
Punitive damages are available in tort to punish and deter egregious conduct, but three prerequisites must be met: (1) the plaintiff must first receive an award of compensatory or nominal damages (or restitutionary damages) — punitive damages cannot stand alone; (2) the defendant's fault must exceed mere negligence, typically requiring malice, oppression, fraud, or conscious disregard of others' rights; and (3) the amount must bear a reasonable relationship to the actual damages, and the Supreme Court has generally cabined punitive awards to single-digit multiples of compensatory damages unless the conduct is particularly extreme.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] received [compensatory/nominal/restitutionary] damages; [defendant's conduct] was [malicious/fraudulent/oppressive] because [facts]; proposed punitive award of [X] is [proportionate/disproportionate] to actual damages of [Y].*

Thus, punitive damages [are/are not] available, and the appropriate amount is [X].

## B. Tort Remedies — Legal Restitution

### Restitutionary Damages (Tort) 🟡
Restitutionary damages in tort are grounded not in the plaintiff's loss but in the unjust enrichment of the defendant — they equal the value of the benefit the defendant gained through tortious conduct. Where both compensatory and restitutionary damages are theoretically available, the plaintiff may pursue both theories but must elect one at or before judgment; the plaintiff will typically choose whichever yields the larger sum. Punitive damages may be paired with a restitutionary award in the same way they attach to compensatory damages.
*Apply: [Defendant] received a benefit of [value] through [tortious conduct]; compensatory damages would equal [X] and restitutionary damages would equal [Y]; plaintiff should elect [the larger figure] because [facts].*

Thus, plaintiff is entitled to [compensatory/restitutionary] damages in the amount of [X].

---

### Replevin 🟢
Replevin is a legal remedy that allows a plaintiff to recover possession of specific personal property through the sheriff before trial. To prevail, the plaintiff must establish two elements: (1) a present right to possession of the particular chattel, and (2) wrongful withholding by the defendant. Procedurally, there must be a preliminary judicial hearing, the plaintiff must post a bond, and the defendant may defeat immediate recovery by posting a redelivery bond. Replevin may be combined with an award of damages for lost use or for the benefit the defendant derived during the period of wrongful possession.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] claims ownership/right to possess [specific item]; [defendant] is wrongfully holding it because [facts]; plaintiff [has/has not] posted a bond; additional damages for lost use during [period] equal [X].*

Thus, plaintiff [can/cannot] obtain replevin of [property] plus [X] in supplemental damages.

---

### Ejectment 🟢
Ejectment is the legal equivalent of replevin for real property: it allows a plaintiff who has a right to possess specific land to recover that possession through the sheriff by ousting the defendant. The plaintiff must prove (1) a right to possession of the real property and (2) that the defendant is wrongfully withholding possession — meaning the defendant must actually occupy or possess the land (for example, an adverse possessor or a holdover tenant). Ejectment does not lie against someone who merely crosses the plaintiff's property without taking possession. As with replevin, ejectment may be coupled with damages for lost use or for the benefit the defendant received during wrongful occupation.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] holds title/right to possess [property]; [defendant] is wrongfully in possession as [adverse possessor/holdover tenant/etc.]; damages for lost use during [period] equal [X].*

Thus, plaintiff [can/cannot] obtain ejectment of [defendant] from [property] plus [X] in supplemental damages.

## C. Tort Remedies — Equitable Restitution

### Constructive Trust 🔴
A constructive trust is an equitable device imposed when money damages are inadequate — typically because the property is unique or the defendant is insolvent — and the defendant holds improperly acquired property to which they have taken title. The court treats the defendant as a constructive trustee who must convey the specific property back to the plaintiff. A constructive trust is the preferred remedy when the property has appreciated in value, because it allows the plaintiff to capture that increase rather than receiving only the original value.
*Apply: [Defendant] wrongfully acquired [property] worth [X at acquisition, Y now]; money damages are inadequate because [unique/defendant insolvent]; property has [appreciated/depreciated]; plaintiff should seek constructive trust to capture [appreciation].*

Thus, a constructive trust should be imposed on [property], requiring defendant to convey it to plaintiff.

---

### Equitable Lien 🟡
An equitable lien is an alternative equitable restitutionary remedy imposed when money damages are inadequate and the defendant holds property to which they have title — but unlike a constructive trust, the court directs a forced sale of the property and pays the proceeds to the plaintiff. If the sale proceeds fall short of the property's fair market value at the time it was taken, the plaintiff receives a deficiency judgment against the defendant's other assets. The equitable lien is the better choice when the property has declined in value because it allows the plaintiff to reach the defendant's remaining assets through the deficiency.
*Apply: [Defendant] wrongfully holds [property] that has declined from [original value X] to [current value Y]; a forced sale yields [Z]; plaintiff seeks a deficiency judgment for [X − Z] against defendant's other assets.*

Thus, an equitable lien with a deficiency judgment is the appropriate remedy where property has depreciated.

---

### Tracing and BFP Cutoff 🟡
When a plaintiff pursues equitable restitution, tracing allows the plaintiff to follow their property — or its identifiable proceeds — through the defendant's hands. If the defendant has converted the original property into other assets, the plaintiff may claim those substitute assets. However, where defendant's assets cannot be traced exclusively to plaintiff's property (for example, where funds are commingled), only an equitable lien is available, not a constructive trust. Critically, a bona fide purchaser (BFP) who acquires the property for value without notice of the plaintiff's claim defeats all equitable remedies; the plaintiff has no in rem claim against the BFP but retains personal claims against the original wrongdoer and becomes a secured creditor with priority to identified property or proceeds (but stands on equal footing for any deficiency).
*Apply: [Plaintiff's property] was converted to [substitute asset] by defendant; tracing [is/is not] possible because [facts]; [third party] is [a BFP/not a BFP] because [notice/value paid].*

Thus, plaintiff [can/cannot] assert equitable remedies against [the property/third party] based on [tracing/BFP rule].

## D. Tort Remedies — Equitable: Injunctions

### Temporary Restraining Order (TRO) 🟢
A TRO is an emergency order that preserves the status quo for up to ten days pending a preliminary injunction hearing. The standard mirrors that of a preliminary injunction, but a TRO may be issued ex parte — without notice to the opposing party — if the applicant demonstrates a good-faith effort to provide notice or shows that notice itself would cause harm. Courts typically require the applicant to post a bond to compensate the opposing party if the TRO later proves unwarranted.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] seeks immediate relief to prevent [threatened harm] before a hearing can be scheduled; notice to [defendant] [was/was not] given or attempted; bond requirement is [met/an issue] because [facts].*

Thus, a TRO [should/should not] issue to preserve the status quo pending the preliminary injunction hearing.

---

### Preliminary Injunction 🔴
A preliminary injunction preserves the status quo between filing and the full trial on the merits. The moving party must establish three elements: (1) irreparable injury that would occur during the pendency of the litigation if the injunction is not issued; (2) a balance of hardships that favors the plaintiff — meaning the harm to the plaintiff if relief is denied outweighs the harm to the defendant if relief is granted; and (3) a likelihood of success on the merits. Notice and a hearing are required, and the court should impose a bond to protect the defendant against wrongful restraint.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] will suffer [irreparable harm] during litigation because [facts]; balance of hardships [favors plaintiff] because [plaintiff's harm X > defendant's burden Y]; likelihood of success [strong/weak] because [legal/factual basis].*

Thus, a preliminary injunction [should/should not] issue.

---

### Permanent Injunction — Four Elements 🔴
A permanent injunction is an equitable remedy granted after a full trial on the merits. The plaintiff must demonstrate: (1) the legal remedy is inadequate — money damages are too speculative, the defendant is insolvent, the harm is irreparable, or awarding damages would require a multiplicity of future suits; (2) enforcement is feasible — courts will not issue an injunction they cannot practically supervise or enforce; (3) the balance of hardships favors the plaintiff — the benefit to plaintiff from the injunction must outweigh the burden imposed on the defendant, though a gross disparity in favor of the defendant may defeat the injunction; and (4) no applicable equitable defenses bar relief.
*Apply: Money damages are inadequate because [speculative/insolvent/irreparable/multiplicity]; enforcement [is/is not] feasible because [supervision issues]; balance: plaintiff gains [X], defendant suffers [Y]; defenses: [laches/unclean hands/etc.].*

Thus, a permanent injunction [should/should not] issue.

---

### Injunction Defenses: Laches and Unclean Hands 🟡
Two equitable defenses commonly bar injunctive relief. Laches applies when the plaintiff unreasonably delayed in seeking relief and that delay prejudiced the defendant — delay alone is insufficient; there must be actual prejudice to the defendant from the plaintiff's inaction. Unclean hands bars relief when the plaintiff has engaged in related inequitable conduct connected to the very matter for which they seek equity; the plaintiff's wrongdoing must be sufficiently related to the dispute, not a remote or unrelated impropriety.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] delayed [X months/years] in seeking the injunction; defendant was prejudiced by [reliance/changed position]; plaintiff [did/did not] engage in related misconduct such as [facts].*

Thus, [laches/unclean hands] [bars/does not bar] injunctive relief.

---

### Injunction Defenses: Impossibility and Free Speech 🟢
Two additional equitable defenses may defeat an injunction. Impossibility defeats an injunction where compliance is physically or legally impossible — if the defendant cannot perform what the court would order, granting the injunction is futile. The free speech defense precludes injunctive relief against publication or expression where the underlying tort is defamation or invasion of privacy grounded in publication; prior restraints on speech face a high constitutional bar and courts are reluctant to enjoin expression even where damages may be available.
*Apply: [Defendant] [cannot comply/compliance is impossible] because [physical/legal facts]; the threatened publication involves [speech/expression] on matter of [public/private concern], raising prior-restraint concerns.*

Thus, [impossibility/free speech] [does/does not] bar the requested injunction.

## E. Contract Remedies — Legal: Damages

### Compensatory Damages — Contract Requirements 🔴
Contract compensatory damages require the same four predicates as tort: (1) actual causation — the breach caused the loss; (2) foreseeability — the damages must have been within the reasonable contemplation of the parties at the time of contracting, not merely at the time of breach; (3) certainty — losses must be calculable with reasonable certainty; and (4) mitigation — the non-breaching party must take reasonable steps to avoid further loss and may not recover for avoidable damages.
*Apply: [Defendant's breach] caused [plaintiff's losses]; those losses were foreseeable at contract formation because [facts]; losses are certain/speculative because [evidence]; plaintiff [did/did not] mitigate by [actions].*

Thus, plaintiff [can/cannot] recover compensatory damages in the amount of [X].

---

### Direct (Expectation) Damages 🔴
Direct damages — also called expectation damages — are the standard contract remedy; they place the non-breaching party in the economic position they would have occupied had the contract been fully performed. In a sale of goods or services context, this typically means the difference between the contract price and the cost of obtaining substitute performance, plus any losses flowing directly from the nonperformance. For a buyer, expectation damages equal the difference between the market price and the contract price at the time and place of breach.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] expected [contract price/performance]; breach left plaintiff with [lesser position]; direct damages = [market/substitute cost − contract price] = [X].*

Thus, plaintiff's direct expectation damages equal [X].

---

### Consequential Damages (Hadley Rule) 🔴
Consequential damages are recoverable for losses that do not flow inherently from every breach of that type of contract but instead arise from the plaintiff's particular circumstances — provided those circumstances were communicated to, and thus reasonably foreseeable by, the defendant at the time of contracting (the Hadley v. Baxendale rule). Lost profits, lost business reputation, and downstream losses are classic consequential damages. If the special circumstances were not communicated at the time of contracting, such damages are cut off.
*Apply: [Plaintiff's consequential losses] — such as [lost profits/lost reputation] — arose from [plaintiff's particular situation]; [defendant] [was/was not] informed of those circumstances at contracting because [facts].*

Thus, consequential damages of [X] [are/are not] recoverable.

---

### Incidental and Nominal Damages (Contract) 🟢
Incidental damages in contract cover the reasonable costs the non-breaching party incurs in dealing with the breach itself — for example, costs of finding a substitute, storing goods, or covering a transaction. They are routinely awarded alongside direct and consequential damages. Nominal damages are available in contract when the breach is proved but no actual loss results; they acknowledge the breach without compensating for a nonexistent injury.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] incurred [costs of cover/storage/transaction] of [X] in response to the breach; no actual economic loss [was/was not] suffered.*

Thus, plaintiff may recover [incidental damages of X / nominal damages] in addition to any direct damages.

---

### Punitive Damages — Not Available in Contract 🔴
Pure breach of contract, standing alone, does not support an award of punitive damages regardless of how willful or deliberate the breach. The policy rationale is that contract law aims at compensation and efficient breach, not punishment. If the same conduct also constitutes an independent tort — such as fraud, conversion, or bad faith — punitive damages may be available under the tort theory, but not through the contract claim itself.
*Apply: [Defendant's conduct] was [willful/deliberate] but arose solely from a contractual obligation; plaintiff seeks punitive damages; [any independent tort — e.g., fraud — does/does not] exist on these facts.*

Thus, punitive damages are [unavailable under the contract claim / available if plaintiff pleads the independent tort of X].

---

### Liquidated Damages 🟡
A liquidated damages clause is enforceable if, at the time of contracting, (1) actual damages were difficult or impossible to ascertain with certainty, and (2) the stipulated sum represented a reasonable forecast of the likely harm — it was not a penalty designed to coerce performance. If the clause is valid, the plaintiff recovers only the liquidated sum, not actual damages, even if actual damages are higher or lower. If the clause is deemed an unenforceable penalty — because the amount is grossly disproportionate to any probable loss — the clause is stricken and the plaintiff is left to prove actual damages.
*Apply: At contract formation, damages from [this type of breach] were [difficult to ascertain] because [facts]; the stipulated amount of [X] [was/was not] a reasonable estimate because [comparison to actual damages Y]; clause [is/is not] a penalty.*

Thus, the liquidated damages clause [is enforceable at X / is void as a penalty, and plaintiff recovers actual damages of Y].

## F. Contract Remedies — Restitution

### Restitution — Unenforceable Contract 🟡
When a contract is unenforceable — for example, due to the Statute of Frauds, lack of capacity, or illegality — and one party has partially or fully performed, that party may seek restitution for the value of the benefit conferred on the other party, measured by its fair market value. Importantly, the plaintiff is not capped by the unenforceable contract rate; if the benefit's market value exceeds what the contract would have paid, the plaintiff recovers the higher market value. If the benefit took the form of specific property that is unique or the defendant is insolvent, the plaintiff may seek return of the property itself.
*Apply: The contract was unenforceable because [Statute of Frauds/incapacity/etc.]; plaintiff conferred a benefit valued at [market X], which [exceeds/equals/falls below] the unenforceable contract rate of [Y]; plaintiff [seeks return of unique property/seeks money restitution].*

Thus, plaintiff recovers [X] in restitution regardless of the unenforceable contract rate.

---

### Restitution — Non-Breaching Party After Breach 🟡
When a contract is breached and the plaintiff is the non-breaching party who has already rendered some performance, the plaintiff may elect restitution as an alternative to expectation damages. Restitutionary recovery equals the fair market value of the benefit conferred — and is not capped by the contract price. This is advantageous when the plaintiff performed at a loss (i.e., the contract rate was below market) because restitution allows recovery of the higher market value. Again, if the benefit involved specific property that is unique or the defendant is insolvent, the plaintiff may recover the property itself.
*Apply: [Plaintiff] (non-breaching party) performed [services/delivered goods] worth [market value X]; contract rate was [Y]; restitution [exceeds/falls below] expectation damages; plaintiff elects [restitution/expectation] because [X > or < Y].*

Thus, the non-breaching plaintiff recovers [X] in restitution.

---

### Restitution — Breaching Party 🟡
Under the traditional rule, a breaching party could recover nothing for part performance already rendered, on the theory that one should not profit from one's own wrong. The modern and majority view (followed in CA) permits the breaching party to recover the value of any benefit conferred on the non-breaching party, subject to two caps: (1) the recovery cannot exceed the contract rate (i.e., the pro-rata share of the contract price for work completed), and (2) it is reduced by any damages the breach caused the non-breaching party. A breaching plaintiff will not be left with a windfall but also will not suffer total forfeiture.
*Apply: [Plaintiff-breacher] partially performed, conferring a benefit of [market value X]; contract rate for completed work = [Y]; non-breaching party suffered damages of [Z] from the breach; breaching party recovers [min(X, Y) − Z].*

Thus, even the breaching party recovers [amount] in restitution under the modern rule.

## G. Contract Remedies — Equitable

### Specific Performance — Five Requirements 🔴
Specific performance is an equitable order compelling a party to perform their contractual obligations. Five requirements must all be satisfied: (1) a valid, certain, and definite contract exists; (2) the plaintiff has performed or tendered all conditions of their own obligations (or has a valid excuse); (3) the legal remedy is inadequate — money damages are too speculative, the defendant is insolvent, the injury is irreparable, or the parties have a history of litigation making repetitive suits an undue burden; (4) enforcement is feasible — the court can practically supervise compliance; and (5) no applicable defense bars relief, including unclean hands, laches, unconscionability (assessed at contract formation), or contract defenses such as mistake, misrepresentation, or the Statute of Frauds.
*Apply: Contract [is/is not] valid, certain, and definite; plaintiff [has/has not] performed their obligations; legal remedy is inadequate because [unique property/insolvent defendant/speculative damages]; enforcement [feasible/problematic] because [supervision issues]; defenses: [none/laches/unclean hands/etc.].*

Thus, specific performance [should/should not] be ordered.

---

### Specific Performance — Real Property and Personal Property Uniqueness 🔴
Real property is always considered unique for purposes of specific performance, regardless of whether identical parcels exist nearby. This means a buyer of real estate is presumptively entitled to specific performance; and under the special seller's rule, a seller of land may also obtain specific performance against a defaulting buyer — even though the seller would only be receiving money — because the seller has a legitimate interest in being relieved of the obligation. For personal property, the uniqueness standard is more demanding: the chattel must be one-of-a-kind, extremely rare, of particular personal significance to the buyer, or rendered unique by surrounding circumstances. Uniqueness for personal property is assessed as of the time of litigation, not the time of contracting.
*Apply: [Property] is [real/personal]; if real property, buyer [is/seller is also] entitled to specific performance; if personal property, [chattel] [is/is not] unique because [one-of-a-kind/severe shortage/personal significance] as of the time of [litigation].*

Thus, [real/personal] property [does/does not] satisfy the uniqueness requirement for specific performance.

---

### Specific Performance — Personal Services and Non-Compete Covenants 🟡
Courts will not specifically enforce personal services contracts — they will not order a person to work for another — both because of practical enforcement difficulties and constitutional concerns about involuntary servitude. However, if the employee's services are unique, a court may enforce a covenant not to compete ancillary to a personal services contract as a negative injunction, preventing the employee from working for a competitor. The covenant must be reasonable in scope — including reasonable limitations on geographic area, duration, and subject matter — and must not impose an undue hardship. California, notably, disfavors non-compete clauses as a general matter, and courts there scrutinize them closely.
*Apply: [Employee/performer] has unique skills; personal services contract [will not be specifically enforced]; covenant not to compete [exists/lacks] reasonable geographic [X], duration [Y], and subject matter [Z] restrictions; CA's strong public policy against non-competes [applies/is an issue].*

Thus, specific performance of the personal services contract is denied, but the non-compete [can/cannot] be enforced as a negative injunction.

---

### Rescission 🔴
Rescission is an equitable remedy that voids a voidable contract and restores the parties to their pre-contract positions. To obtain rescission, the plaintiff must establish: (1) a recognized ground for rescission — such as mutual mistake of a material fact, unilateral mistake where the other party knew or should have known of it, misrepresentation (requiring actual reliance by the plaintiff), or other formation defect showing lack of genuine assent; and (2) no applicable defense — laches or unclean hands may bar relief, but the plaintiff's own negligence in making the mistake is not a defense to rescission. An important election-of-remedies rule applies: if the plaintiff first sues for breach of contract damages, that election bars a subsequent rescission claim; but suing for rescission first does not bar a later damages claim, and a party may plead both simultaneously as long as they elect one before judgment. If the plaintiff has already rendered performance, restitution of that performance may be combined with rescission.
*Apply: Grounds for rescission: [mutual mistake of material fact / unilateral mistake known to defendant / misrepresentation with actual reliance]; defenses: [laches/unclean hands]; plaintiff [has/has not] first sought damages (election issue); restitution of [prior performance] also sought.*

Thus, rescission [is/is not] available, and restitution [accompanies it / is unavailable because plaintiff already elected damages].

---

### Reformation 🟡
Reformation is an equitable remedy that rewrites a written contract to conform to the parties' actual agreement when the writing inaccurately expresses their true intent. Three elements are required: (1) there was a valid underlying contract — a genuine meeting of the minds on the terms; (2) there are recognized grounds for reformation, which include mutual mistake (both parties were wrong about what the writing said or meant) or misrepresentation by one party that caused the other to sign an inaccurate document; unilateral mistake alone does not support reformation unless the other party knew of the mistake; and (3) no equitable defenses apply, such as laches or unclean hands. Reformation is unavailable against a bona fide purchaser who relies on the written instrument.
*Apply: The parties actually agreed on [term X]; the written contract erroneously states [term Y]; this resulted from [mutual mistake / misrepresentation by defendant]; [defendant/third party] [did/did not] know of the mistake; defenses: [laches/unclean hands/BFP issue].*

Thus, reformation [should/should not] be granted to correct [the written term] to reflect [the actual agreed term].
