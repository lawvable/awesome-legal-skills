## A. Competence, Diligence & Communication

### Competence 🟢
An attorney must provide competent representation, meaning the attorney possesses and applies the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation reasonably necessary for the particular matter. Under ABA Model Rule 1.1, a lawyer may achieve the requisite competence by studying the area of law, so long as doing so imposes no undue expense or delay on the client, or by associating with a lawyer already competent in that area. The California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) Rule 1.1 tracks this standard. Neither set of rules permits an attorney simply to charge ahead in unfamiliar territory without taking curative steps.
*Apply: [Attorney] undertook [matter] despite [lacking knowledge/skill], and [did/did not] take steps to become competent or associate with qualified counsel.*
> **CA divergence:** None material — both ABA and CRPC impose the same competence floor; the distinction arises in the malpractice standard (see Malpractice block).

Thus, [attorney's conduct] [satisfies/violates] the duty of competence under both ABA MR 1.1 and CRPC 1.1.

---

### Diligence 🟢
An attorney must pursue each representation with reasonable diligence and promptness; a lawyer may not let a matter languish or drag to a close, and must not neglect the client's interests. ABA Model Rule 1.3 frames this as pursuing "with reasonable diligence and promptness." CRPC 1.3 employs identical language. Diligence encompasses zealous advocacy within the bounds of the rules but does not authorize improper delay tactics.
*Apply: [Attorney] [delayed filing/failed to respond/let deadline pass] in [matter], which [did/did not] reflect reasonable diligence.*
> **CA divergence:** CA additionally prohibits delay tactics designed to harass an adversary or to serve the attorney's personal convenience or financial gain (see also Expediting Cases block).

Thus, [attorney's conduct] [satisfies/violates] the duty of diligence under ABA MR 1.3 and CRPC 1.3.

---

### Scope of Representation — Client vs. Attorney Decision-Making 🟢
The allocation of decision-making authority between an attorney and client follows a substantive-versus-procedural divide. The client retains ultimate authority over objectives and decisions that concern the client's substantive legal rights: whether to testify in a criminal case, whether to waive a jury trial, whether to accept a settlement offer in a civil case, and whether to enter a plea. The attorney controls decisions relating to legal strategy and litigation procedure: which motions to file, what witnesses to call, which discovery to seek, and how to frame legal arguments. An attorney may limit the scope of a representation if the client gives informed consent and the limitation is reasonable under the circumstances. ABA Model Rule 1.2 and CRPC 1.2 both reflect this allocation.
*Apply: [Attorney] [made a decision/declined to follow instruction] regarding [settlement/strategy/procedural step], and the question is whether that decision belonged to [attorney] or [client].*
> **CA divergence:** None material; both ABA MR 1.2 and CRPC 1.2 draw the same substantive/procedural line and permit scope limitations with informed consent.

Thus, [attorney's] [decision/refusal to act] [was/was not] within the attorney's proper zone of authority under ABA MR 1.2 / CRPC 1.2.

### Communication 🔴
An attorney must keep a client reasonably informed about the status of the matter and must promptly comply with reasonable requests for information. Under ABA Model Rule 1.4, the attorney must also promptly notify the client of any decision requiring the client's informed consent and must explain matters sufficiently to allow the client to make informed decisions. California's 2018 update to CRPC 1.4 is broader: in addition to keeping the client reasonably informed about significant developments, the California rule expressly requires the attorney to (1) promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance requiring disclosure or the client's informed consent; (2) reasonably consult with the client about the client's objectives and how to achieve them; and (3) advise the client of any limitations on the lawyer's conduct, including those methods the client requested that the rules prohibit.
*Apply: [Attorney] [failed to inform/return calls/notify of settlement offer] to [client] regarding [matter].*
> **CA divergence:** CRPC 1.4's 2018 amendment explicitly adds the obligation to consult about objectives and to flag limitations on permissible conduct — broader than plain ABA MR 1.4 text.

Thus, [attorney's conduct] [satisfies/violates] the duty of communication under ABA MR 1.4 and CRPC 1.4.

## B. Confidentiality & Attorney-Client Privilege

### Duty of Confidentiality — Scope 🔴
The duty of confidentiality is an ethical obligation that prohibits an attorney from voluntarily disclosing any information relating to the representation of a client, regardless of whether the client requested secrecy or whether disclosure would actually harm the client. Under ABA Model Rule 1.6, this duty is vast: it covers all information relating to the representation, from whatever source. CRPC 1.6 and California Business & Professions Code § 6068(e) impose the same broad obligation. The duty attaches even before a formal attorney-client relationship is formed — consultation for the purpose of seeking representation triggers confidentiality — and continues beyond the end of the representation, including after the client's death.
*Apply: [Attorney] disclosed [information] about [client] to [third party] outside the representation.*
> **CA divergence:** None in scope — both are extremely broad. The critical CA divergence appears in the exceptions (see below).

Thus, disclosing information relating to the representation without an applicable exception violates the duty of confidentiality under both ABA MR 1.6 and CRPC 1.6 / Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e).

---

### Confidentiality — Exceptions (ABA vs. CA) 🔴
ABA Model Rule 1.6(b) lists several permissive disclosure exceptions: the attorney *may* (but is not required to) reveal information to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm; to prevent the client from committing a crime or fraud likely to cause substantial financial injury; to mitigate such injury; to secure legal advice about compliance; to establish a claim or defense in a dispute with the client; or when compelled by court order or law. California's rule is materially narrower and imposes a duty rather than mere permission in the most serious case: under Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e)(2) and CRPC 1.6(b)(1), a California attorney *may* reveal — but is not required to — confidential information to the extent the attorney *reasonably believes* disclosure is *necessary* to prevent a criminal act the attorney reasonably believes is likely to result in death or substantial bodily harm. Before doing so, the California attorney must first make a good-faith effort to persuade the client not to commit the act and must inform the client of the attorney's decision to disclose. Crucially, California's exception covers only prevention of death or substantial bodily harm, not financial crimes or fraud — unlike ABA's broader menu of exceptions.
*Apply: [Attorney] learned that [client] plans to [crime/act] that would [result in harm] to [party].*
> **CA divergence:** CA is significantly narrower — no financial-crime exception, and disclosure requires a prior good-faith attempt to dissuade the client. ABA MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3) permits disclosure to prevent financial harm; CA does not.

Thus, [attorney's disclosure/non-disclosure] [is/is not] justified under [ABA MR 1.6 / CRPC 1.6 / § 6068(e)].

### Distinguishing Confidentiality from Attorney-Client Privilege 🟡
The duty of confidentiality and the attorney-client privilege are related but distinct. The attorney-client privilege is an evidentiary rule that protects confidential communications made between an attorney and client for the purpose of obtaining or rendering legal advice from compelled disclosure in legal proceedings; it belongs to the client and may be waived only by the client. The duty of confidentiality is an ethical rule far broader in scope: it protects all information relating to the representation — not merely privileged communications — and governs the attorney's voluntary disclosures outside the courtroom. An ethical violation of the confidentiality duty does not, by itself, create or destroy privilege, and privilege can exist independently of the ethical duty. On bar essays, issues of privilege arise under the law of evidence; issues of the ethical duty arise under professional responsibility rules.
*Apply: [Attorney] was asked [in deposition/by court order/by third party] to reveal [communication/information] regarding [client's matter].*
> **CA divergence:** California's statutory privilege (Evid. Code §§ 950–962) is broader than federal common law privilege and includes corporate clients and prospective clients; the ethical duty under § 6068(e) and CRPC 1.6 is coextensive in scope.

Thus, [the communication] [is/is not] protected by the attorney-client privilege, and separately, [attorney's disclosure] [does/does not] violate the ethical duty of confidentiality.

### Confidentiality — Compelled Disclosure & Self-Defense Exceptions 🟢
An attorney may disclose confidential information when compelled to do so by a valid court order, applicable law, or ethical rules themselves. Additionally, an attorney may reveal information to the extent reasonably necessary to establish a claim against the client (e.g., to collect an unpaid fee) or to defend against a claim by the client (e.g., a malpractice suit), or to respond to allegations in a disciplinary proceeding. Both ABA MR 1.6(b)(5)–(6) and CRPC 1.6(b)(3)–(5) recognize these exceptions. The disclosure is limited to what is reasonably necessary for the specific purpose.
*Apply: [Attorney] disclosed [information] to [court/disciplinary authority/in fee dispute] in response to [client's claim/court order].*
> **CA divergence:** None significant; both sets of rules recognize compelled disclosure and self-defense exceptions.

Thus, [attorney's disclosure] [is/is not] permitted under the compelled-disclosure or self-defense exception to confidentiality.

## C. Conflicts of Interest

### Concurrent Conflicts — Current Client vs. Current Client 🔴
An attorney may not represent a client whose interests are directly adverse to another current client (an actual conflict), even in an unrelated matter, unless both clients give informed written consent and the attorney reasonably believes effective representation of each is possible. Under ABA Model Rule 1.7, the test for a concurrent conflict is (1) whether the representation of one client is directly adverse to another current client, or (2) whether a significant risk exists that the representation will be materially limited by the attorney's responsibilities to another client, a third person, or by the attorney's own interests. If an actual conflict exists, consent is insufficient unless the attorney can honestly conclude that competent, diligent representation is possible for each client simultaneously. CRPC 1.7 parallels this framework but frames the test as whether there is a "significant risk" of material limitation. Regardless of consent, representation is categorically impermissible where prohibited by law or where the matter involves a dispute between the clients themselves.
*Apply: [Attorney] simultaneously represented [Client A] and [Client B], whose interests were [adverse/potentially conflicting] in [matter or separate matter].*
> **CA divergence:** Under CRPC 1.7, the imputed-disqualification rules differ in remedy — a California attorney will not be disciplined for another firm member's imputed conflict so long as the attorney did not personally participate (see Imputed Disqualification block).

Thus, [attorney's] dual representation [does/does not] constitute an impermissible concurrent conflict under ABA MR 1.7 / CRPC 1.7.

---

### Former Client Conflicts 🟡
Once a representation concludes, an attorney must not represent a new client in the same or a substantially related matter in which that new client's interests are materially adverse to those of the former client, unless the former client gives informed written consent. ABA Model Rule 1.9 and CRPC 1.9 both define "substantially related" by reference to whether confidential information obtained during the former representation would normally have been relevant to the present matter — if so, the court presumes that such information was in fact obtained, creating a disqualifying conflict. The duty not to use or reveal a former client's confidential information persists indefinitely.
*Apply: [Attorney] seeks to represent [new client] against [former client] in a matter that [is/may be] substantially related to the prior representation.*
> **CA divergence:** None structural; both ABA and CA use the substantial-relationship test. CA tracks federal disqualification doctrine closely.

Thus, [attorney's] representation of [new client] against [former client] [is/is not] barred by the former-client conflict rule under ABA MR 1.9 / CRPC 1.9.

### Prospective Clients 🟡
A person who consults an attorney for the purpose of obtaining legal services qualifies as a "prospective client" even if no engagement is formed. Under ABA Model Rule 1.18 and CRPC 1.18 (2018), the attorney owes the prospective client a duty of confidentiality as to any information disclosed during the consultation. More critically, if the prospective client revealed information material to the matter and that information is confidential, the attorney is disqualified from representing a client with materially adverse interests in the same or substantially related matter. A prospective client does not include a person who communicates unilaterally without a reasonable expectation the attorney will consider being retained, or who communicates in bad faith without genuinely seeking legal advice.
*Apply: [Attorney] consulted with [potential client] who disclosed [material information], then sought to represent [adverse party] in the same matter.*
> **CA divergence:** CRPC 1.18 was added in the 2018 update and tracks ABA MR 1.18. Screen-and-disclose procedures can cure imputation to firm colleagues.

Thus, [attorney's] conflict based on the prospective-client consultation [bars/does not bar] the subsequent representation.

### Personal-Interest Conflicts & Business Transactions with Clients 🔴
An attorney must not enter into a business transaction with a client or knowingly acquire a pecuniary interest adverse to a client unless (1) the terms of the transaction are fair and reasonable to the client, (2) those terms are fully disclosed and transmitted in a writing the client can understand, (3) the client is advised in writing that independent counsel is advisable and given a reasonable opportunity to seek such counsel, and (4) the client provides informed written consent in a signed writing. ABA Model Rule 1.8(a) and CRPC 1.8.1 both impose these four requirements. Violations are taken seriously because the attorney's superior knowledge and the client's trust create a structurally unequal bargaining position.
*Apply: [Attorney] entered into [business deal/loan/property purchase] with [client] without [fair terms/full disclosure/written consent/opportunity for independent counsel].*
> **CA divergence:** Under CRPC 1.8.3, California prohibits an attorney from inducing a substantial gift from a client or drafting an instrument conveying such a gift unless the client is related to the attorney. ABA MR 1.8(c) prohibits both inducing and drafting. California's rule on loans differs: CRPC 1.8.5 permits an attorney to lend money to a client for any purpose after the representation has commenced, provided there is a written promise to repay — broader than ABA, which restricts advances to litigation expenses only.

Thus, [attorney's] [business transaction/loan/gift arrangement] with [client] [complies with/violates] the personal-interest conflict rules under ABA MR 1.8 / CRPC 1.8.1.

### Gifts from Clients 🔴
An attorney may not solicit a substantial gift from a client, nor draft any legal instrument — a will, trust, deed, or similar document — that conveys a substantial gift to the attorney or to the attorney's family member, unless the client is the attorney's relative. ABA Model Rule 1.8(c) prohibits both acts. California's CRPC 1.8.3 prohibits only inducing a client to make a substantial gift; unlike the ABA rule, it does not prohibit drafting the instrument, though it does prohibit preparing legal instruments that give the attorney or a related person a gift where the client is not a blood relative or registered domestic partner.
*Apply: [Attorney] drafted a will/trust for [client] naming [attorney/attorney's family] as a substantial beneficiary, and [client] is/is not a relative.*
> **CA divergence:** ABA bars drafting such instruments for non-relatives; CA's CRPC 1.8.3 focuses on inducing the gift, not on drafting. In practice, the conduct often triggers both prohibitions, but on a CA essay, note the distinction.

Thus, [attorney's] act of [drafting/soliciting] a substantial gift [does/does not] violate ABA MR 1.8(c) and/or CRPC 1.8.3.

### Sexual Relations with Clients 🔴
An attorney is strictly prohibited from engaging in sexual relations with a current client unless a consensual sexual relationship already existed between them when the attorney-client relationship commenced. CRPC 1.8.10 (2018 update) codifies this bright-line prohibition in California. ABA Model Rule 1.8(j) adopts the same rule. The prohibition exists because the inherent imbalance of power in the attorney-client relationship creates serious risks of exploitation, impaired judgment, and divided loyalty — harms that cannot be consented away after the fact.
*Apply: [Attorney] entered into a sexual relationship with [client] after the representation commenced, and no prior relationship existed.*
> **CA divergence:** None material — both ABA and CA adopted identical prohibitions, and both grandfather pre-existing relationships.

Thus, [attorney's] sexual relationship with [client] [does/does not] violate ABA MR 1.8(j) / CRPC 1.8.10.

### Publication Rights Contracts with Clients 🔴
An attorney may not acquire literary or media rights — rights to a client's story, publication rights, or similar interests — based on information relating to the representation, before the representation has concluded. ABA Model Rule 1.8(d) and CRPC 1.8.7 both impose this prohibition. The concern is that the attorney's independent judgment on case strategy will be skewed by the prospect of a commercially valuable story, potentially to the client's detriment. Once the representation has fully ended, the prohibition no longer applies.
*Apply: [Attorney] negotiated or accepted rights to [client's story/film rights/book deal] while the underlying representation was still pending.*
> **CA divergence:** California discourages such arrangements even after representation, and CRPC 1.8.7 contains a nuanced rule: a California attorney may acquire such rights after representation ends, but only if the court (in the event of a challenge) is satisfied that the client clearly understood the terms and consented. ABA MR 1.8(d) prohibits the acquisition only during the representation, with no post-representation restrictions.

Thus, [attorney's] acquisition of [publication/media rights] [does/does not] violate ABA MR 1.8(d) / CRPC 1.8.7.

### Loans and Advances to Clients 🔴
Under ABA Model Rule 1.8(e), an attorney may not provide financial assistance to a client in connection with pending or contemplated litigation, except (1) the attorney may advance court costs and expenses of litigation, with repayment contingent on the outcome of the matter; and (2) an attorney representing an indigent client may pay court costs outright without any repayment obligation. California's rule is significantly broader: CRPC 1.8.5 permits an attorney to lend money to a client for any purpose — not just litigation expenses — after the representation has commenced, provided there is a written promise from the client to repay. Unlike the ABA rule, California does not limit loans to litigation-related costs. Both jurisdictions prohibit an attorney from promising to pay a client's debts in order to induce the client to retain the attorney.
*Apply: [Attorney] [loaned money to/paid personal debts of/advanced non-litigation funds for] [client] during the course of the representation.*
> **CA divergence:** This is a major CA/ABA split. ABA limits advances to litigation costs (plus indigent exception). California permits any-purpose loans during an existing representation, provided a written IOU exists. California also prohibits promises to pay client debts to gain the engagement (both jurisdictions agree on that prohibition).

Thus, [attorney's] financial assistance to [client] [is/is not] permissible under ABA MR 1.8(e) / CRPC 1.8.5.

### Prospective Limitation on Malpractice Liability 🔴
An attorney may not enter into an agreement with a client that prospectively limits the attorney's liability to the client for malpractice, or that requires the client to waive the right to bring a malpractice claim, unless permitted by law and the client is independently represented in making the agreement. ABA Model Rule 1.8(h)(1) and CRPC 1.8.8(a) both prohibit prospective waivers of malpractice liability. The rationale is that clients in attorney-client relationships are presumed to be in a position of informational disadvantage and are ill-positioned to evaluate such waivers. By contrast, an attorney may settle a claim or a potential claim for past malpractice directly with a client, provided the client is advised in writing of the right to seek independent counsel and given a reasonable opportunity to do so.
*Apply: [Attorney] included a [clause/provision] in the [retainer/engagement agreement] purporting to limit [attorney's] liability for malpractice or to waive [client's] right to sue.*
> **CA divergence:** None material; both ABA MR 1.8(h) and CRPC 1.8.8 prohibit prospective waivers while permitting settlement of existing claims with proper notice and opportunity for independent counsel.

Thus, [attorney's] prospective malpractice waiver provision [does/does not] violate ABA MR 1.8(h) / CRPC 1.8.8.

### Lawyer as Necessary Witness at Trial 🔴
An attorney may not act as an advocate in a trial in which the attorney is likely to be a necessary witness, subject to limited exceptions. ABA Model Rule 3.7 permits the attorney to testify (and continue as trial counsel) if (1) the testimony relates to an uncontested matter; (2) the testimony concerns the nature and value of legal services rendered in the case; or (3) disqualification would work substantial hardship on the client. The prohibition on serving as both advocate and witness reflects the risk that the jury will be confused about whether the attorney is speaking as counsel or as a fact witness, and that the attorney's credibility will be assessed rather than the client's case. CRPC 3.7 tracks the ABA rule but with one notable California divergence: California permits an attorney to testify before any factfinder other than a jury (e.g., a bench trial or arbitration) if the client provides informed written consent.
*Apply: [Attorney] sought to both advocate for [client] and testify as a fact witness in [jury trial/bench trial/arbitration].*
> **CA divergence:** California allows advocate-witness overlap in bench trials and arbitrations with written client consent. ABA MR 3.7's hardship exception is available in both, but California's consent pathway is broader.

Thus, [attorney's] dual role as advocate and witness [is/is not] permissible under ABA MR 3.7 / CRPC 3.7.

### Close Relationships Between Counsel — Same Matter 🟡
When attorneys who are closely related — spouses, domestic partners, or other intimate partners — represent opposing parties in the same matter, a potential conflict of interest arises that requires disclosure and consent. ABA Model Rule 1.7, Comment [11], recognizes that immediate family relationships between opposing counsel may create loyalty or confidentiality risks that materially limit each attorney's representation. CRPC 1.7 and its official comment address the same concern. Neither ABA nor CA treats close-relationship conflicts as automatically imputed to other firm members — this is a personal conflict only, not an imputed one. California extends the concern beyond spouses to any "intimate relationship," covering a broader range of personal ties.
*Apply: [Attorney A] and [Attorney B], who are [spouses/domestic partners/in an intimate relationship], represent opposing parties in [matter].*
> **CA divergence:** California extends the conflict concern to any intimate relationship, not just marriage or registered domestic partnership. No imputed disqualification applies to firm colleagues in either jurisdiction for this type of conflict.

Thus, the close relationship between [Attorney A] and [Attorney B] [creates/does not create] a conflict requiring disclosure and written consent under ABA MR 1.7 / CRPC 1.7.

### Imputed Disqualification 🔴
When one attorney in a firm has a conflict of interest, that conflict is ordinarily imputed to every other attorney in the firm, because the firm functions as a single entity sharing information and loyalty. Under ABA Model Rule 1.10, imputed disqualification applies to all "associated" lawyers. Two exceptions limit imputation: first, if the conflicted attorney is screened from the matter, receives no portion of the fee in that matter, and the former client receives prompt written notice and periodic certifications of compliance, imputation may be avoided. Second, if the conflict is based solely on the conflicted attorney's personal interests (not on confidential client information), imputation does not apply. Under California's CRPC 1.10, the framework is similar but with a critical disciplinary distinction: a California attorney will not be personally disciplined for the conflict of a fellow firm member if the attorney did not personally participate in or know of the violation.
*Apply: [Attorney A], a member of [firm], has a conflict with [party/client]; [Attorney B] at the same firm seeks to represent [adverse party].*
> **CA divergence:** California's CRPC 1.10 limits the discipline a non-participating lawyer faces for a firm-mate's conflict. Under both ABA and CA, screening can cure imputed disqualification when a lateral attorney brings a conflict into the firm, subject to notice requirements.

Thus, [Attorney B's] representation [is/is not] imputed to [Attorney A's] conflict, subject to [screening/consent] under ABA MR 1.10 / CRPC 1.10.

### Former Government Attorney 🟡
An attorney who previously worked for the government and personally and substantially participated in a specific matter may not later represent a private client in that same matter. ABA Model Rule 1.11 and California's analog both prohibit this "revolving door" representation. The prohibition is matter-specific: general policymaking or regulatory work does not constitute a "matter," which requires a specific dispute between identifiable parties. The former government attorney's conflict may be cured for firm colleagues through screening, notification of the government agency, and the attorney's receipt of no fee from the matter.
*Apply: [Attorney] was a government [prosecutor/regulator/agency lawyer] who worked on [specific matter] and now seeks to represent [private party] in the same matter.*
> **CA divergence:** California has not formally adopted ABA MR 1.11 in full, but CRPC 1.11 addresses the issue. California separately and explicitly prohibits a former criminal prosecutor from participating in the defense of the same matter.

Thus, [attorney's] prior government work [does/does not] bar private representation under ABA MR 1.11 / CRPC 1.11.

### Conflicts — Third-Party Interference 🟢
An attorney's duty of loyalty runs exclusively to the client; no third party may direct or impair that loyalty. Compensation from a third party is permissible only with the client's informed consent, provided the third party does not interfere with the attorney's independence or the attorney-client relationship. When representing an organization, the attorney's client is the entity itself — not its officers, shareholders, or employees — and the attorney must act in the entity's best interests even when those conflict with the interests of its constituents.
*Apply: [Third party] is [paying attorney's fees/instructing attorney] on behalf of [client], creating potential interference with independent judgment.*
> **CA divergence:** None structural; both ABA MR 1.8(f) and CRPC 1.8.6 permit third-party payment subject to informed consent and non-interference requirements.

Thus, [third-party compensation/direction] [does/does not] violate the attorney's duties of loyalty and independence under ABA MR 1.8(f) / CRPC 1.8.6.

## D. Fees

### Reasonableness of Fees 🔴
An attorney's fee must be reasonable, determined by reference to the totality of circumstances including: the time and labor required; the difficulty of the matter; the skill needed; the fee customarily charged in the locality for similar services; the amount involved and results obtained; the time limitations imposed; the nature and length of the professional relationship; and the experience, reputation, and ability of the attorney. Under ABA Model Rule 1.5(a), the standard is simply "reasonable." California Business & Professions Code § 6147–6148 and CRPC 1.5 hold that fees must not be "unconscionably high" — a higher threshold than "reasonable" that is harder to trigger, though unreasonable fees can constitute a lesser violation. California also requires the attorney to agree to submit the fee dispute to binding arbitration if the client requests it.
*Apply: [Attorney] charged [client] $[amount] for [services], which appears [excessive/reasonable] in light of [circumstances].*
> **CA divergence:** ABA uses a "reasonableness" standard; California uses "unconscionably high" as the disciplinary floor — making it harder to find a fee violation in California, though the unconscionability inquiry is separate from a civil claim for excessive fees.

Thus, [attorney's] fee [is/is not] reasonable under ABA MR 1.5 / [unconscionable under] CRPC 1.5 and Bus. & Prof. Code § 6148.

### Written Fee Agreements 🔴
California imposes written fee agreement requirements far more broadly than the ABA. Under ABA Model Rule 1.5(b), a new attorney-client relationship's fee basis should be communicated, preferably in writing, but a written agreement is mandatory only for contingent fee arrangements. Under California Business & Professions Code § 6148, a written fee agreement is required for any matter likely to involve fees exceeding $1,000, and the agreement must specify the basis for the fee, the services to be rendered, and the responsibilities of each party. The written agreement requirement is excused in California only if (1) the fee is under $1,000, (2) the client is a corporation, (3) the services are routine for a regular client, or (4) an emergency or impracticality makes it unreasonable. Failure to comply with § 6148 renders the fee agreement voidable at the client's election, though the attorney may recover in quantum meruit.
*Apply: [Attorney] entered a verbal fee arrangement with [client] for [amount/type of matter] without a written agreement.*
> **CA divergence:** This is a major CA/ABA split. The ABA generally requires writing only for contingent fees; California § 6148 requires writing for virtually any substantive matter.

Thus, [attorney's] failure to provide a written fee agreement [does/does not] violate California Bus. & Prof. Code § 6148 / ABA MR 1.5(b).

### Contingent Fees 🔴
A contingent fee arrangement must be confirmed in a signed written agreement that states the percentage or method of calculation of the fee, identifies which expenses will be deducted from the recovery, and specifies whether the percentage is calculated before or after deduction of expenses. Both ABA Model Rule 1.5(c) and California Bus. & Prof. Code § 6147 require these elements. California's § 6147 adds that the written agreement must also state that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable, and must identify how work not covered by the contingency will be compensated. Contingent fees are categorically prohibited in criminal cases and in domestic relations matters where the fee is contingent on the securing of a divorce or on the amount of support, alimony, or property settlement obtained — both ABA and California (post-2018 update) agree on these prohibitions.
*Apply: [Attorney] agreed to represent [client] on a contingency basis in [criminal/domestic/civil] matter and [did/did not] provide a compliant written agreement.*
> **CA divergence:** California § 6147 requires additional disclosures (fee negotiability, non-contingency work) not mandated by ABA MR 1.5(c). Failure to comply renders the agreement voidable.

Thus, [attorney's] contingent fee arrangement [complies with/violates] ABA MR 1.5(c) / California Bus. & Prof. Code § 6147.

### Fee Splitting Between Attorneys 🔴
Two attorneys who are not members of the same firm may split a fee only if (1) the split is disclosed to the client in writing and the client consents, (2) the total fee is not excessive/unconscionable, and (3) the division is proportional to the services performed by each attorney or each attorney assumes joint responsibility for the matter. Under ABA Model Rule 1.5(e), all three elements are required. California's rule, updated in 2018, requires that the client provide informed written consent at the time of the agreement (or as soon as practicable), that the fee not be increased solely because of the division, and that the attorneys enter a written agreement among themselves — but California does NOT require that the split be proportional to work done; any agreed division is permissible so long as each attorney assumes full responsibility or the client consents to the division.
*Apply: [Attorney A] referred [client] to [Attorney B] and the two agreed to split the resulting fee [without disclosure / disproportionately / without written agreement].*
> **CA divergence:** California drops the proportionality requirement of ABA MR 1.5(e)(1). Additionally, California (post-2018) permits limited referral fees in certain circumstances (see Referral Fees block), whereas the ABA generally bars pure referral fees.

Thus, the fee-splitting arrangement [does/does not] comply with ABA MR 1.5(e) / CRPC 1.5.1.

### Referral Fees 🔴
Under ABA Model Rule 7.2(b), an attorney generally may not give anything of value to a person for recommending the attorney's services, with narrow exceptions for lawyer referral services and certain reciprocal referral arrangements. ## F. Candor to the Tribunal

### Candor — False Statements of Law & Duty to Cite Adverse Authority 🟢
An attorney must not knowingly make a false statement of law to a court, and must not misrepresent the state of legal authority. Presenting a legal argument the attorney knows to be frivolous — lacking any good-faith basis — also constitutes a candor violation. The duty of candor to the court supersedes the duty of confidentiality and loyalty when they conflict.

> **CA divergence:** None material; both ABA and CRPC impose the duty to disclose controlling adverse authority.

### Candor — False Evidence & Client Perjury 🔴
An attorney may not offer evidence that the attorney knows to be false and must take reasonable remedial measures if false evidence is inadvertently introduced. The most contested application is client perjury. Under ABA Model Rule 3.3(b), if an attorney comes to know that a client intends to testify falsely, the attorney must counsel the client to testify truthfully, seek to withdraw if counseling fails, and — if withdrawal is denied or impossible — inform the tribunal of the deception. In civil cases, the attorney must simply refuse to sponsor false testimony. In criminal cases, the defendant's Sixth Amendment right to counsel and Fifth Amendment right to testify create tension: if the criminal defendant insists on perjuring, the ABA approach is to tell the judge. California's approach differs: California courts and ethics authorities permit the attorney to allow the client to testify in narrative form (without question-and-answer guidance from the attorney) while refraining from using the false testimony in argument. ### Candor — Physical Evidence & Fruits of Crime 🟡

### Mistaken Receipt of Inadvertently Sent Documents 🟢

### Duty to Uphold the Law — Client Fraud & Assistance in Crime 🔴
An attorney must not counsel a client to engage in conduct the attorney knows is criminal or fraudulent, and must not assist a client in such conduct. ABA Model Rule 1.2(d) and CRPC 1.2.1 both draw this line. ## H. Advertising & Solicitation

## K. Reporting Misconduct

### Duty to Report Another Attorney's Misconduct 🔴

## L. Malpractice
