# Substantive Critique Guide for Law Review Articles

A systematic approach to evaluating the substance, argumentation, and scholarly quality of law review articles.

## I. Thesis and Argument Structure

### Core Questions
- **Clear thesis**: Is the article's central claim clearly stated, preferably in the introduction?
- **Original contribution**: Does the article offer new insights, not merely summarize existing law?
- **Scope appropriateness**: Is the thesis manageable for an article-length treatment?
- **Normative vs. descriptive**: Is it clear whether the article is descriptive (what the law is) or normative (what the law should be)?

### Red Flags
- Thesis buried in middle of article or never explicitly stated
- Multiple competing theses that pull in different directions
- Thesis too broad ("This article examines contracts") or too narrow ("This article analyzes footnote 12 of one case")
- Thesis changes halfway through without acknowledgment
- Promises not delivered on ("Part III will demonstrate..." but Part III doesn't)

---

## II. Logical Reasoning and Argumentation

### Argument Quality Checklist
- **Logical flow**: Do premises lead to conclusions? Are steps in reasoning explicit?
- **Internal consistency**: Does the article contradict itself?
- **Anticipation of counterarguments**: Are opposing views addressed fairly and substantively?
- **Evidence sufficiency**: Are claims backed by adequate supporting evidence?
- **Alternative explanations**: Are alternative interpretations considered and distinguished?

### Common Logical Fallacies to Identify
1. **Strawman**: Misrepresenting opponents' positions to make them easier to attack
2. **False dichotomy**: Presenting only two options when more exist
3. **Slippery slope**: Claiming without justification that one thing will inevitably lead to extreme consequences
4. **Appeal to authority**: Relying on authority rather than reasoning
5. **Post hoc ergo propter hoc**: Assuming causation from correlation
6. **Cherry-picking**: Selecting only favorable evidence while ignoring contrary evidence
7. **Circular reasoning**: Conclusion assumed in premises
8. **Ad hominem**: Attacking person rather than argument

### Argument Structure Issues
- **Non sequiturs**: Conclusions that don't follow from premises
- **Unsupported assumptions**: Claims treated as self-evident that require proof
- **Equivocation**: Using same term with different meanings
- **Missing steps**: Logical leaps that skip necessary intermediate reasoning
- **Overstatement**: Claims stronger than evidence supports

## III. Research Quality and Completeness

### Adequate Coverage Assessment
- **Leading cases**: Are landmark cases in the area addressed?
- **Recent developments**: Is recent (last 5 years) scholarship and case law included?
- **Counterexamples**: Are contrary cases or scholarship acknowledged?
- **Jurisdictional scope**: If claims are general, is survey adequate? If narrow, is that clearly stated?
- **Interdisciplinary sources**: Where relevant, are non-legal sources (empirical studies, economics, philosophy) incorporated?

### Research Gaps to Flag
- Failure to cite clearly relevant Supreme Court cases
- Ignoring major circuit split on the issue
- Missing obvious counterarguments from literature
- Outdated sources when recent scholarship exists
- Cherry-picking only sources that support thesis
- Relying on sources outside their areas of expertise
- Citing sources for propositions they don't support

### Source Quality Issues
- Over-reliance on secondary sources instead of primary law
- Citing dissents or dicta as if binding precedent
- Using student notes as primary authority for major claims
- Relying on unpublished or withdrawn opinions
- Outdated statutory citations (not checking for amendments)
- Mischaracterizing sources or taking quotations out of context

---

## IV. Legal Analysis Rigor

### Doctrinal Analysis
- **Accuracy**: Does article correctly state the law?
- **Distinction of cases**: Are cases distinguished on proper grounds (facts, holdings, reasoning)?
- **Statutory interpretation**: If analyzing statutes, are canons of construction properly applied?
- **Precedential weight**: Is weight of authority properly characterized (binding, persuasive, dictum)?
- **Holding vs. dicta**: Is distinction made and respected?

### Common Errors in Legal Analysis
- Confusing ratio decidendi with obiter dicta
- Treating persuasive authority as binding
- Ignoring that case was overruled or limited
- Citing cases outside their jurisdictional authority
- Misreading holdings (claiming case stands for something it doesn't)
- Ignoring procedural posture (treating dicta at motion to dismiss as settled law)
- Anachronism (applying modern standards to historical cases)
- Conflating different standards of review

### Policy Analysis
- **Consequences examined**: Are practical effects of proposed rules considered?
- **Unintended consequences**: Are potential negative effects identified?
- **Comparative analysis**: Are approaches from other jurisdictions or contexts examined?
- **Feasibility**: Is proposed solution practically implementable?
- **Trade-offs acknowledged**: Are competing values and their tensions recognized?

---

## V. Empirical Claims and Data

### Data Integrity (when applicable)
- **Methodology**: Is research method sound and clearly described?
- **Sample size**: Is sample adequate for conclusions drawn?
- **Statistical significance**: Are statistical claims properly supported?
- **Confounding variables**: Are alternative explanations for patterns addressed?
- **Reproducibility**: Is enough detail provided to reproduce analysis?
- **Data sources**: Are data sources reliable and properly cited?

### Common Empirical Errors
- Claiming causation from correlation
- Overgeneralizing from small or unrepresentative samples
- Selection bias in case selection
- Confirmation bias in data interpretation
- Failing to account for confounding variables
- Cherry-picking data that supports hypothesis
- Misusing statistical tests or misinterpreting results

---

## VI. Normative Arguments

### Normative Framework Assessment
- **Theoretical grounding**: Is normative position based on identifiable theory (rights-based, utilitarian, libertarian, etc.)?
- **Consistency**: Are normative principles applied consistently throughout?
- **Justification**: Are value judgments defended, not just asserted?
- **Competing values**: Are tensions between values (liberty vs. security, efficiency vs. fairness) acknowledged?

### Red Flags in Normative Arguments
- Asserting values as universal without argument
- Inconsistent application of principles
- Shifting between different normative frameworks without acknowledgment
- Making normative claims that rest on empirical assumptions not verified
- Ignoring that reasonable people disagree on normative premises
- Treating policy preferences as legal requirements

---

## VII. Originality and Contribution

### Evaluating Contribution
- **New synthesis**: Does article combine existing ideas in new ways?
- **Novel application**: Does article apply existing frameworks to new contexts?
- **Original critique**: Does article identify previously unrecognized problems?
- **New framework**: Does article propose new way of analyzing issues?
- **Empirical contribution**: Does article provide new data or analysis?
- **Doctrinal clarification**: Does article resolve confusion in existing law?

### Insufficient Contributions
- Merely summarizing existing case law or scholarship
- Restating what others have already argued
- Describing problem without proposing solution
- Making obvious points that don't need elaboration
- Historical survey without analytical payoff

---

## VIII. Article Structure and Organization

### Structural Analysis
- **Introduction**: Does it frame problem, state thesis, and roadmap article?
- **Part organization**: Are parts logical? Does each serve clear purpose?
- **Progressive development**: Does argument build systematically?
- **Signposting**: Are transitions clear? Is reader always oriented?
- **Conclusion**: Does it synthesize argument and highlight implications?

### Structural Problems
- **Front-loading**: Packing introduction with substance rather than overview
- **Tangents**: Sections that don't advance thesis
- **Poor sequencing**: Discussing implications before establishing premises
- **Imbalance**: Disproportionate space to minor points
- **Orphaned sections**: Parts disconnected from overall argument
- **Missing roadmap**: Readers lost because path not previewed

---

## IX. Specific Substantive Issues by Article Type

### Doctrinal Articles
- Accuracy of doctrinal description
- Identification of circuits splits, doctrinal confusion, or inconsistencies
- Proposed resolution of doctrinal problems
- Implications for future cases

### Theoretical Articles
- Coherence of theoretical framework
- Application to concrete legal problems
- Engagement with competing theories
- Practical implications of theory

### Empirical Articles
- Research design soundness
- Data quality and adequacy
- Statistical analysis appropriateness
- Legal implications of findings

### Historical Articles
- Source adequacy (primary vs. secondary)
- Contextualization
- Relevance to contemporary law
- Avoiding presentism

### Comparative Articles
- Adequate understanding of compared systems
- Fair comparison (not cherry-picking best/worst aspects)
- Translation issues addressed
- Transferability analysis (what can be borrowed)

### Interdisciplinary Articles
- Competence in both disciplines
- Genuine integration (not superficial use of other discipline)
- Accurate representation of non-legal field
- Legal implications clearly drawn

## X. Common Substantive Weaknesses Summary

### Critical Issues (Major Revisions Needed)
- Fundamental misstatement of law
- Core argument is logically invalid
- Central claims unsupported by evidence
- Thesis unclear or incoherent
- Major relevant authority ignored
- Fatal methodological flaws (for empirical work)

### Significant Issues (Substantial Revision Recommended)
- Inconsistent arguments
- Important counterarguments unaddressed
- Research gaps in key areas
- Overstated claims not supported by evidence
- Structural problems undermining clarity
- Insufficient engagement with contrary scholarship

### Minor Issues (Revision Beneficial)
- Tangential sections that could be cut
- Some overstatement that should be qualified
- Minor research gaps that should be filled
- Arguments that could be strengthened
- Better organization possible
- Additional citations needed for completeness

---

## XI. Revision Priorities

When conducting substantive review, prioritize:

1. **Accuracy first**: Correct any misstatements of law or fact
2. **Logic second**: Fix logical fallacies and non sequiturs
3. **Completeness third**: Fill research gaps and address counterarguments
4. **Organization fourth**: Improve structure and flow
5. **Refinement last**: Polish arguments and strengthen weaker points

Remember: The goal is to make the article's contribution as clear, well-supported, and persuasive as possible while maintaining the author's voice and vision.
