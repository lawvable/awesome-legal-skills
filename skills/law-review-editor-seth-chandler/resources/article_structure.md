# Law Review Article Structure Guide

Best practices for organizing and structuring scholarly legal articles. This guide covers both macro-structure (overall organization) and micro-structure (paragraph and section-level organization).

## I. Standard Article Components

### Essential Elements (in order)
1. **Title**
2. **Author information** (name, affiliation, credentials)
3. **Abstract** (100-250 words)
4. **Table of Contents** (for longer articles)
5. **Introduction**
6. **Body** (typically 3-6 main parts)
7. **Conclusion**
8. **Footnotes/Endnotes**

### Optional but Common Elements
- **Acknowledgments** (often in footnote *)
- **Dedication**
- **Appendices** (for data, lengthy quotations, model statutes)
- **Bibliography** (in addition to footnotes)

---

## II. Title

### Effective Title Characteristics
- **Descriptive**: Clearly indicates article's subject
- **Specific**: Not overly broad or vague
- **Concise**: Usually 5-12 words
- **Professional**: Avoid cute or overly clever titles
- **Keywords**: Include terms for database searchability

### Title Formats
1. **Simple descriptor**: "The Problem of Standing in Environmental Cases"
2. **Colon subtitle**: "Beyond Intent: A New Framework for Antitrust Liability"
3. **Question form**: "Does the First Amendment Protect Commercial Speech?"
4. **Imperative**: "Rethinking Corporate Personhood"

### Title Red Flags
- Too clever by half (obscures subject matter)
- Multiple colons or subtitles
- Longer than 20 words
- Vague buzzwords without specificity
- Inconsistent with article's actual focus

---

## III. Abstract

### Purpose and Function
- Standalone summary of entire article
- Enables quick assessment by readers and editors
- Indexed in databases (affects discoverability)
- Should entice readers to continue

### Effective Abstract Structure
Typically 1-2 paragraphs addressing:
1. **Problem/question**: What issue does article address?
2. **Gap**: What's missing in current law or scholarship?
3. **Argument/thesis**: What does article claim or propose?
4. **Methodology/approach**: How does article proceed?
5. **Contribution**: What's new or different?
6. **Implications**: Why does it matter?

### Abstract Best Practices
- Write abstract last (after article complete)
- Use concrete language, avoid jargon
- No footnotes or citations in abstract
- State thesis clearly and early
- Make argument and contribution explicit
- Keep to 100-250 words

### Abstract Red Flags
- Vague description without stating thesis
- Too much background, not enough payoff
- Promises not delivered in article
- Jargon-heavy or impenetrable
- Simply describes article structure rather than substance

---

## IV. Introduction

### Introduction Functions
1. **Frame problem**: Establish why topic matters
2. **Review literature**: Position article in existing scholarship
3. **State thesis**: Clearly articulate central claim
4. **Preview argument**: Roadmap the analysis
5. **Engage reader**: Create interest and momentum

### Typical Introduction Structure (15-25% of article)

#### A. Opening Hook (1-2 paragraphs)
Options:
- Recent case or news event
- Hypothetical scenario
- Provocative question
- Puzzle or paradox in current law
- Quotation that frames issue

#### B. Problem Statement (2-4 paragraphs)
- What's the issue?
- Why is it important?
- Who is affected?
- What are current stakes?

#### C. Literature Review (3-8 paragraphs)
- What has been said on this topic?
- What are competing views?
- What gaps exist in current scholarship?
- Where does this article fit?

**Note**: Don't do exhaustive literature review in introduction. Save detailed engagement for body.

#### D. Thesis Statement (1-2 paragraphs)
- Clear, explicit statement of article's central claim
- Should be impossible to miss
- Often benefits from italics or "This Article argues that..."

#### E. Roadmap (1-2 paragraphs)
- "Part I does X. Part II does Y..."
- Brief preview of each main section
- Clarifies article's structure and method

#### F. Optional: Limitations and Scope (1 paragraph)
- What article does NOT address
- Boundaries of argument
- Assumptions being made

### Introduction Best Practices
- **Lead with strength**: Open with most compelling framing
- **Thesis early and often**: State thesis explicitly, not just implied
- **Roadmap is essential**: Never skip the roadmap paragraph
- **Avoid false suspense**: Don't hide the argument to create drama
- **Concise literature review**: Save detailed engagement for body
- **Show stakes**: Explain why topic matters

### Introduction Red Flags
- Thesis not explicitly stated
- No roadmap paragraph
- Literature review overwhelms introduction
- Too much historical background before getting to point
- Promises thesis "will emerge" rather than stating it
- Introduction longer than 30% of article

---

## V. Body Structure

### Overall Organization Principles
- **Logical progression**: Each part builds on previous ones
- **Self-contained parts**: Each part has clear purpose and internal coherence
- **Consistent level of generality**: Parts should be parallel in scope
- **Effective transitions**: Connect parts smoothly

### Common Body Structures

#### Structure 1: Problem → Solution
- Part I: The Problem (existing law is inadequate)
- Part II: Causes/Sources of the Problem
- Part III: The Solution (proposed approach)
- Part IV: Objections and Responses

#### Structure 2: Critique → Reconstruction
- Part I: The Current Framework (description)
- Part II: Problems with Current Framework (critique)
- Part III: Alternative Framework (proposal)
- Part IV: Advantages of New Framework

#### Structure 3: Descriptive → Normative
- Part I: What the Law Is (doctrinal survey)
- Part II: Tensions and Inconsistencies (analysis)
- Part III: What the Law Should Be (normative argument)
- Part IV: Implementation

#### Structure 4: Historical → Contemporary
- Part I: Historical Background
- Part II: Evolution of Doctrine
- Part III: Current State of Law
- Part IV: Future Directions

#### Structure 5: Theoretical → Applied
- Part I: Theoretical Framework
- Part II: Application to Area of Law
- Part III: Implications and Extensions
- Part IV: Objections

### Typical Part Functions

#### Opening Parts (I-II)
**Common functions**:
- Establish background
- Survey existing law or scholarship
- Identify problem or puzzle
- Develop theoretical framework
- Present data or empirical findings

**Pitfall**: Too much background before getting to argument

#### Middle Parts (II-IV)
**Common functions**:
- Develop central argument
- Analyze cases or statutes in detail
- Apply theoretical framework
- Present evidence for thesis
- Distinguish alternatives

**This is the heart of the article**

#### Concluding Parts (IV-VI)
**Common functions**:
- Address objections
- Discuss implications
- Consider application to related areas
- Acknowledge limitations
- Propose future research

## VI. Section and Subsection Organization

### Hierarchical Numbering Systems

#### Option 1: Roman Numerals (Most Common)
```
I. First Main Part
   A. First Subsection
      1. First Sub-subsection
         a. First Sub-sub-subsection
```

#### Option 2: Arabic Numerals
```
1. First Main Part
   1.1 First Subsection
       1.1.1 First Sub-subsection
```

#### Option 3: Mixed
```
I. First Main Part
   1. First Subsection
      a. First Sub-subsection
```

### Heading Best Practices
- **Descriptive headings**: "The Problem with Specific Intent" not "The Problem"
- **Parallel structure**: If one heading is a question, others should be too
- **Appropriate length**: Usually 3-10 words
- **No orphan headings**: Don't have single subsection (if there's A, there must be B)
- **Consistent formatting**: Same level headings get same formatting

### Section Length Guidelines
- **Main parts (I, II, III)**: 15-30% of article each (5,000-10,000 words)
- **Subsections (A, B, C)**: 500-2,000 words typically
- **Sub-subsections (1, 2, 3)**: 200-800 words typically
- Avoid radical imbalances (Part I: 2,000 words, Part II: 20,000 words)

---

## VII. Paragraph-Level Organization

### Effective Paragraphs
- **One idea per paragraph**: Each paragraph makes one point
- **Topic sentences**: First sentence states paragraph's point
- **Development**: Body of paragraph develops/supports topic sentence
- **Transitions**: Last sentence connects to next paragraph or section
- **Appropriate length**: 100-250 words typically (4-8 sentences)

### Paragraph Problems
- **Mega-paragraphs**: Paragraphs over 400 words should usually be split
- **Choppy paragraphs**: String of 1-2 sentence paragraphs (except for emphasis)
- **Unclear topic sentences**: Reader can't identify paragraph's point
- **Lack of development**: Topic sentence without support
- **Orphaned quotes**: Block quotes not integrated into argument

### Paragraph Structure Patterns

#### Pattern 1: Point-First
```
[Topic sentence stating point]
[Evidence/support]
[Analysis/explanation]
[Connection to thesis]
```

#### Pattern 2: Evidence-First
```
[Presentation of evidence/example]
[Analysis of what it shows]
[General point drawn from it]
```

#### Pattern 3: Problem-Solution
```
[Problem or tension]
[Explanation of problem]
[Solution or resolution]
```

---

## VIII. Transitions and Signposting

### Macro-Level Transitions (Between Parts)
- Begin each part with overview paragraph
- End each part with bridge to next part
- Use explicit transition sentences:
  - "Having established X, I now turn to Y..."
  - "With this framework in place, Part II examines..."
  - "Before proceeding to the implications, Part III addresses objections..."

### Micro-Level Transitions (Between Paragraphs)
Transition words and phrases:
- **Addition**: Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, Similarly
- **Contrast**: However, Nevertheless, In contrast, On the other hand
- **Causation**: Therefore, Consequently, As a result, Thus
- **Sequence**: First, Second, Next, Finally
- **Example**: For instance, For example, To illustrate
- **Emphasis**: Indeed, In fact, Notably, Importantly

### Signposting Language
- "This Article proceeds in four parts..."
- "As explained in Part II..."
- "Recall that..."
- "The following Section addresses..."
- "Returning to the central question..."
- "As demonstrated above..."

## IX. Conclusion

### Conclusion Functions
1. **Synthesize argument**: Pull threads together
2. **Restate thesis**: In light of what's been shown
3. **Highlight implications**: So what? What follows from this?
4. **Acknowledge limitations**: What questions remain?
5. **Future research**: What comes next?

### Conclusion Structure (Typical)

#### Paragraph 1: Restate Problem and Thesis
- Brief reminder of issue and article's main claim
- NO new arguments here

#### Paragraphs 2-3: Synthesize Main Points
- How parts fit together
- What has been demonstrated
- Key takeaways

#### Paragraph 4: Implications
- Practical consequences
- Doctrinal ramifications  
- Broader theoretical significance

#### Paragraph 5: Limitations and Future Work (Optional)
- What article didn't address
- Questions raised for future research
- Invitation to engage with argument

### Conclusion Best Practices
- **Strong opening sentence**: Don't start with "In conclusion..."
- **No new arguments**: Conclusion synthesizes, doesn't introduce
- **Appropriate length**: 3-5% of article (shorter than introduction)
- **End on strong note**: Last sentence should resonate
- **Avoid apologizing**: Don't undermine your argument at the end

### Conclusion Red Flags
- Simply restates introduction
- Introduces new arguments or evidence
- Overly apologetic about limitations
- Too long (10%+ of article)
- Ends with whimper rather than strong statement
- "In conclusion" or "To sum up" opening

## X. Footnotes

### Function of Footnotes in Law Reviews
1. **Citations**: Primary function is attribution and support
2. **Elaboration**: Additional points tangential to main text
3. **Counterarguments**: Address objections without disrupting flow
4. **Qualifications**: Nuance claims in main text
5. **Cross-references**: Point to other parts of article
6. **Empirical details**: Methodology, data sources
7. **Tangents**: Interesting points that would distract in main text

### Footnote Organization
- **Order**: Numbered sequentially throughout article
- **Placement**: After punctuation (clause or sentence)
- **Callout frequency**: Varies, but typically every 1-3 sentences in legal writing
- **Substantive vs. citation**: Some footnotes purely citational, others substantive with cites

### Footnote Best Practices
- **Main text stands alone**: Text should be readable without footnotes
- **Substantive footnotes readable**: Not just string cites
- **Proper signals**: Use Bluebook signals correctly (*see*, *cf.*, etc.)
- **Order citations**: Follow Bluebook order rules
- **Pinpoint cites**: Always include specific page numbers
- **Short forms**: Use *id.* and *supra* appropriately

### Footnote Problems
- **Text incomprehensible without footnotes**: Essential content relegated to notes
- **Overlong footnotes**: Notes longer than page of text
- **Excessive cross-referencing**: "See supra note 47" too frequently
- **String cites without explanation**: 10 case citations with no analysis
- **Outdated citations**: Citing overruled cases or superseded statutes

## XI. Special Structural Considerations

### For Long Articles (30,000+ words)
- **Table of contents**: Essential for navigation
- **Part introductions**: Each main part needs preview paragraph
- **More signposting**: Remind readers where they are more frequently
- **Appendices**: Consider moving supplementary material to appendices
- **Executive summary**: Consider longer abstract or separate summary

### For Short Articles (10,000- words)
- **Streamlined structure**: Fewer parts (3-4 instead of 5-6)
- **Combined sections**: May combine literature review into introduction
- **Tighter focus**: Resist urge to cover everything
- **Shorter conclusion**: 1-2 pages may suffice

### For Empirical Articles
- **Methodology section**: Essential; explain research design
- **Results section**: Present findings clearly
- **Tables and figures**: Use visuals effectively
- **Appendices**: Detailed data, additional tests, etc.
- **Replication materials**: Consider making data/code available

### For Response/Critique Pieces
- **Fair presentation**: Accurately summarize target article
- **Clear disagreement**: State precisely where/why you disagree
- **Substantive engagement**: Not just "I disagree" but "Here's why"
- **Constructive tone**: Collegial disagreement

---

## XII. Common Structural Mistakes

### Major Structural Problems
1. **No clear thesis**: Reader finishes article unsure of main point
2. **Poor part division**: Parts don't serve distinct functions
3. **Imbalanced structure**: Huge Part I, tiny Part III
4. **Missing roadmap**: Reader lost because path not previewed
5. **Tangential parts**: Sections unconnected to thesis
6. **Conclusion introduces new arguments**: Surprises instead of synthesis

### Organizational Red Flags
- More than 6 main parts (suggests poor organization)
- Parts less than 5% of article (combine or cut)
- Multiple single subsections (if there's A, there must be B)
- Deeply nested outline (I.A.1.a.i.α is too deep)
- Parts or subsections without any text (just subdivisions)
- Inconsistent heading formatting

### Flow Problems
- Abrupt topic changes without transition
- Repeating same analysis in multiple places
- Discussing implications before establishing premises
- Historical background that never connects to thesis
- Front-loading: too much in introduction, too little in body

---

## XIII. Structural Revision Checklist

### Article Level
- [ ] Title accurately reflects content?
- [ ] Abstract clearly states thesis and contribution?
- [ ] Introduction frames problem, reviews literature, states thesis, and roadmaps article?
- [ ] Parts serve distinct, clear functions?
- [ ] Parts are balanced in length and importance?
- [ ] Each part begins with overview and ends with transition?
- [ ] Argument builds logically from part to part?
- [ ] Conclusion synthesizes without introducing new arguments?

### Part Level
- [ ] Each part has clear organizing principle?
- [ ] Subsections are parallel in structure and scope?
- [ ] No orphan subsections (single A without B)?
- [ ] Headings are descriptive and informative?
- [ ] Each section advances thesis?

### Paragraph Level
- [ ] Topic sentences state each paragraph's point?
- [ ] Paragraphs develop single idea?
- [ ] Paragraph length appropriate (not too long or short)?
- [ ] Effective transitions between paragraphs?
- [ ] Main text readable without footnotes?

### Overall
- [ ] Structure serves argument (not just following template)?
- [ ] Organization helps reader follow reasoning?
- [ ] No tangents or orphaned sections?
- [ ] Appropriate signposting throughout?
- [ ] Reader always knows where they are in argument?

---

## XIV. Structure and Argument: The Connection

Remember: **Structure is not just formatting—it's part of the argument.**

- Structure shows logical relationships
- Organization reveals priorities
- Sequencing affects persuasiveness
- Good structure makes strong argument clearer
- Bad structure can obscure good argument

The goal is not to follow a template mechanically but to organize the article in the way that best serves the argument and helps readers follow the analysis.
