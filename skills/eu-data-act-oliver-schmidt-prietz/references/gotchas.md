# Gotchas

This file is the cross-cutting failure-mode catalogue. Each entry is a trap that catches careful readers of the Data Act, has high consequences when missed, and shows up frequently in practitioner work. The catalogue is not exhaustive; it is the set of recurring errors the skill must actively check against every output.

The catalogue is grouped by category for scanning, but the skill checks all of them on every relevant matter. A category tag in square brackets at the end of each entry name makes the catalogue greppable.

The format for each entry is consistent: **the trap** (what gets read wrong), **the correct reading** (what the regulation actually says), and **when it bites** (which scenario contexts surface the trap). Citations are inline.

---

## 1. Article 2 numbering trap [definitions]

**The trap.** Article 2 of the Data Act is the definitions article with 43 numbered definitions. References elsewhere in the regulation to "Article 5(3)" or "Article 4(8)" point to operative articles, not to sub-clauses of Article 2. Conversely, "Article 2(5)" is the definition of "connected product", not a sub-clause of operative article 2. A skill that conflates these produces wrong citations and wrong analyses.

**The correct reading.** When citing Art. 2(N), the skill is citing definition N. When citing Art. 5(3), the skill is citing paragraph 3 of operative Article 5 (the gatekeeper exclusion). The regulation uses the same notation for both, so the disambiguation is contextual: definitions are stable terminology, operative paragraphs impose duties.

**When it bites.** Any analysis citing Art. 2 sub-clauses, especially Art. 2(8) (data processing service), Art. 2(12) (user), Art. 2(13) (data holder), Art. 2(17) (readily available data), Art. 2(38) (exportable data). Easy to slip when listing several articles in a single sentence.

## 2. Manufacturer is not always the data holder [role mapping]

**The trap.** Treating "manufacturer" and "data holder" as synonyms. The two roles often coincide but do not have to. A manufacturer can contract out the data-holder function. A related service provider who is not the manufacturer can be the data holder for the data generated through that service. The data-holder role attaches to whoever controls access to readily available data, not to whoever built the hardware or wrote the software.

**The correct reading.** Art. 2(13) defines data holder by reference to right or obligation to use and make available data. FAQ Q21 confirms: "Determining who the data holder is does not depend on who produced the hardware or software, but on who controls access to the readily available data." A connected product can have several data holders in parallel (manufacturer for some data, related service provider for other data, component supplier with embedded SIM card for another subset).

**When it bites.** Multi-party IoT scenarios with component suppliers, related services bundled separately, or aftermarket service providers. Also bites in second-hand resale where the original manufacturer-as-data-holder relationship may have shifted.

## 3. The user who is not the data subject becomes a controller [GDPR overlay]

**The trap.** Assuming that the GDPR layer falls only on the data holder when the user makes a data access request. When the user is an enterprise (not a natural person who is the data subject), the user becomes the controller for any personal data generated by the connected product or related service when it exercises Data Act rights.

**The correct reading.** Recital 34 of the Data Act states: "Where the user is not the data subject but an enterprise, including a sole trader, and not in cases of shared household use of the connected product, the user is considered to be a controller." FAQ Q25a elaborates: such a user "will have to make an assessment on an appropriate legal basis for providing access or for making personal data available". Art. 4(12) and 5(7) of the Data Act explicitly condition data-holder disclosure to a non-data-subject user on the existence of a valid GDPR legal basis.

**When it bites.** Fleet operators requesting telematics data from employee-driven vehicles. Employers requesting connected-device data generated by employees. Industrial operators requesting data from machines used by individual workers. Any B2B scenario where the user is an enterprise and the underlying data includes personal data.

## 4. "Without undue delay" has no numeric SLA [timelines]

**The trap.** Treating "without undue delay" as a fixed period and supplying a number. The phrase appears throughout the Data Act (Art. 4(1), 4(7), 4(8), 5(1), 5(10), 5(11), 11(2), 17(2), 18(1), 21(5), 31(3), 37(15), 38(3)) and has no statutory numeric meaning. The CJEU and Member State courts interpret it case-by-case based on context. Suggesting "this means 72 hours" or "this means 30 days" invents a deadline that the regulation does not impose.

**The correct reading.** The regulation distinguishes carefully between deadlines it specifies numerically and obligations it phrases as "without undue delay". Numeric deadlines include the Art. 18(2) 5/30 working-day decline-or-modify window for Ch V requests, the Art. 25(2)(d) two-month maximum notice period for cloud switching, the Art. 25(2)(a) 30-day transitional period (extendable to 7 months under Art. 25(4) on demonstrated technical unfeasibility), the Art. 25(2)(g) 30-day minimum retrieval period, the Art. 21(4) six-month retention period for research recipients of Ch V data, and the Art. 10(9) 90-day dispute settlement decision window. Everywhere else "without undue delay" means "promptly in light of all the circumstances", which is fact-specific.

**When it bites.** Drafting Art. 4(1) or 5(1) access response procedures. Drafting Art. 4(7)/5(10) withholding-or-suspension notices. Drafting Art. 4(8)/5(11) refusal letters. Drafting Art. 38 complaint procedures. Drafting Art. 11(2) misuse notification letters.

## 5. Article 4(2) is not a five-item checklist [safety/security handbrake]

**The trap.** Reading Art. 4(2) as a list of independent conditions, often presented as a five-item checklist. The provision has a conditional structure with cumulative limbs and procedural obligations, not a flat enumeration. Skills that produce a five-bullet "Art. 4(2) requirements" output misrepresent the structure.

**The correct reading.** Art. 4(2) permits a contractual restriction or prohibition on accessing, using, or further sharing data where (i) such processing could undermine security requirements of the connected product, AND (ii) those security requirements are laid down in Union or national law, AND (iii) the consequence is a serious adverse effect on the health, safety, or security of natural persons. Sectoral authorities may provide technical expertise. If the data holder refuses to share data on this basis, it must notify the competent authority designated pursuant to Art. 37. The handbrake is bilateral (negotiated with the user, not unilaterally invoked); FAQ Q25 confirms it is "the 'safety and security handbrake'" and that users may challenge the refusal before competent authorities, courts, or a dispute settlement body.

**When it bites.** Any scenario involving safety-critical connected products (vehicles, medical devices, industrial machinery, building management systems). Any client matter where a data holder is considering invoking safety or security to limit access.

## 6. Trade-secret handbrake is a ladder, not a switch [trade secrets]

**The trap.** Treating trade-secret status as a unilateral basis for refusing data access. A data holder claims trade-secret protection over the requested data and stops there. This collapses a three-stage ladder into a single-step refusal.

**The correct reading.** The trade-secret regime is graduated. Stage 1 (Art. 4(6) for users, Art. 5(9) for third parties): identify the trade-secret data prior to disclosure, agree proportionate technical and organisational safeguards (model contractual terms, confidentiality agreements, strict access protocols, technical standards, codes of conduct). Stage 2 (Art. 4(7), Art. 5(10)): withhold or suspend the sharing only if no agreement is reached, if the recipient fails to implement agreed measures, or if confidentiality is undermined. Stage 3 (Art. 4(8), Art. 5(11)): refuse on a case-by-case basis only in exceptional circumstances, demonstrating highly likely serious economic damage based on objective elements, in writing, without undue delay, with competent-authority notification. Recital 31 confirms: "Data holders cannot, in principle, refuse a data access request under this Regulation solely on the basis that certain data is considered to be a trade secret".

**When it bites.** Any client matter where a data holder wants to resist a user or third-party data access request. Any drafting of a refusal letter. Any advisory on what protections are available against compelled disclosure.

## 7. Recital 31 "serious AND irreparable" conjunction [trade secrets]

**The trap.** Treating "serious economic damage" in Art. 4(8) and 5(11) as a single threshold, met by demonstrating serious damage alone. The recital that defines the term uses a conjunction that is easy to read past.

**The correct reading.** Recital 31 states: "Serious economic damage implies serious and irreparable economic loss." Both elements are required: the loss must be serious AND it must be irreparable. Damage that is severe but recoverable through ordinary commercial mitigation does not meet the standard. Damage that is permanent but small does not meet the standard either. The combination is what justifies the exceptional refusal under Art. 4(8) or 5(11).

**When it bites.** Drafting Art. 4(8) or 5(11) refusal letters. Reviewing such refusals. Advising on whether a trade-secret refusal can be sustained. The conjunction error is the single most common drafting defect in Data Act practitioner work in this area; the method file flags this as "the circular trap".

## 8. Recital 31 objective elements are illustrative, not exhaustive [trade secrets]

**The trap.** Treating the three objective elements named in Recital 31 (enforceability of trade secret protection in third countries; nature and level of confidentiality of the data requested; uniqueness and novelty of the connected product) as the closed set of admissible grounds for an Art. 4(8) or 5(11) refusal. A refusal that ticks these three boxes is assumed sufficient; a refusal relying on other objective elements is assumed insufficient.

**The correct reading.** Recital 31 introduces the list with "in particular", which is illustrative phrasing throughout the regulation. The actual test is "objective elements ... demonstrating the concrete risk of serious economic damage expected to result from a specific data disclosure". A data holder may rely on other objective elements (e.g. the competitive position of the data recipient, the specific risk of reverse engineering, the inadequacy of the safeguards offered in light of past incidents). Conversely, satisfying the three listed elements does not automatically satisfy the test if the damage demonstration is weak.

**When it bites.** Drafting refusals where the obvious three elements do not fit the facts. Reviewing refusals to test whether they over-rely on the listed elements at the expense of damage demonstration. Advising on whether new categories of risk (e.g. AI training risks, cybersecurity exposure) can ground a refusal.

## 9. Ch II related service is not the same as Ch VI data processing service [chapter scoping]

**The trap.** Conflating the two service definitions. A "related service" under Art. 2(6) is a digital service tied to a connected product. A "data processing service" under Art. 2(8) is cloud computing (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS). They can coincide (e.g. a SaaS that operates a fleet of connected products) but they are distinct legal categories with different operative chapters and different obligations. Mixing them produces wrong analyses.

**The correct reading.** Related services are governed by Ch II (data access rights flowing from the connected product). Data processing services are governed by Ch VI (switching and interoperability) and Ch VII (third-country governmental access). FAQ Q10 clarifies the related service test: bidirectional data exchange AND impact on connected-product behaviour. FAQ Q58a confirms that data processing services cover all three NIST-derived delivery models. A single provider can offer both kinds of services simultaneously and be governed by both chapters.

**When it bites.** Connected-vehicle telematics platforms that are both related services (to the car) and data processing services (to the platform customer). Industrial IoT scenarios where a SaaS controls connected machines. Any output that uses "service" without specifying which kind.

## 10. Functional equivalence is an IaaS-only obligation [Ch VI switching]

**The trap.** Applying Art. 30(1) functional equivalence as a universal Ch VI obligation. PaaS and SaaS providers are assumed to owe their customers the same level of post-switch functionality that IaaS providers owe.

**The correct reading.** Art. 30(1) applies only to providers of data processing services "that concern scalable and elastic computing resources limited to infrastructural elements such as servers, networks and the virtual resources necessary for operating the infrastructure" — that is, IaaS. PaaS and SaaS providers owe a different set of obligations under Art. 30(2) and (3): make open interfaces available, ensure compatibility with future common specifications or harmonised standards when published in the Union repository, and (under Art. 30(5)) export exportable data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format. FAQ Q58a and Q58b confirm the asymmetry. Recital 86 confirms: "This Regulation does not constitute an obligation to facilitate functional equivalence for providers of data processing services other than those offering services of the IaaS delivery model."

**When it bites.** Advising a SaaS provider on switching obligations. Reviewing a PaaS provider's contracts for Art. 25 compliance. Drafting a customer's exit strategy from a SaaS provider that expects functional equivalence and will not get it.

## 11. Article 5(3) gatekeeper exclusion has tripartite reach [DMA overlay]

**The trap.** Reading Art. 5(3) as a single prohibition: gatekeepers cannot receive data from users under Art. 5. The provision is broader than that.

**The correct reading.** Art. 5(3) excludes any DMA-designated gatekeeper as an eligible third party. The exclusion has three operative limbs: (a) a gatekeeper cannot solicit or commercially incentivise a user to make data available to one of the gatekeeper's services that the user obtained under Art. 4(1); (b) a gatekeeper cannot solicit or commercially incentivise a user to request the data holder to make data available under Art. 5(1) to one of the gatekeeper's services; (c) a gatekeeper cannot receive data from a user that the user obtained under Art. 4(1). All three are cumulative prohibitions on the gatekeeper. Plus Art. 6(2)(d) prohibits any third party from making data it has received available to a gatekeeper. The exclusion is bidirectional: gatekeeper cannot receive directly and cannot receive indirectly through a third-party intermediary. FAQ Q36 confirms.

**When it bites.** Any Art. 5 third-party request involving a large platform. Any onward-sharing arrangement that might end up at a gatekeeper. Any commercial incentive structure offered by a gatekeeper to users in exchange for data. Verifying gatekeeper status of recipients in data-sharing chains.

## 12. Article 6(2) is a closed list of third-party prohibitions [third-party obligations]

**The trap.** Reading Art. 6(2) as illustrative examples of bad third-party behaviour, with the implication that other unlisted prohibitions can be inferred.

**The correct reading.** Art. 6(2) sets out a closed list of prohibitions on third parties who receive data under Art. 5. The list is exhaustive on its face: making the exercise of choices or rights unduly difficult including via dark patterns; profiling beyond strict necessity to provide the requested service; onward sharing without user contract and without trade-secret safeguards; sharing with gatekeepers; using the data to develop a competing connected product or sharing for that purpose; using the data adversely to the security of the connected product; disregarding agreed trade-secret safeguards; and preventing a consumer user from sharing the data with other parties. Unlisted behaviours are not prohibited by Art. 6(2) (though they may be prohibited by other Union law, e.g. GDPR, competition law, consumer protection law).

**When it bites.** Drafting third-party data-recipient contracts. Reviewing third-party permitted-use scopes. Advising a third party on what it can lawfully do with received data. Note the asymmetry: most Data Act prohibitions are illustrative ("in particular"); Art. 6(2) is closed.

## 13. The custom-built carve-out under Art. 31 is narrower than industry usage suggests [Ch VI scope]

**The trap.** Treating any cloud service that is configured per customer as falling within the Art. 31(1) custom-built carve-out. SaaS solutions with customer-specific configurations, white-labelled services, and contractually negotiated SLAs are assumed to qualify.

**The correct reading.** Art. 31(1) applies only to data processing services "of which the majority of main features has been custom-built to accommodate the specific needs of an individual customer or where all components have been developed for the purposes of an individual customer, and where those data processing services are not offered at broad commercial scale via the service catalogue of the provider of data processing services." Two qualifying alternatives in the first limb (majority of main features OR all components) AND the negative limb (not offered at broad commercial scale). Standard SaaS offerings configured per customer typically fail the negative limb because they are in the service catalogue. The Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal (COM(2025) 833 final) would broaden this carve-out for pre-12-September-2025 contracts and for SME and small-mid-cap providers, but as of today the carve-out remains narrow.

**When it bites.** Advising SaaS providers on whether Ch VI obligations apply. Reviewing white-labelled cloud arrangements. Drafting standard service catalogues that the provider hopes to exclude from Ch VI by labelling as custom-built. Note the proposal flag: a Digital Omnibus-adopted broadening is plausible but not law.

## 14. Sui generis database right does not apply to Data Act in-scope data [IP overlay]

**The trap.** Assuming that data holders retain a sui generis database right under Art. 7 of Directive 96/9/EC that they can assert to block data access. This was a real defensive layer pre-Data Act and has been removed for in-scope data.

**The correct reading.** Art. 43 of the Data Act provides that the sui generis right under Art. 7 of Directive 96/9/EC does not apply when data is obtained from or generated by a connected product or related service falling within the scope of the regulation, in particular in relation to Arts. 4 and 5. Recital 112 confirms this is intended to "eliminate the risk that holders of data in databases obtained or generated by means of physical components, such as sensors, of a connected product and a related service or other machine-generated data, claim the sui generis right under Article 7 of Directive 96/9/EC, and in so doing hinder, in particular, the effective exercise of the right of users to access and use data". Databases containing data outside the Data Act scope can still attract the sui generis right under the normal conditions.

**When it bites.** Reviewing data holder defences in litigation or pre-litigation correspondence. Drafting data-sharing demands where the data holder has previously asserted sui generis protection. Advising on the residual scope of database protection for mixed datasets (some in-scope, some out-of-scope data).

## 15. Ch III compensation is data-recipient-to-data-holder, not the other way around [compensation]

**The trap.** Confusing the direction of compensation flows. In Ch III mandatory B2B data sharing, compensation goes from the data recipient to the data holder. Skills that produce outputs reversing the direction (treating data holder as paying the recipient) misstate the basic economics.

**The correct reading.** Art. 9(1) permits compensation agreed between data holder and data recipient for making data available; the data holder is the recipient of compensation. Art. 9(4) caps compensation at directly-related costs (no margin) when the data recipient is an SME or not-for-profit research organisation. The data holder must provide the recipient with sufficient detail to assess whether the compensation complies with Art. 9 requirements. Note that Ch V compensation (Art. 20) follows a different model: public sector bodies pay the data holder for non-emergency exceptional-need data (technical and organisational costs plus reasonable margin); emergency data under Art. 15(1)(a) is free of charge except where claimed by microenterprises or small enterprises.

**When it bites.** Drafting Ch III compensation clauses. Drafting Ch V compensation demands. Reviewing data-sharing agreements for compensation symmetry. Drafting Art. 8(3) non-discrimination challenges where comparable categories of recipients have been charged differently.

## 16. Reasonable compensation guidelines are forthcoming, not published [compensation]

**The trap.** Citing Commission guidelines on the calculation of reasonable compensation as if they have been adopted. As of May 2026 they have not been.

**The correct reading.** Art. 9(5) requires the Commission to adopt guidelines on calculating reasonable compensation, taking into account the advice of the EDIB. FAQ Q72 (v1.4, 22 January 2026) states the guidelines are expected to be adopted by Q2/Q3 2026. Until adopted, compensation analysis relies on the regulation itself (Art. 9 for Ch III, Art. 20 for Ch V) and on general principles of fairness, reasonableness, and non-discrimination from Art. 8.

**When it bites.** Drafting compensation demands or rebuttals. Advising on whether a proposed compensation is reasonable. Reviewing data-recipient challenges under Art. 8(3) non-discrimination. Skill outputs should flag the absence of guidelines and note that the answer relies on the regulation alone.

## 17. Article 11 unauthorised use remedies require a predicate, not just suspicion [enforcement]

**The trap.** Treating Art. 11(2) remedies (erasure of unlawfully used data, ending production of derivative goods, compensation for the harmed party, informing the user) as automatically available when a data holder suspects misuse. The remedies are conditional on Art. 11(3) predicates.

**The correct reading.** Art. 11(2) remedies apply where a third party or data recipient has (a) provided false information, deployed deceptive or coercive means, or abused gaps in the technical infrastructure to obtain data; (b) used the data for unauthorised purposes including the development of a competing connected product (an Art. 6(2)(e) breach); (c) unlawfully disclosed data to another party; (d) failed to maintain agreed Art. 5(9) trade-secret measures; or (e) altered or removed the data holder's technical protection measures without agreement. Suspicion of breach without one of these predicates does not unlock the Art. 11 remedies. Art. 11(4) extends the same regime to users who alter or remove technical protections or fail to maintain trade-secret safeguards. Art. 11(5) extends user-side rights when a data recipient breaches Art. 6(2)(a) or (b).

**When it bites.** Drafting Art. 11 demand letters. Advising on whether a suspected misuse case can support stop-production or destruction orders. Reviewing technical protection measure (TPM) drafting in Art. 11(1) contractual schemes to ensure they are not overbroad or discriminatory.

## 18. Article 32(3) third-country access requires conjunction of three procedural conditions [third-country gate]

**The trap.** Reading Art. 32(3) as a disjunctive list of options for compliance with a third-country court order. A provider receives the order, picks one of the three conditions, and complies.

**The correct reading.** Art. 32(3) applies in the absence of an applicable international agreement (Art. 32(2)). Where the provider would be put in conflict with Union or Member State law, transfer to or access by the third-country authority shall take place only where ALL three conditions are met: (a) the third-country system requires reasons and proportionality of the decision to be set out and requires the decision to be specific in character (e.g. by establishing a sufficient link to suspected persons or infringements); AND (b) the reasoned objection of the addressee is subject to review by a competent third-country court or tribunal; AND (c) the competent third-country court or tribunal is empowered to take duly into account the relevant legal interests of the provider of the data protected by Union or Member State law. The conditions are cumulative. Failing any one of them means the third-country authority may not be granted access on the Art. 32(3) path. The provider may consult the relevant national body or authority competent for international cooperation in legal matters (Bundesjustizamt in Germany per FAQ Q63, Bureau de l'entraide pénale internationale in France).

**When it bites.** Drafting Art. 32 compliance frameworks for cloud providers. Reviewing third-country government data access requests. Advising customers of cloud providers on the limits of Art. 32 protection. Note that personal data is governed by GDPR Chapter V, not Data Act Art. 32; the Art. 32 regime is for non-personal data only.

## 19. The Data Act FAQ is not authoritative [source fidelity]

**The trap.** Citing FAQ entries as if they were binding interpretations. Phrasing like "the FAQ requires", "under the FAQ", or "the Commission has ruled in the FAQ" treats the document as authority rather than as guidance.

**The correct reading.** The FAQ opens with an explicit disclaimer: "This document should not be considered as representative of the European Commission's official position. The replies to the FAQs do not extend in any way the rights and obligations deriving from applicable legislation nor introduce any additional requirement. The expressed views are not authoritative and cannot prejudge any future actions the European Commission may take, including potential positions before the Court of Justice of the European Union." The FAQ is the Commission's compiled interpretation, valuable as persuasive authority for how a regulator will likely think, but not a substitute for the regulation, the recitals, or the CJEU's eventual case law. When the FAQ adds detail the regulation does not address (e.g. FAQ Q5a on edge processing, Q22a on technical and practical access requirements, Q34a on pre-application data), the skill flags the FAQ position as the Commission's view and notes that no court has ruled.

**When it bites.** Every output that cites the FAQ. Particularly important when the FAQ takes a position that goes beyond the regulation text (which is most of the substantive FAQ entries).

## 20. The Digital Omnibus is a proposal, not law [current law vs proposal]

**The trap.** Treating the Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal (COM(2025) 833 final, 19 November 2025) as adopted. Drafting client deliverables that assume the proposed amendments are in force. The proposal is consequential for the Data Act (proposed new trade-secret refusal ground for third-country misuse risk; narrowed Ch V circumstances; expanded Art. 31 custom-built carve-out; explicit Art. 25 early-termination penalties; consolidation absorbing DGA, Open Data Directive, Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation, and Platform-to-Business Regulation), but as of the skill's source date it is in co-legislator negotiation and has not been adopted.

**The correct reading.** Current law is Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 as published in the OJ. Outputs that touch a provision affected by the Omnibus must state the current law first, then flag the proposed amendment second, then note the legislative status (co-legislator negotiation, not adopted). Never present a proposal as if it were law. Never omit the proposal when it is materially relevant; the user is making decisions today that will be affected if the proposal is adopted. The Digital Omnibus tracker file (`sources/digital-omnibus-amendments-tracker.md`) is the reference list of affected provisions.

**When it bites.** Any output touching trade-secret refusals (Art. 4(8), 5(11)); Ch V circumstances (Art. 15); Ch VI custom-built carve-out (Art. 31); Ch VI early-termination penalties (Art. 25); cross-references to DGA, Open Data Directive, Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation, or Platform-to-Business Regulation. The status of these proposals should be re-checked at the start of any major deliverable; this catalogue may already be out of date by the time the skill runs.

## How the skill uses this catalogue

The skill checks each entry against the matter at hand before producing output. The check is not narrative ("did I think about this?") but operational ("is this trap present in the scenario, and have I avoided it in the draft?"). The catalogue is the second pass over a draft: first the seven-step flow produces an analysis, then the gotcha check verifies that none of the high-frequency errors are encoded into it.

Entries 6, 7, and 8 (the trade-secret ladder, the "serious AND irreparable" conjunction, the illustrative-list reading) are interlocked. The method file groups them as "the circular trap". A trade-secret refusal that misses any of the three is unreliable.

Entries 19 and 20 (FAQ framing, current law vs proposal) are source-fidelity rules that the method file also flags. They appear here because they are also failure modes: outputs that get them wrong are common defects, not just abstract discipline lapses.

This catalogue is not closed. As the skill produces more outputs and the Commission, EDIB, and national courts produce more guidance and case law, new failure modes will surface. When a new trap is identified, add an entry here. When the Digital Omnibus is adopted (or formally withdrawn), revise entries 7, 8, 13, and 20.
