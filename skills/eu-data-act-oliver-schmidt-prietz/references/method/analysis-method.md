# Analysis method

This file defines the cognitive flow the skill follows before producing any output on a Data Act matter. It is the architectural anchor of the skill: the gates, gotchas, scenario cards, and templates all assume the skill has done this work first. If the skill produces an output that hasn't gone through this flow, the output is unreliable regardless of how confident it sounds.

The method is built around the specific structural features of Regulation (EU) 2023/2854. It is not a generic compliance method. The Data Act is horizontal (so sectoral overlays matter every time), bilateral on most obligations (so role mapping is load-bearing), graduated in its trade-secret protections (so single-step refusal logic is wrong), and time-layered in its applicability (so temporal scoping is part of every answer). The method handles all four.

---

## The seven-step flow

Every substantive Data Act question goes through these steps in order. The skill does not skip steps and does not run them in parallel. A step's output feeds the next step. If a step cannot be completed because the facts are missing, the skill stops and asks (see "When to ask vs when to proceed" below); it does not fabricate the missing fact and continue.

### Step 1: Scope check

Determine whether the Data Act applies to the matter at all. The Data Act has personal scope (Art. 1(3)), material scope (Art. 1(2)), and explicit carve-outs (Art. 1(6)). The carve-outs include criminal law enforcement, customs, taxation, national security, voluntary data-sharing arrangements between private and public entities, and several specific Union law instruments. The Data Act is also without prejudice to GDPR, ePrivacy, IP law, and consumer protection law (Art. 1(5), (8), (9)).

Most matters are in scope, but the carve-outs catch real cases. Do not skip the check. If a scenario involves law-enforcement access to vehicle telematics, the answer is in Art. 1(6), not in Ch V. If a scenario involves voluntary data sharing under a pre-existing private arrangement, Ch III may not apply at all (FAQ Q3, Art. 44(1)).

For products and services, scope also depends on whether the connected product was "placed on the Union market" (Art. 2(22), FAQ Q8). Placement is the first transfer of ownership after manufacture, on a per-individual-product basis. Products manufactured in the EU for export are not placed on the EU market. Products imported by a consumer for personal use after purchase abroad are not placed on the EU market. Mobile products (ships, planes, cars) merely circulating in the EU are not placed unless ownership transfer happened in the EU (FAQ Q9). Registration in a Member State is an indicator of placement but not conclusive.

### Step 2: Chapter identification

Identify which chapter or chapters govern the matter. The Data Act's chapters are functionally distinct and the operative rules differ substantially between them. A scenario about cloud switching is Ch VI; about user IoT access is Ch II; about mandatory sharing under another EU law is Ch III; about contractual unfairness between enterprises is Ch IV; about public-sector exceptional-need requests is Ch V; about third-country government access is Ch VII; about data-space interoperability or smart contracts is Ch VIII.

Many real matters span chapters. A connected-car aftermarket scenario typically involves Ch II (user access) and frequently Ch IV (unfair terms in the data-sharing contract) and may touch Ch VI (telematics platform switching). Cross-chapter scenarios need a separate analysis under each chapter, not a single blended one. If two chapters point in different directions, that's not a defect of the analysis; that's the regulation's structure.

### Step 3: Role mapping

For every entity in the scenario, identify which Data Act role(s) the entity plays. The Data Act roles are: user (Art. 2(12)), data holder (Art. 2(13)), data recipient (Art. 2(14)), third party in the Art. 5 sense, customer of a data processing service (Art. 2(30)), provider of a data processing service, public sector body (Art. 2(28)). Separately, identify the GDPR roles each entity plays for any personal data involved: data subject, controller, joint controller, processor.

These mappings do not run in parallel. A single entity can play multiple roles, and the roles can shift across the scenario. The manufacturer of a connected product is not always the data holder (FAQ Q21). A user who is not the data subject becomes a controller under GDPR when requesting personal data generated by the product (Recital 34 of the Data Act). A data holder can simultaneously be a trade-secret holder under Directive (EU) 2016/943. A company can be a user for one product and a data holder for another (FAQ Q34), but it cannot be both user and data holder for the same data.

The role determines which obligations apply. An output that doesn't make role mapping explicit is hiding the most important judgment the analysis required. Make it explicit; show the role for each entity for each phase of the scenario.

### Step 4: Fact-category sorting

Classify the data in scope along independent dimensions. Each data point has values on each dimension simultaneously, and the operative rules depend on the combination.

- **Personal vs non-personal data.** Art. 2(3), (4). Drives the GDPR overlay. Mixed datasets (inextricably linked personal and non-personal) trigger GDPR for the personal part (Art. 1(5), Recital 7).
- **Raw / pre-processed vs inferred or derived.** Recital 15. Only raw and pre-processed data are in scope of the Ch II access right. Inferred or derived data resulting from proprietary algorithms or substantial additional investment are out of scope. Anonymisation or pseudonymisation does not transform raw data into derived data (FAQ Q13).
- **Product data vs related service data vs other.** Art. 2(15), (16). Both are in scope of Ch II if other conditions are met. Other data (e.g. content, internal infrastructure data) is not.
- **Readily available vs not readily available.** Art. 2(17). Data the data holder can lawfully obtain without disproportionate effort. If a connected product's design prevents external storage or transmission, the data is not readily available (FAQ Q5a, Recital 20).
- **Content vs non-content.** Recital 16. Textual, audio, or audiovisual content typically covered by IP is excluded. Sensor outputs that happen to be image-based (collision warning cameras, agricultural plant-health cameras) are in scope because they are not destined for human consumption (FAQ Q6).
- **Trade-secret data vs non-trade-secret data.** Directive (EU) 2016/943, Art. 2(1). Trade-secret status triggers the graduated protection regime under Art. 4(6)-(8) and 5(9)-(11). The data holder identifies trade secrets and proposes safeguards; status alone does not defeat access.

A single dataset will hold combinations of these. A telematics feed from a connected car typically contains: personal data (about the driver) inextricably linked with non-personal data (about road conditions), pre-processed sensor outputs (in scope) alongside derived risk scores (out of scope), product data (engine telemetry) alongside related service data (navigation usage), some of it readily available and some of it not (depending on architecture), no content, and some elements claimed as trade secrets. The analysis must sort all of this before it can apply the operative rules.

### Step 5: Limb-by-limb application

Apply the operative articles by testing each limb separately. Data Act provisions are typically multi-limb cumulative tests. Mis-applying them by treating limbs as alternatives, or by skipping a limb because it "obviously applies", is the most common Data Act analytical error.

Examples:

- **Art. 2(5) "connected product"**: (i) obtains, generates, or collects data about use or environment, AND (ii) communicates that data via electronic communications service, physical connection, or on-device access, AND (iii) primary function is not storing, processing, or transmitting data on behalf of any party other than the user. A server or router fails limb (iii). A prototype is out of scope on a separate ground (Recital 14, FAQ Q7).
- **Art. 2(6) "related service"**: (i) digital service other than ECS, (ii) connected with the product at purchase/rent/lease such that absence would prevent functions, OR subsequently connected to add/update/adapt functions. Two basic conditions per FAQ Q10: bidirectional data exchange AND impact on connected-product behaviour. Power supply and connectivity are not related services. Repair and maintenance are not related services.
- **Art. 4(8) trade-secret refusal**: (i) exceptional circumstances, AND (ii) the data holder is a trade-secret holder, AND (iii) the data holder demonstrates highly likely serious economic damage from disclosure, AND (iv) the demonstration is based on objective elements, AND (v) the refusal is on a case-by-case basis, AND (vi) the refusal is in writing without undue delay, AND (vii) the competent authority is notified. All cumulative. Missing any limb means the refusal is not lawful.
- **Art. 15(1) "exceptional need"**: either limb (a) (public emergency response, alternative means infeasible) OR limb (b) (non-emergency, non-personal data only, specific legal task, alternatives exhausted including market purchase). Limb (b) is not available against microenterprises or small enterprises (Art. 15(2)). Limb (a) does not require market-purchase exhaustion; limb (b) does (FAQ Q44, Q45).

The skill writes out each limb explicitly when the analysis depends on more than one. Do not collapse limbs into a single sentence. Do not assume a limb is satisfied because the others are.

### Step 6: Cross-regime gate check

Before producing an output, run the cross-regime gates. Each gate file in `references/gates/` defines a specific overlay that may modify the Data Act answer. The gates are:

- **GDPR / ePrivacy overlay** (`gdpr-overlay.md`). Always applicable if personal data or terminal-equipment access is involved. Art. 1(5) sets the conflict rule.
- **Trade Secrets Directive overlay** (`trade-secrets-directive.md`). Applicable if any data in scope is claimed as a trade secret.
- **DMA gatekeeper exclusion** (`dma-gatekeeper.md`). Applicable to any Art. 5 third-party request and to the data recipient's permitted onward sharing under Art. 6(2)(d).
- **Sectoral lex specialis** (`sectoral-lex-specialis.md`). Warn-only catalogue. Applicable if the scenario involves a regulated sector (vehicles, medical devices, financial services, energy, agriculture, AI systems, eIDAS, NIS2 covered entities, Cyber Resilience Act covered products).
- **Member-state implementing law** (`member-state.md`). Warn-only catalogue. Applicable to enforcement (Art. 37, 40), competent-authority designation, and dispute settlement.

A gate result can change the Data Act answer (e.g. GDPR prevails in conflict, Art. 1(5)), narrow it (e.g. gatekeeper cannot be a Ch II third party), or simply flag a parallel obligation the user needs to handle separately (e.g. MDR for a medical device). The output reports the gate result alongside the Data Act analysis; it does not bury the gate or treat it as an afterthought.

### Step 7: Synthesis with current-law-vs-proposal discipline

Produce the output. Before doing so, check the Digital Omnibus tracker (`sources/digital-omnibus-amendments-tracker.md`) for any proposed amendment that affects the provisions used in the analysis. The Commission tabled COM(2025) 833 final on 19 November 2025 with significant proposed Data Act changes (new trade-secret refusal ground for third-country misuse risk; narrowed Ch V circumstances; Art. 31 custom-built carve-out expanded; Art. 25 early-termination penalties; absorption of DGA, Open Data Directive, Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation, and Platform-to-Business Regulation into a new chapter structure). As of the skill's source date, the proposal is in co-legislator negotiation and is not law.

If an affected provision is used in the analysis, the output states the current law first and flags the proposed amendment second. The current law is what governs today; the proposal is forward-looking awareness. Never present a proposal as if it were law. Never omit the proposal when it is materially relevant; the user is making decisions today that will be affected if the proposal is adopted.

## Cognitive disciplines

These apply across all seven steps. They are habits the skill must maintain throughout the analysis, not separate stages.

### Illustrative vs exhaustive listings

The Data Act and its recitals frequently use phrases like "in particular", "such as", "including", and "for example" before lists. These are illustrative, not exhaustive. Treating an illustrative list as closed is a high-frequency Data Act error.

Two consequential examples:

- **Recital 31 objective elements for Art. 4(8) and 5(11) refusal.** The recital lists "the enforceability of trade secrets protection in third countries, the nature and level of confidentiality of the data requested, and the uniqueness and novelty of the connected product." The list is introduced by "in particular." It is not the closed set of admissible elements. A data holder may rely on other objective elements that demonstrate highly likely serious economic damage; conversely, ticking these three boxes does not satisfy the test if the damage demonstration is weak.
- **Art. 31(1) custom-built carve-out for data processing services.** The article applies to services "of which the majority of main features has been custom-built to accommodate the specific needs of an individual customer or where all components have been developed for the purposes of an individual customer." The carve-out has cumulative qualifying limbs ("majority of main features" or "all components") plus a separate negative limb ("not offered at broad commercial scale via the service catalogue"). Reading the qualifying limb as one of several illustrative possibilities is wrong; reading the negative limb as merely illustrative is also wrong.

The skill reads each list with attention to its framing word. When in doubt, treat the list as illustrative and the operative test as the principle the list illustrates.

### Role bifurcation

The same legal entity can play multiple Data Act roles and multiple GDPR roles at once, and the roles can shift across phases of a scenario. The skill does not assume role permanence. Examples:

- A car manufacturer that is the data holder at purchase becomes the controller under GDPR for personal data the car generates. If the manufacturer outsources data holding to a fleet-management company under contract, the data-holder role shifts; the controller role may or may not shift depending on who determines processing purposes and means.
- A SaaS vendor that is a customer of an IaaS provider for its own backend is, in that relationship, the user of the IaaS service. In its relationship with its own SaaS customers, it is the provider of a data processing service. Ch VI obligations apply to both directions.
- An employer that gives an employee a connected work device may be the user under Art. 2(12). The employee interacting with the device may be the data subject for any personal data generated. Recital 34 places the user-not-data-subject in the controller role under GDPR.

Map the roles per entity per phase. Show the mapping in the output. Do not hide it in prose.

### Bilateral handbrakes and graduated protection

The Data Act has three handbrake regimes that are commonly mis-applied:

- **Safety / security handbrake (Art. 4(2)).** Restricts or refuses access if processing could undermine security requirements laid down in EU or national law, resulting in serious adverse effects to health, safety, or security of natural persons. Negotiated bilaterally between user and data holder. Requires notification to the competent authority. Is not a unilateral right of the data holder.
- **Trade-secret handbrake for users (Art. 4(6)-(8)).** Three-stage ladder. (i) Identify trade secrets and agree safeguards prior to disclosure (Art. 4(6)). (ii) Withhold or suspend if no agreement or if the user fails to implement or undermines confidentiality (Art. 4(7)). (iii) Refuse on a case-by-case basis only if the trade-secret-holder data holder demonstrates highly likely serious economic damage based on objective elements (Art. 4(8)). All require competent-authority notification.
- **Trade-secret handbrake for third parties (Art. 5(9)-(11)).** Parallel three-stage ladder for Art. 5 third-party recipients. Same structure, different reference to whom the data is being shared.

These are graduated. A data holder cannot jump straight to refusal under Art. 4(8) or 5(11) without first attempting safeguards under (6) and (9). Outputs that treat refusal as the default response to a trade-secret claim are wrong. The default is access with safeguards; refusal is exceptional.

### The circular trap

The trade-secret regime contains a circular trap. Recital 31 says "Data holders cannot, in principle, refuse a data access request under this Regulation solely on the basis that certain data is considered to be a trade secret, as this would subvert the intended effects of this Regulation." Art. 4(8) and 5(11) refusal is "exceptional" and requires objective demonstration of "highly likely serious economic damage", which Recital 31 explains means "serious AND irreparable economic loss."

The trap: treating Art. 4(8) as a one-step "trade secret therefore refusal" rule. The correct reading is: trade-secret status is necessary but not sufficient for refusal. Sufficiency requires the additional showing of highly likely serious AND irreparable economic damage, demonstrated on objective elements, case-by-case, in writing without undue delay, with competent-authority notification. The same trap exists in Recital 31's "serious economic damage" language: the recital clarifies that "serious economic damage implies serious AND irreparable economic loss." Treating "serious" alone as sufficient (without "irreparable") is the conjunction error.

The skill flags this trap when drafting any Art. 4(8) or 5(11) analysis, refusal letter, or advisory note. The trap is the single most common Data Act drafting error in the practitioner literature.

### Temporal applicability

Almost every Data Act answer needs a temporal scope check. The relevant dates from Art. 50:

- **Entry into force**: 11 January 2024.
- **General application**: 12 September 2025.
- **Design obligation under Art. 3(1)**: applies only to connected products and related services placed on the market after 12 September 2026.
- **Chapter III (mandatory sharing under other EU law)**: applies in relation to obligations under EU or national law that enter into force after 12 September 2025.
- **Chapter IV (unfair contractual terms)**: applies to contracts concluded after 12 September 2025. Applies from 12 September 2027 to contracts concluded on or before 12 September 2025 if those contracts are of indefinite duration or expire at least 10 years from 11 January 2024. So a 2024 perpetual data-sharing agreement comes under Art. 13 unfairness control on 12 September 2027, not before.
- **Chapter II readily-available data**: only data generated or collected after the entry into application is in scope (FAQ Q4, Q34a). Pre-application data is out of scope of mandatory sharing even if the connected product was placed on the market before 12 September 2025.

The skill states the relevant temporal scope when the answer depends on it. Do not assume "the Data Act applies now" without checking.

### Verbatim citation

When the skill makes a claim about what the Data Act says, it quotes from `sources/regulation-2023-2854.md`. It does not paraphrase from training data, and it does not paraphrase from the FAQ when the underlying regulatory text would do.

The reason is reliability. Training data may be inaccurate, outdated, or reflect a draft version. The regulation file is the source of truth for the skill. Citing the file by article and paragraph (e.g. "Art. 4(2)", "Art. 25(2)(g)", "Recital 31") and quoting the operative language is how the skill avoids drift and how the user can verify the analysis.

Quotes should be short, accurate, and operative. Use ellipsis to elide non-load-bearing material. Do not invent quotes. If a passage is not in the regulation file, the analysis must not rely on it.

### FAQ framing

The Commission FAQ (sources/faq-v1-4.md) is non-authoritative. The FAQ opens with: "This document should not be considered as representative of the European Commission's official position. The replies to the FAQs do not extend in any way the rights and obligations deriving from applicable legislation nor introduce any additional requirement. The expressed views are not authoritative and cannot prejudge any future actions the European Commission may take, including potential positions before the Court of Justice of the European Union."

Outputs that rely on the FAQ must frame it as the Commission's interpretation, not as binding. Use phrases like "the Commission, in its FAQ guidance, takes the view that..." or "the Commission's published interpretation is...". Never write "the FAQ requires", "the FAQ provides", or "under the FAQ" as if the FAQ were law. The regulation is law; the FAQ is interpretation.

When the FAQ and the regulation appear to diverge, the regulation governs. When the FAQ adds detail the regulation does not address (e.g. FAQ Q5a on edge processing, Q22a on technical and practical requirements, Q58a on PaaS/SaaS coverage), the skill treats the FAQ as persuasive but not conclusive, and notes that a court has not ruled on the question.

## Output and clarification rules

### When to ask vs when to proceed

Practitioners hate two failure modes: AI that refuses to start until every fact is supplied, and AI that confidently fabricates facts to produce an answer. The skill avoids both.

Proceed when:
- The missing fact is binary and reasonable assumptions in both directions can be analysed in parallel (e.g. "if the data holder is the trade-secret holder, then... if it is not, then...").
- The missing fact is unlikely to change the legal conclusion (e.g. the precise category of an SME for an analysis that doesn't turn on size).
- The user has signalled a hypothetical or sketch-level question ("walk me through how Art. 5 works").

Ask when:
- The missing fact is single-point and changes the conclusion (e.g. is the data recipient an SME for Art. 9(4) compensation purposes; was the contract concluded before or after 12 September 2025 for Art. 13 applicability).
- The role mapping is ambiguous and proceeding would require assuming a role assignment.
- The scenario could involve a sectoral overlay (vehicles, medical devices, financial services, energy, AI systems) and the skill needs to know which one before running the gate.
- The Digital Omnibus proposal materially changes the answer and the user has not indicated whether they want a current-law analysis, a proposal analysis, or both.

When asking, ask one question at a time. Do not present a clarification checklist. The user's question contained information; the skill uses what was given before asking for more.

When proceeding with an assumption, state the assumption in the output ("I assume X; if X is wrong, the analysis differs as follows...").

### What every Data Act answer must contain

A complete Data Act answer contains, at minimum:

1. A statement of the scope and chapter(s) engaged.
2. An explicit role mapping for each entity, including any GDPR role overlap.
3. The relevant fact-category classification of the data in scope.
4. The operative articles applied, with each limb tested.
5. Any cross-regime gate result that modifies the answer.
6. The temporal applicability of the conclusion.
7. A current-law-vs-proposal flag if the Digital Omnibus materially affects the analysis.
8. Where the analysis depends on facts the skill assumed, the assumptions stated explicitly.

These are not an output template; they are the substantive content the analysis must cover. The format (prose, bullets, table) follows the scenario card or template in use. What matters is that the substance is present, in order, and visible to the reader.

### When to refuse

The skill refuses to produce an output when:

- The user asks the skill to draft a refusal under Art. 4(8) or 5(11) on the basis of trade-secret status alone, without the additional showing of highly likely serious and irreparable economic damage. This is the circular trap; producing the refusal would encode the trap into a client deliverable. The skill explains the trap and asks for the additional facts.
- The user asks the skill to opine on a sectoral question (vehicles, medical devices, DORA, NIS2, CRA, AI Act, eIDAS, energy) without acknowledging the sectoral overlay. The skill flags the overlay and either runs the horizontal Data Act analysis with a clear gate (preferred) or redirects to specialist counsel for the sectoral piece.
- The user asks the skill to interpret a Member State implementing law that has not yet been notified or adopted. The skill flags the gap and provides only the horizontal Data Act analysis.
- The user asks the skill to act as the Court of Justice of the European Union. The skill is a practitioner aid, not a tribunal. It can analyse, it can predict, it cannot adjudicate.

Refusal is not "I cannot help with this." Refusal is "the analysis you're asking for would be wrong as posed; here is what we need to do differently."

## How this method connects to the rest of the skill

The seven-step flow is the spine. The gates (`references/gates/*.md`) feed into Step 6. The gotchas catalogue (`references/gotchas.md`) is the cross-cutting failure-mode list the skill checks throughout the flow. The scenario cards (forthcoming in Phase 5) are pre-walked applications of the flow for the common scenario types. The templates (forthcoming in Phase 6) are drafting outputs that assume the analysis has been done.

If at any point the skill finds itself producing output without having run the flow, the output is broken and must be redone. The flow is not optional. It is the difference between a Data Act answer and a Data Act guess.
