# GDPR and ePrivacy overlay

This gate runs whenever any personal data is in scope, whenever the scenario involves access to or storage in terminal equipment, or whenever the user is a natural person. In practice this is most Data Act scenarios involving consumers, employees, drivers, patients, or any other identified or identifiable natural person.

The gate is operationally complex. The Data Act does not regulate personal data protection; it complements GDPR and ePrivacy. The interaction is governed by Art. 1(5) of the Data Act, which is the bridge clause, and by sector-specific provisions throughout Ch II that condition data holder obligations on the existence of a valid GDPR legal basis. Mis-applying the bridge — for example, treating the Data Act as a legal basis under Art. 6 GDPR, or assuming GDPR and the Data Act run on parallel tracks without interaction — is one of the most common Data Act analytical failures.

The gate's structure: scope and applicability; the Art. 1(5) bridge clause; Case A vs Case B (user-as-data-subject vs user-not-data-subject); ePrivacy and terminal equipment; joint controllership scenarios; mixed datasets; data subject rights under the Data Act vs GDPR; supervisory authority competence; what the skill produces in the output.

---

## When the gate applies

The gate runs whenever any of the following is true:

- The data in scope includes personal data, including in datasets where personal and non-personal data are inextricably linked
- The user is a natural person (consumer, employee, driver, household member, sole trader acting in personal capacity)
- The user is an enterprise but the data generated by the use of the connected product or related service is personal data of natural persons (drivers, employees, customers, patients)
- The scenario involves storage of data in, or access to data stored in, the terminal equipment of an end user (Internet of Things equipment connected directly or indirectly to a public communications network qualifies as terminal equipment under Recital 36)
- The scenario involves processing that may amount to profiling under Art. 4(4) GDPR
- The data in scope includes special categories of personal data under Art. 9 GDPR
- The data subject is not the user and the user is making a Data Act access request that would result in personal data being made available to the user or to a third party
- The scenario involves transfer of personal data to a third country

When in doubt, the skill runs the gate. The cost of a missed GDPR or ePrivacy obligation is high; the cost of a redundant check is low.

## The Art. 1(5) bridge clause

Art. 1(5) of the Data Act is the operative bridge between the Data Act and data protection law. The provision has three operative limbs that must be read together.

**Limb one: without prejudice.** "This Regulation is without prejudice to Union and national law on the protection of personal data, privacy and confidentiality of communications and integrity of terminal equipment, which shall apply to personal data processed in connection with the rights and obligations laid down herein, in particular Regulations (EU) 2016/679 and (EU) 2018/1725 and Directive 2002/58/EC, including the powers and competences of supervisory authorities and the rights of data subjects."

GDPR, EUDPR (the EU institutions' data protection regulation), and the ePrivacy Directive apply in full to any personal data processing arising in the context of Data Act activities. The Data Act does not displace, narrow, or modify these regimes. Supervisory authority powers (Art. 51 et seq. GDPR) and data subject rights (Arts. 12-22 GDPR) are unaffected.

**Limb two: complementarity for Ch II rights.** "Insofar as users are data subjects, the rights laid down in Chapter II of this Regulation shall complement the rights of access by data subjects and rights to data portability under Articles 15 and 20 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679."

For a user who is also the data subject, Ch II rights are additive to GDPR rights. The user has Art. 15 GDPR access and Art. 20 GDPR portability rights for any personal data processed about them. The Data Act adds, on top, the right to access readily available product data and related service data (regardless of whether the data is personal, non-personal, or mixed), the right to port the data to a third party in real time and on a structured machine-readable basis, and the right to apply these rights to data processed on any GDPR legal basis (not just consent or contract as required by Art. 20 GDPR). FAQ Q18 makes the complementarity explicit. Recital 35 elaborates.

**Limb three: conflict rule.** "In the event of a conflict between this Regulation and Union law on the protection of personal data or privacy, or national legislation adopted in accordance with such Union law, the relevant Union or national law on the protection of personal data or privacy shall prevail."

Where the Data Act would require disclosure of personal data and the disclosure would breach GDPR (no valid legal basis, no derogation under Art. 9 GDPR for special categories, no adequate transfer mechanism for third-country transfers), GDPR prevails. The Data Act access right does not override GDPR constraints. FAQ Q1 confirms.

The bridge clause means two things in practice. First, GDPR compliance is a precondition of Data Act compliance for any personal data processing. Second, the Data Act does not create a GDPR legal basis. The data holder cannot rely on the Data Act access right as the Art. 6(1) GDPR legal basis for disclosure to a non-data-subject user or third party. The legal basis must come from elsewhere.

## Case A: user is the data subject

Where the user is the data subject (a consumer using a connected product they own, a driver using their personal car), the legal-basis question for the data holder's disclosure is straightforward.

The user requests access to their own personal data. The data holder discloses to the data subject. This is the GDPR Art. 15 (right of access) and Art. 20 (right to data portability) scenario, with the Data Act extending and supplementing those rights. The data holder's legal basis for the disclosure is one of the Art. 6(1) GDPR bases the data holder was already relying on for processing the data: contract performance (Art. 6(1)(b)), legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f)), or whichever basis is documented in the data holder's records of processing activities.

The Data Act access right itself is not a separate Art. 6(1) legal basis. It is a statutory channel through which the data subject exercises GDPR rights enhanced by Data Act extensions. Recital 7 of the Data Act states: "This Regulation does not constitute a legal basis for the collection or generation of personal data by the data holder."

Key practical points in Case A:

- **Real-time portability is in scope.** Unlike Art. 20 GDPR, which requires data portability only "where technically feasible," Art. 5(1) Data Act requires data holders to make data available "continuously and in real-time" where relevant and technically feasible. The Data Act ensures technical feasibility is engineered for; it does not stop at the GDPR's optional standard.
- **All data is in scope, not just data the user provided.** Art. 20 GDPR is limited to data the data subject has provided to the controller. The Data Act covers all readily available product data and related service data regardless of how it was generated (actively, passively, by sensors, by inference within the raw/pre-processed range). Recital 35 makes this contrast explicit.
- **Legal basis of the underlying processing does not matter.** Art. 20 GDPR is limited to processing under Art. 6(1)(a) (consent) or Art. 6(1)(b) (contract). Art. 5 Data Act applies regardless of the legal basis. A data subject can port telematics data even if the underlying processing is on Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests.
- **Art. 5(3) GDPR special category protections remain.** A contract under Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR (e.g. between the data holder and the data subject) does not authorise the data holder or a third party to process Art. 9 GDPR special category data without a separate Art. 9 condition. Recital 35 of the Data Act explicitly reserves this: "in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679, a contract does not allow for the processing of special categories of personal data by the data holder or the third party."

## Case B: user is not the data subject

Where the user is an enterprise (employer, fleet operator, hospital, rental company) and the data generated by the connected product or related service is personal data of natural persons who are not the user (employees, drivers, patients, renters), the legal-basis question is significantly more complex. This is the high-risk scenario for compliance.

Recital 34 of the Data Act is the operative interpretive provision: "Where the user is not the data subject but an enterprise, including a sole trader, and not in cases of shared household use of the connected product, the user is considered to be a controller."

The user becomes a controller under GDPR for the personal data it requests under Art. 4(1) of the Data Act or that it requests to be ported to a third party under Art. 5(1). The user must comply with all GDPR controller obligations: have a valid Art. 6(1) legal basis (and where applicable Art. 9(2) condition), provide data subject information under Arts. 13/14, respond to data subject rights requests, maintain records of processing activities, conduct DPIAs where required.

The data holder's position is parallel but distinct. The data holder is also a controller for its own processing of the same personal data (e.g. for product improvement, related service provision, contractual performance with the user). The data holder's disclosure to the user is a separate processing operation that requires its own legal basis.

Art. 4(12) of the Data Act conditions the data holder's disclosure to a non-data-subject user on the existence of a valid GDPR legal basis: "Where the user is not the data subject whose personal data is requested, any personal data generated by the use of a connected product or related service shall be made available by the data holder to the user only where there is a valid legal basis for processing under Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and, where relevant, the conditions of Article 9 of that Regulation and of Article 5(3) of Directive 2002/58/EC are fulfilled."

Art. 5(7) of the Data Act applies the same rule to disclosure to a third party.

The legal basis question: which Art. 6(1) GDPR basis can the data holder rely on? The Commission's FAQ Q25a takes the view that the data holder must make a case-by-case assessment. Candidate bases:

- **Art. 6(1)(a) consent.** The data subject consents to the disclosure to the user. Practical for some scenarios (consumer-facing products with consent flows); impractical for others (industrial IoT, employee monitoring). Consent must meet the GDPR standard: freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, revocable, and not the basis where there is a clear imbalance of power.
- **Art. 6(1)(b) contract.** Disclosure is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party. Workable where the data subject is a party to the contract (e.g. car-sharing agreement). Not workable where the data subject is the employee of the user.
- **Art. 6(1)(c) legal obligation.** Disclosure is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject. Recital 7 of the Data Act explicitly rejects the Data Act itself as such a legal obligation for non-data-subject access. Other Union or national law obligations may apply.
- **Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests.** Disclosure is necessary for the legitimate interests pursued by the data holder or by the user, balanced against the rights and freedoms of the data subject. Likely the most commonly relied-on basis but requires a documented legitimate interests assessment (LIA). The user's interest in receiving the data, the data holder's interest in complying with the Data Act, and the data subject's reasonable expectations all feature in the balancing.

Where consent is not available and Art. 6(1)(b) is not engaged, the practical fallback is often Art. 6(1)(f) supplemented by anonymisation or pseudonymisation. The Commission's FAQ Q25a takes the view that anonymisation is a valid alternative: "Depending on the circumstances of the request, the controller could explore whether providing the data is necessary for the performance of the contract with the data subject or service legitimate interest of data holder or a third party." The data holder may "alternatively provide anonymised data."

Anonymisation removes the data from GDPR scope entirely (Recital 26 GDPR). It is the cleanest solution where the user's purpose does not require personal data. The data holder should always assess whether anonymisation or aggregation meets the user's need before relying on a Art. 6 legal basis for personal data disclosure.

**The under-explored point.** Case B exposes a gap that practitioners regularly miss: the Data Act gives the user the access right, but Art. 4(12) and 5(7) make the right conditional on the data holder establishing a GDPR legal basis. Where the data holder cannot establish a basis (e.g. no consent, no contract with the data subject, weak legitimate interest in light of intrusive monitoring), the access right cannot be exercised for personal data. The Commission's view is that anonymisation is the route; in practice, the user often wants the personal data specifically (for accident investigation, performance monitoring, billing reconciliation) and anonymisation defeats the purpose. The deliverable should be explicit about this gap.

## ePrivacy and terminal equipment

The ePrivacy Directive (Directive 2002/58/EC) protects the confidentiality of communications and the integrity of terminal equipment. Art. 5(3) ePrivacy requires consent for the storing of information, or the gaining of access to information already stored, in the terminal equipment of a subscriber or user, except where strictly necessary for the provision of an information society service explicitly requested by the user or for the sole purpose of the transmission of a communication.

Recital 36 of the Data Act applies ePrivacy to connected products: "Access to any data stored in and accessed from terminal equipment is subject to Directive 2002/58/EC and requires the consent of the subscriber or user within the meaning of that Directive unless it is strictly necessary for the provision of an information society service explicitly requested by the user or by the subscriber or for the sole purpose of the transmission of a communication. ... Internet of Things equipment is considered to be terminal equipment if it is directly or indirectly connected to a public communications network."

Two operative consequences.

**Connected products are typically terminal equipment.** Most connected products fall within the ePrivacy Directive's terminal equipment concept. A connected car, a smart appliance, a wearable, a smart meter that connects to a public network all qualify. Art. 5(3) ePrivacy applies to information stored in or accessed from these products.

**Consent is required for storage or access.** Where the connected product stores information and that information is accessed or further stored beyond what is strictly necessary for the user-requested service, consent under Art. 5(3) ePrivacy is required. The consent standard is the GDPR consent standard (Art. 4(11) and Art. 7 GDPR). The Strictly Necessary exception is narrow.

For Data Act scenarios, this means:

- **Data holder access to data stored on the connected product.** If the data is read from the product's on-device storage, ePrivacy Art. 5(3) consent is in principle required unless the access is strictly necessary for the related service the user has requested.
- **User access to data stored on the connected product.** The user's own access to their own data is typically permissible without separate consent, but where the connected product is shared (household, fleet, multi-user), one user accessing data attributable to another user implicates both ePrivacy and GDPR.
- **Third-party access via Art. 5 of the Data Act.** A third party reading data from on-device storage requires the underlying ePrivacy consent. The Art. 5(1) Data Act right does not displace this.

The practical pattern: data holders rely on consent for non-strictly-necessary on-device data collection, separately from the GDPR consent or other legal basis for subsequent processing. Two layers of consent or other authorisation are often needed: ePrivacy for the storage/access from terminal equipment, and GDPR for the subsequent processing of any personal data so obtained.

The proposed ePrivacy Regulation (Commission proposal of 10 January 2017, COM(2017) 10) would have updated and replaced the 2002 Directive but is in extended legislative limbo and has not been adopted as of the skill's source date. The skill relies on the current Directive plus national implementing law.

## Joint controllership

Where the data holder and the user are joint controllers within the meaning of Art. 26 GDPR, both must agree the allocation of GDPR responsibilities in a joint controller arrangement. Recital 34 of the Data Act flags this: "Where the data holder and the user are joint controllers within the meaning of Article 26 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, they are required to determine, in a transparent manner by means of an arrangement between them, their respective responsibilities for compliance with that Regulation."

Joint controllership is fact-driven. The CJEU's case law (notably Wirtschaftsakademie C-210/16, Jehovah's Witnesses C-25/17, and Fashion ID C-40/17) establishes that joint controllership exists where two or more entities jointly determine the purposes and means of processing. The arrangement under Art. 26 must reflect the actual roles, and the essence must be made available to data subjects.

For Data Act Case B scenarios, the practical question is: are the data holder and the user joint controllers, or two separate controllers operating in succession? Both characterisations are plausible depending on the facts. A fleet operator that defines what telematics data is collected and for what purpose, jointly with the connected vehicle manufacturer, may be a joint controller with the manufacturer for that processing. A fleet operator that simply requests and receives data the manufacturer has already collected for its own purposes may be a separate downstream controller.

The skill flags joint controllership as a possibility wherever the facts support it. The drafting consequence is a joint controller agreement under Art. 26 GDPR.

## Mixed datasets

A dataset where personal and non-personal data are inextricably linked is governed by GDPR for the personal data, by the Data Act for both, and by the Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2018/1807) for the non-personal data where applicable. Recital 7 of the Data Act addresses this: "Those Union legislative acts provide the basis for sustainable and responsible data processing, including where datasets include a mix of personal and non-personal data."

Operationally, the skill treats the dataset as personal data for the personal portion and applies the GDPR overlay. Anonymisation of personal data does not transform non-personal data; it removes the GDPR overlay for the anonymised portion. Where anonymisation is partial or where re-identification risk is high, the dataset remains personal data in substance.

Practical drafting consequence: data-sharing contracts should address mixed datasets explicitly, allocating responsibility for GDPR compliance (typically to whichever party determines the purposes), specifying anonymisation or pseudonymisation where required, and confirming that the parties have considered re-identification risk.

## Data subject rights under the Data Act vs GDPR

The interaction of data subject rights under the two regimes is non-trivial.

- **Right of access (Art. 15 GDPR).** Survives in full. The Data Act adds the user's access right under Art. 4(1), which for a user-data-subject is complementary. A data subject can exercise either right or both.
- **Right to rectification (Art. 16 GDPR).** Survives in full. The Data Act does not address rectification of personal data. The data subject relies on Art. 16 GDPR.
- **Right to erasure (Art. 17 GDPR).** Survives in full for personal data. The Data Act does not establish a parallel right to erasure of non-personal data (FAQ Q33 confirms: "The Data Act does not establish a 'right to erasure' for non-personal data, similar to the GDPR"). Recital 39 Data Act addresses third-party erasure obligations: "The requirement to erase data when no longer required for the purpose agreed with the user, unless otherwise agreed in relation to non-personal data, complements the data subject's right to erasure pursuant to Article 17 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679."
- **Right to data portability (Art. 20 GDPR).** Survives in full and is significantly extended by Art. 5 Data Act. The Data Act's extension applies to all readily available product and related service data regardless of legal basis (not limited to Art. 6(1)(a) or 6(1)(b) processing), regardless of whether the data was actively provided or passively observed (Art. 20 GDPR's "provided by the data subject" limitation is removed), and with real-time technical feasibility engineered into the data holder's design obligations.
- **Right to object (Art. 21 GDPR).** Survives in full. Particularly relevant for processing on Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests.
- **Rights related to automated decision-making (Art. 22 GDPR).** Survive in full. Art. 6(2)(b) of the Data Act adds a specific prohibition on third parties using received data for profiling unless strictly necessary to provide the service, beyond what Art. 22 GDPR requires.

The skill ensures the deliverable does not give the impression that Data Act rights replace GDPR rights or that GDPR rights are unavailable for any matter touched by the Data Act. Where a data subject has both rights available, the skill identifies both and explains which is more advantageous for the matter at hand.

## Supervisory authority competence

Art. 37(3) of the Data Act: "The supervisory authorities responsible for monitoring the application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 shall be responsible for monitoring the application of this Regulation insofar as the protection of personal data is concerned. Chapters VI and VII of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 shall apply mutatis mutandis."

The supervisory authorities (DPAs) are competent for the personal data aspects of Data Act enforcement. GDPR Chapters VI (independent supervisory authorities) and VII (cooperation and consistency) apply mutatis mutandis. The DPA's tasks and powers under Art. 57-58 GDPR are exercised in the Data Act context for personal data.

For mixed enforcement (personal data plus Data Act-specific issues), the DPA and the Data Act competent authority (designated under Art. 37(1)) must cooperate. The skill does not predict how cooperation will work in practice; this depends on Member State implementation and inter-authority agreements. The skill flags the dual competence and notes that complaints may be lodged with either authority for matters touching both regimes.

Art. 40(4) confirms: "For infringements of the obligations laid down in Chapter II, III and V of this Regulation, the supervisory authorities responsible for monitoring the application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 may within their scope of competence impose administrative fines in accordance with Article 83 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and up to the amount referred to in Article 83(5) of that Regulation."

The DPAs may impose Art. 83(5) GDPR fines (the higher band: up to EUR 20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher) for Data Act Chapter II, III, and V infringements within their competence. This is a high-consequence enforcement track and a strong reason for early DPA engagement in any Data Act matter touching personal data.

## What the skill produces in the output

When the gate is run, the output includes a dedicated "GDPR and ePrivacy overlay" section. Minimum content:

- Identification of any personal data in scope.
- Case A or Case B characterisation, with the consequences for legal basis.
- The Art. 6(1) GDPR legal basis the data holder relies on or must establish, with reasoning.
- Where Art. 9 GDPR special categories are in scope, the Art. 9(2) condition.
- Where terminal equipment is in scope, the ePrivacy Art. 5(3) consent or strictly necessary analysis.
- Where joint controllership is plausible, a flag and recommendation for an Art. 26 GDPR arrangement.
- Where mixed datasets are involved, an allocation of responsibility.
- Identification of the supervisory authority competent for personal data aspects.
- Where anonymisation is a practical solution, a flag and recommendation.

The GDPR overlay typically modifies the Data Act answer in one of three ways: it confirms the access right is exercisable (legal basis exists, no conflict); it narrows the access right (some personal data cannot be disclosed without an additional condition or without anonymisation); or it blocks the access right (no legal basis available and anonymisation defeats the purpose). The skill states which of the three outcomes applies.

## Sample phrasing

The skill may use phrasing of this kind in deliverables:

> The user [name] is the data subject for the personal data in scope (Case A). Art. 1(5) of the Data Act complements the data subject's GDPR rights with the Ch II access right. The data holder may disclose to the user on the basis of Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR (contract performance), which is the same basis on which the data holder is processing the data for the related service. The Ch II right adds real-time portability and removes the Art. 20 GDPR provided-by-the-data-subject limitation.

Or, for Case B:

> The user [name] is an enterprise (fleet operator); the data subjects are the drivers of the vehicles. Recital 34 of the Data Act characterises the user as a controller for the requested personal data. Art. 4(12) of the Data Act conditions the data holder's disclosure on a valid GDPR legal basis. Consent (Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR) is not workable in the employment context; Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR is not available because the data subjects are not parties to the contract between the data holder and the user. Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR legitimate interests is the candidate basis, supported by a documented LIA balancing the user's interest in fleet management against the drivers' reasonable expectations and privacy rights. The data holder should also consider whether anonymisation of the telematics data meets the user's purpose; if it does, anonymised disclosure removes the data from GDPR scope and resolves the legal-basis question.

The phrasing is calibrated. The skill names the case, names the legal basis, names the conditions, and states the practical recommendation.
