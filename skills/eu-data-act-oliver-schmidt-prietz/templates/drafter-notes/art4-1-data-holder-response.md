# Drafter notes: art4-1-data-holder-response

The Art. 4(1) response is the data holder's primary substantive engagement with the user. Substantive defects in this letter shape the entire later trajectory of the matter, including whether the data holder can credibly reach stage 3 of the trade-secret handbrake later. Good responses lock in the analytical posture early; bad ones invite competent-authority complaint within weeks.

## Substantive risks of using the template

- **Identifying trade secrets late, or not at all in writing.** The data holder bears the trade-secret identification burden under Art. 4(6). The identification must be in the relevant metadata. A response that delivers data without identifying which records are trade-secret-protected has waived the protection for those records. The template's section 3 forces the identification at the response stage; do not defer it to "subsequent communication." The Commission's view in FAQ Q23 is that the identification and the safeguards must be in place prior to disclosure.
- **Proposing disproportionate safeguards.** Stage-1 safeguards are negotiated under Art. 4(6) and the standard is proportionality. Demanding on-premises-only access where remote access is technically secure, or banning all internal sharing within the user organisation, is not proportionate and will be unwound by the competent authority on complaint. The McIntyre OSS enforcement signals (see `references/gates/trade-secrets-directive.md` enforcement-signal section) show that competent authorities have ordered resumption where stage-1 negotiation broke down because the data holder demanded too much. Lead with a proportionate package; reserve harder safeguards for facts that justify them.
- **Conflating "readily available data" exclusion with trade-secret refusal.** Where requested items fall outside Art. 4(1) scope (because they are derived data per Recital 15, not generated in the use of the connected product or related service, or not readily available within Art. 2(17)), the data holder declines on scope grounds, not on trade-secret grounds. Mixing the two confuses the record and may make a later Art. 4(8) refusal harder to sustain on the trade-secret portion. The template forces the distinction in section 2.
- **Asking for verification information beyond what is necessary.** Art. 4(5) bars the data holder from demanding more verification information than necessary. Common over-asks: full corporate documents to confirm an enterprise user; passport copies to confirm a natural-person user; ten-year purchase history to identify the connected product. Where the user has enclosed adequate user-status evidence, do not ask for more.
- **The Art. 4(12) representation.** Where the user is not the data subject, the data holder is conditioning disclosure on the user having a GDPR Art. 6(1) basis. The template treats the user's representation as sufficient and notes that the data holder relies on it. This is reasonable for typical commercial users; for high-risk personal data (special categories, sensitive contexts), the data holder may want to ask for confirmation of the legal basis before disclosing. See `references/gates/gdpr-overlay.md`.
- **Use restrictions notice.** Section 6 is informational, not gatekeeping. The Art. 4(10) and Art. 4(11) restrictions bind the user regardless of whether the data holder notes them. The notice serves the data holder's later enforcement: if the user breaches Art. 4(10) and the matter goes to a competent authority or court, the documented notice strengthens the data holder's position.
- **Real-time delivery hedging.** Art. 4(1) requires continuous and real-time delivery "where relevant and technically feasible." Data holders sometimes overclaim infeasibility to avoid building the capability. The template requires reasons in writing where infeasibility is asserted; if the reasons are weak, expect a complaint. The "without undue delay" qualifier has no fixed numeric value (see `references/gotchas.md` entry 4) but is read by competent authorities as days-to-weeks, not months-to-quarters.

## Common drafting mistakes the drafter should check for

- Saying "we comply" without specifying what is being delivered. A response that does not enumerate the data categories invites the user to argue for broader scope later.
- Failing to provide the metadata. Art. 4(1) explicitly covers the relevant metadata. A delivery without metadata is non-compliant.
- Saying "for free" rather than "free of charge to the user." The data holder may not charge the user under Ch II. Saying "for free" is fine but be precise where the user has indicated potential third-party onward sharing; under Art. 5(1) the data holder may charge the third party but not the user.
- Inserting use restrictions beyond Arts. 4(10) and (11). The data holder cannot contract additional restrictions on the user that the Data Act does not provide for. Art. 4(13) is the only path to a use contract for non-personal data, and even that path is constrained by the prohibition on deriving insights about the user.
- Treating the response as a contract negotiation. The Art. 4(1) right is not contingent on contract; the data holder cannot make compliance conditional on the user accepting additional terms beyond the Art. 4(6) safeguards. A response that bundles the safeguards into a general services agreement that the user must sign is procedurally defective.
- Failing to identify the competent authority for redress. Section 8 of the template references the competent authority of the Member State of the data holder's establishment. Identify the actual Member State; do not leave the placeholder.
- Refusing on trade-secret grounds without engaging stage 1. The Art. 4(7) (withholding) and Art. 4(8) (refusal) routes are sequential, not alternative, to Art. 4(6). A first response that refuses on trade-secret grounds without proposing stage-1 safeguards skips a step the regulation requires.

## Length and tone

Practitioner-direct. The user has asked for data; the data holder is delivering it (with appropriate identification of trade-secret items). This is not an adversarial communication. Where the response includes substantial limitations, the limitations are explained in factual terms, not defensively.
