# Drafter notes: art17-public-sector-request

The Art. 17 request is the public-sector-body-side counterpart to the data holder's Art. 18 decline. The substantive demand on Art. 17 requests is high: ten Art. 17(1) content items, nine Art. 17(2) procedural items, plus Art. 22 cross-border procedure where applicable. The most common drafting failure is under-substantiating the exceptional need under Art. 15(1).

## Substantive risks of using the template

- **Art. 15(1)(a) vs Art. 15(1)(b) is the foundational fork.** Art. 15(1)(a) covers public emergencies. Art. 15(1)(b) covers non-emergency specific public-interest tasks, and is limited to non-personal data. The decline windows differ: 5 working days for emergency requests, 30 working days for other requests (Art. 18(2)). Section 3 of the template forces the fork. Drafters who mix the grounds (treating a non-emergency request as an emergency to gain the shorter decline window) invite a decline under Art. 18(2)(c).
- **Microenterprises and small enterprises are exempt from Art. 15(1)(b).** Art. 15(2). The exemption does not apply to Art. 15(1)(a) emergencies. Drafters must identify the data holder's enterprise size before issuing an Art. 15(1)(b) request. A request directed to an exempt data holder is invalid.
- **Art. 16(2) carve-out for criminal, customs, taxation.** The Ch V regime does not apply to activities for the prevention, investigation, detection or prosecution of criminal or administrative offences, the execution of criminal or administrative penalties, customs, or taxation. Requests for those purposes go through other Union or national law instruments. A misrouted Art. 17 request is procedurally defective.
- **Section 3 (demonstration of exceptional need) is the critical drafting work.** The request must demonstrate the need; the data holder may decline under Art. 18(2)(c) if it does not. Drafters who treat section 3 as a recitation of the regulatory standard rather than as a fact-specific substantiation produce requests that data holders decline routinely.
- **Section 8 (personal data) interlocks with the GDPR.** Art. 17(2)(e) requires the requesting body to demonstrate that non-personal data is insufficient before requesting personal data, and even then to request data in pseudonymised form. Art. 17(2)(i) requires notification to the GDPR supervisory authority. Drafters must run the GDPR overlay. The default posture for non-emergency requests is non-personal-data-only (Art. 15(1)(b) is limited to non-personal data); personal data is available only under Art. 15(1)(a) emergencies and only with the pseudonymisation discipline.
- **Trade-secret discipline under Art. 19(3) is constitutive.** Public sector bodies must, prior to disclosure of trade secrets, take all necessary and appropriate technical and organisational measures to preserve confidentiality. Section 14 of the template commits the requesting body to this. Public sector bodies that have not pre-thought the trade-secret regime invite a decline or a substantial delay while safeguards are negotiated.
- **Public availability under Art. 17(2)(g) is mandatory absent a risk-to-public-security assessment.** The data coordinator publishes the request online unless publication would create a risk to public security. Requesting bodies cannot suppress publication for political or operational convenience.
- **Cross-border under Art. 22 changes the procedure.** Where the data holder is in a different Member State, the requesting body must notify the competent authority of the data holder's Member State, and that competent authority then transmits the request (or rejects it on duly substantiated grounds). Section 19 of the template surfaces this; many requesting bodies forget the Art. 22 procedure and address the data holder directly, exposing the request to procedural challenge.
- **Compensation under Art. 20 is automatic where Art. 15(1)(a) applies.** Emergency-grounded data is provided free of charge (other than by micro/small enterprises which are exempt from the obligation but may claim compensation). Non-emergency grounded data attracts fair compensation. The Art. 17 request does not negotiate compensation; that is for a separate communication, but the request should note awareness.

## Common drafting mistakes the drafter should check for

- Asserting an emergency without identifying the public-emergency declaration or equivalent. "Significant public health concern" or "important policy initiative" are not emergencies within Art. 15(1)(a).
- Failing to demonstrate alternative-means exhaustion under Art. 15(1)(a) second limb or Art. 15(1)(b)(ii). The regulation is explicit: market purchase, existing reporting obligations, and new legislative measures must all be considered and ruled out (or attempted).
- Requesting personal data under Art. 15(1)(b). Art. 15(1)(b) is non-personal data only.
- Requesting personal data without specifying the GDPR safeguards. Art. 17(1)(g) and Art. 17(2)(e).
- Specifying a deadline shorter than the Art. 18(2) decline window. The data holder is entitled to up to 5 or 30 working days to decline or modify; demands for next-day compliance are not compatible with the regulation.
- Omitting the Art. 17(2)(g) data coordinator transmission. Public sector body requests of Member State origin must go through the data coordinator for online publication.
- Forgetting Art. 17(2)(j) - this paragraph does not exist in the regulation; the items go (a) through (i). Drafters should not invent items.
- Treating the requesting body as exempt from Art. 19 obligations on receipt of the data. The obligations attach on receipt; section 5 commits the requesting body in writing.
- Failing to address the Art. 22 cross-border procedure. A misrouted cross-border request is procedurally defective.
- Combining requests from multiple bodies into a single document. Each requesting body issues its own request; combinations weaken the legal posture for each.
- Missing the Digital Omnibus check. The Digital Omnibus proposal includes amendments to Art. 15; the drafter should check `sources/digital-omnibus-amendments-tracker.md` for the current legislative status but should draft on current law.

## Length and tone

Formal, exhaustive, factually grounded. The Art. 17 request is closer to a regulatory order than to a commercial communication; the data holder will scrutinise it for procedural and substantive compliance. The requesting body should produce a request that survives close reading.

The tone is neutral. The requesting body is exercising a regulatory power; threats, urgency-language without basis, and assertions of compliance without substantiation undermine the position.
