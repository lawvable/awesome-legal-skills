# Likelihood of Confusion — DuPont (US) & Masterpiece (Canada)

Apply both frameworks when the search hits a mark that may conflict. Knockout-level analysis requires touching the **big four** factors per jurisdiction; full clearance applies all of them.

---

## United States — DuPont factors

Authority: *In re E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (CCPA 1973). Codified application: TMEP 1207.01.

The thirteen factors:

1. **Similarity of the marks** — appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression. The dominant element gets the most weight; design-plus-words marks are usually dominated by the words.
2. **Similarity of the goods/services** — see `nice-classes.md` for relatedness pairings.
3. **Similarity of trade channels** — same buyers, same outlets?
4. **Conditions under which sales are made** — impulse buy vs. sophisticated purchaser. Higher cost / more deliberate = lower confusion risk.
5. **Fame of the senior mark** — famous marks get broader protection (Polaroid principle).
6. **Number and nature of similar marks on similar goods** — a crowded field dilutes any single mark.
7. **Actual confusion** — rare in knockout context; useful in litigation.
8. **Length of time of concurrent use without confusion** — only relevant if the parties have coexisted.
9. **Variety of goods on which the mark is used** — house marks (Apple, Nike) span more classes.
10. **Market interface** — consent, coexistence, prior dealings.
11. **Right to exclude** — established by registration.
12. **Extent of potential confusion** — de minimis vs. substantial.
13. **Any other established fact probative of effect of use**.

### Knockout-level subset (the big four)

For knockout memos, focus on:

1. **Mark similarity** (sight / sound / meaning / commercial impression)
2. **Goods/services relatedness**
3. **Trade channel overlap**
4. **Senior mark strength**

If three of those four point to confusion, code 🔴. If two, 🟡. If one or zero, 🟢.

### Useful quotes for the memo

> "The fundamental inquiry mandated by §2(d) goes to the cumulative effect of differences in the essential characteristics of the goods and differences in the marks." — *In re Nat'l Data Corp.*, 753 F.2d 1056 (Fed. Cir. 1985).

> "When marks would appear on virtually identical goods or services, the degree of similarity necessary to support a conclusion of likely confusion declines." — *Century 21 Real Estate Corp. v. Century Life of America*, 970 F.2d 874 (Fed. Cir. 1992).

## Canada — Masterpiece / Veuve Clicquot / s. 6(5)

Authorities:

- *Masterpiece Inc v Alavida Lifestyles Inc*, 2011 SCC 27.
- *Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin v Boutiques Cliquot Ltée*, 2006 SCC 23.
- *Mattel, Inc v 3894207 Canada Inc*, 2006 SCC 22.
- *Trademarks Act*, RSC 1985, c T-13, s. 6(5).

Section 6(5) factors (all surrounding circumstances, including):

(a) **Inherent distinctiveness** of the trademarks and the extent to which they have become known.
(b) **Length of time** the trademarks have been in use.
(c) **Nature of the goods, services or business**.
(d) **Nature of the trade**.
(e) **Degree of resemblance** between the trademarks in appearance, sound, or in the ideas suggested by them.

### The Masterpiece refinements

- **Resemblance is paramount.** The Court in Masterpiece treated 6(5)(e) as the most important factor: if the marks look/sound/connote alike, the rest of the analysis is uphill for the junior user.
- **Casual consumer somewhat in a hurry test.** Examine the marks from the perspective of a casual consumer — not a careful side-by-side comparison. First impression and imperfect recollection matter.
- **Geographic considerations are subsumed.** Unlike US common-law analysis, Canadian s. 6 confusion is national in scope for registered marks.
- **No "all of the wares" defense.** A senior registered mark for a broad class of goods can block a junior user even if the senior is only actually using the mark on a narrow subset.

### Veuve Clicquot — fame and depreciation

Section 22 of the Trademarks Act gives owners of famous marks a separate cause of action for **depreciation of goodwill** even absent confusion. Elements:

1. The senior mark is famous in Canada.
2. The junior use is sufficiently linked to the senior mark in the mind of the casual consumer.
3. The junior use is likely to depreciate the goodwill (e.g., dilution, tarnishment, blurring).

If a knockout surfaces a famous mark (Coca-Cola, Tiffany, Veuve Clicquot, well-known house marks), flag s. 22 risk in addition to s. 6 confusion.

### Knockout-level subset (the big three for Canada)

1. **Degree of resemblance** (6(5)(e)) — the dominant factor.
2. **Nature of goods/services and trade** (6(5)(c)+(d)).
3. **Inherent distinctiveness + acquired fame** (6(5)(a)).

### Useful quotes for the memo

> "[T]he test to be applied is a matter of first impression in the mind of a casual consumer somewhat in a hurry who sees the [mark] at a time when he or she has no more than an imperfect recollection of the [other] trade-marks, and does not pause to give the matter any detailed consideration or scrutiny, nor to examine closely the similarities and differences between the marks." — *Veuve Clicquot*, 2006 SCC 23 at para 20.

> "[R]esemblance is defined as the quality of being either like or similar... The s. 6(5)(e) factor, the degree of resemblance between the marks, will often have the greatest effect on the confusion analysis." — *Masterpiece*, 2011 SCC 27 at paras 49–50.

## Common-law / passing-off considerations

### United States (Lanham Act §43(a) + state law)

Common-law rights flow from use in commerce in a geographic area. A junior federal applicant can be defeated by a senior common-law user under §2(d). Canada has parallel doctrine under the Trade-marks Act s. 7(b).

### Canada (s. 7(b))

Three elements (*Ciba-Geigy Canada Ltd v Apotex Inc*, [1992] 3 SCR 120):
1. Goodwill in the mark.
2. Misrepresentation by the junior user causing confusion.
3. Damage (actual or potential).

A common-law user with reputation in a Canadian region can sustain a passing-off claim even without registration.

## How to write the analysis paragraphs

For each top-3 conflict, write 2-4 sentences applying the above. Pattern:

> "[Senior mark] is registered/pending for [goods] in Class [N]. The marks share [identical/strong/moderate/weak] resemblance in [sight/sound/meaning]; goods are [identical/related/unrelated]; trade channels [overlap/diverge]. Senior mark appears [strong/moderate/weak] based on [length of use / fame / crowded field]. Under DuPont, this is a [🟢/🟡/🔴] hit. Under Masterpiece (if applicable), the s. 6(5)(e) resemblance factor [governs/cuts in junior's favor] and the overall risk is [🟢/🟡/🔴]."

Keep the analysis tight — the memo is a knockout, not a registrability opinion.
