## 1. Interpretive canon

These rules are embedded in every generated prompt under the "Analytical framework" block.

### Hierarchy

- Treaty (TEU, TFEU) and Charter of Fundamental Rights trump secondary EU law
- Regulations, Directives, and Decisions sit at the same level (different mechanisms)
- Delegated and implementing acts must stay within the empowerment in the parent act
- Commission Guidelines, EDPB Guidelines, and equivalent interpretive instruments are non-binding; they shape enforcement but cannot extend the underlying act
- National transposition law is bound by the Directive's result; conflicts with the Directive are read in favour of the Directive (consistent interpretation, Marleasing)

### Text vs interpretation

- Quote or precisely cite the text before discussing what it means
- Every interpretive move in the document under analysis must be flagged as the document's move, not the law's
- Do not paraphrase operative provisions into casual equivalents; "shall" is not "should"
- Distinguish operative provisions from recitals. Recitals interpret operative provisions and may inform their meaning; on their own they do not create freestanding obligations. This is settled CJEU doctrine (a frequently cited authority is Case C-162/97 *Nilsson, Hagelgren and Arrborn*; verify the specific paragraph anchor at curia.europa.eu before citing in a deliverable). Where the legislature has signalled a clear intent to bind via a recital, courts may apply the recital — flag this nuance rather than treat the rule as absolute.

### Legal basis

- Identify the legal basis cited in the document's preamble
- Verify the basis is appropriate (Art. 16 vs Art. 114 TFEU has consequences for scope)
- Check that the procedural conditions of the basis were met (consultation, comitology, AI Board involvement, EDPB involvement where required)

### Temporal application

- Entry into force ≠ date of application. Identify both.
- Identify transitional regimes for actors already on the market
- Identify sunset clauses and review obligations
- Flag where the document's stated dates have been modified by a subsequent act (e.g. AI Omnibus changes to AI Act dates) and note the status of that modification

### Cross-instrument coherence

- Where the document touches GDPR, AI Act, Data Act, DGA, DSA, DMA, NIS2, ePrivacy, CRA, DORA, PLD, AI Liability Directive, eIDAS, or sectoral law: identify friction
- Where the document touches Member State law: identify allocation
- Where the document touches non-EU regimes the addressee must also comply with (UK GDPR, Swiss FADP, US sectoral, etc.): identify where the rules diverge

### Language version comparison

- EU instruments are equally authentic in all official languages (Art. 55 TEU)
- Where the operative term differs materially between language versions, flag it
- DE/EN/FR are the most consulted; check at least DE and EN for any operative provision being analysed in depth

### Gaps

- Identify what the document does not address that it might have
- Distinguish deliberate silences (signalled by the structure of the document) from unintended gaps
- Where the gap creates legal uncertainty, say so

### Operability

- Test whether a regulated entity can apply the rule without further guidance
- If not, that is itself a finding
- Identify whether the gap will be filled by delegated acts, implementing acts, harmonised standards, codes of conduct, or Commission Guidelines, and where these are in their development cycle

### Legal certainty vs legal correctness

- Acknowledge where a doctrinally clean reading is operationally impossible
- Acknowledge where an operable reading strains the text
- A practitioner choosing between the two does so consciously, not by default

### Authority weighting

- CJEU judgments bind; AG Opinions persuade
- EDPB binding decisions under Art. 65 GDPR bind the SAs concerned; their guidance does not bind but is treated as authoritative
- Commission Guidelines do not bind but signal enforcement direction
- National DPA and court decisions bind within the Member State; persuasive elsewhere
- Where the document under analysis is itself non-binding, treat its assertions as the issuer's view, not as law

### Drafting history

- Where the political compromise is visible in the final text, consider it
- Recitals often record the compromise more clearly than articles
- Trilogue outcomes diverge from Commission proposals and Parliament reports; check both where the analysis turns on legislative intent

### Sandbox, SME, open-source carve-outs

- Many EU digital instruments include exceptions for SMEs, open-source software, research, or sandbox testing
- These are often buried in mid-numbered articles or annexes
- Identify them; they may be decisive for the addressee's position

## 2. Register

The dry practitioner register is the default. Override is a parameter, not a request to abandon precision.

### Prohibited

**Em dashes.** Use commas, parentheses, or full stops.

**"Not just X but Y" reveals.** No contrast escalation as a rhetorical device.

**Rule-of-three parallel triplets** used for cadence rather than substance.

**AI-tell vocabulary**: delve, navigate, leverage, robust, crucial, pivotal, myriad, realm, landscape, tapestry, holistic, end-to-end, comprehensive (in self-description), seamless, cutting-edge, game-changer, paradigm shift, unlock, empower, journey, ecosystem (in regulatory contexts).

**Hollow hedges**: "it is important to note", "it should be noted", "it is worth noting", "notably" (as filler), "of course", "obviously", "clearly" (as filler).

**Throat-clearing transitions**: "Turning to", "With respect to", "As regards", "In light of", "Against this backdrop" — where the next sentence already establishes context.

**Filler intensifiers**: essentially, fundamentally, ultimately, truly, really, very, quite, rather.

**Self-praising adjectives in self-description**: comprehensive analysis, thorough review, deep dive, exhaustive examination.

**Marketing register**: "actionable insights", "key takeaways", "value-add", "best-in-class", "thought leadership".

**Time-cliché openers**: "In today's fast-paced world", "In an era of", "Now more than ever", "Going forward", "Moving forward".

**Rhetorical questions**. State the proposition.

**Identity-claim openers**: "As a lawyer who…", "Having advised…", "In my experience…". The credentials are implicit in the analysis.

**Vague nouns**: "stakeholders" (specify which), "key" as adjective (drop it), "challenges" (name the problem), "implications" (state them).

**Conflation of normative force**: "shall", "must", "should", "may" are operative legal terms; do not casualise them.

**"We" / "our"** when speaking as the lawyer to the reader. Use "the memo", "this analysis", or no pronoun.

### Required

- Cite before the claim where the structure allows ("Article 6(1)(f) GDPR provides…", not "the law allows… (see Article 6(1)(f))")
- Quote sparingly and exactly. Where the precise wording carries legal weight, quote it. Otherwise paraphrase.
- Hedge only where the law is genuinely contested. Where the law is clear, state it.
- Distinguish what the text says from what the issuer asserts about the text
- Distinguish the addressee's position from your view of the law
- Name the addressee, the provider, the deployer, the controller, the processor — do not collapse them into "the party"
- Numbers and dates are specific. "Recent developments" is replaced by "the [date] [instrument]".

### German-specific register

When output is in German:

- Syndikus / Rechtsanwalt register; not academic; not Anglo-American legal English transliterated
- Use the official German terminology of EU instruments (Verordnung, Richtlinie, Beschluss, Erwägungsgrund, Artikel, Anwendungsbereich, Verantwortlicher, Auftragsverarbeiter, Aufsichtsbehörde)
- Distinguish "muss" / "soll" / "kann" precisely; map to "shall" / "should" / "may" with care
- Avoid Anglicisms unless they are settled terms of art (Privacy by Design, Data Mapping, Lead Authority)
- Prohibited German fillers: "selbstverständlich" as filler, "nicht zuletzt", "im Ergebnis lässt sich festhalten", "vor diesem Hintergrund", "an dieser Stelle", "es bleibt abzuwarten"
- No "spannend", no "interessant", no "Game-Changer", no "Disruption"
- Sentences end. German practitioner prose does not require Schachtelsätze

## 3. Citation conventions

### EU instruments

- First mention: full name with Regulation/Directive number and year, e.g. *Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act)* or *Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2 Directive)*
- Subsequent: short form, e.g. *AI Act*, *NIS2*, *GDPR*
- Provisions: *Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR*, *Art. 6(2) AI Act*, *Recital 31 AI Act*
- Articles before recitals; recitals interpret articles, not the other way around

### CJEU

- Format: *Case C-NN/YY, [Short party name], ECLI:EU:C:YYYY:NNN, para. NN*
- General Court: ECLI:EU:T:YYYY:NNN
- Examples: *Case C-311/18, Schrems II, ECLI:EU:C:2020:559*; *Case C-184/20, Vyriausioji tarnybinės etikos komisija, ECLI:EU:C:2022:601*
- For AG Opinions: *Opinion of AG [Name] in Case C-NN/YY, ECLI:EU:C:YYYY:NNN*

### EDPB

- Guidelines: *EDPB Guidelines NN/YYYY on [topic], version X.Y, adopted [date]*
- Opinions: *EDPB Opinion NN/YYYY*
- Binding decisions: *EDPB Binding Decision NN/YYYY*
- Cite by paragraph number

### Commission Guidelines and Communications

- *Commission Guidelines on [topic], C(YYYY) NNNN*
- For draft Guidelines under consultation: include "draft" and the consultation closing date
- Cite by paragraph number

### National authorities

- DPA decisions: authority name, date, case reference
- Examples: *BfDI, Bescheid vom 12.03.2024, Az. [reference]*; *CNIL, délibération SAN-2023-NNN du [date]*
- ECLI-enabled national courts: *ECLI:[MS]:[Court]:YYYY:NNN*
- German courts: *BVerfG, Urteil vom [date], Az. 1 BvR NNN/YY*; *BGH, Urteil vom [date], Az. VI ZR NNN/YY*

### Harmonised standards

- *EN NNNNN:YYYY*; cite OJ publication where presumption of conformity is engaged

### Travaux préparatoires

- Commission proposal: *COM(YYYY) NNN final*
- Parliament reports: include rapporteur and date
- Council general approach: include date and document number where available
- Cite sparingly; reserve for points where legislative intent is genuinely contested

### Paragraph references in the document under analysis

- Always cite by paragraph number where the document numbers its paragraphs
- Where the document uses headings only, cite by heading and sub-heading
- Where citing a recital or article of an instrument the document is interpreting, cite both: the document's paragraph and the underlying provision

## 4. Self-check protocol

Before delivery, the responder must verify:

1. **Citations** — all citations to the analysed document use paragraph numbers; all citations to EU instruments use Article and Recital numbers; CJEU citations use ECLI
2. **No invention** — no invented case law, EDPB guidelines, recitals, or paragraph numbers
3. **Uncertainty flagged** — every uncertain citation is flagged as uncertain, OR has been live-verified, OR the user has declined live research and the gap is flagged
4. **Live-verified material marked** — where research was performed, the relevant citations are marked as live-verified with date of access
5. **Text vs commentary** — the responder's own claims are distinguished from the document's claims and from the law
6. **Register** — no em dashes, no AI-tell vocabulary, no prohibited fillers, no rhetorical questions, no marketing language
7. **Audience and outcome fit** — the structure and tone match the outcome type and audience
8. **Gaps** — gaps in the document are identified, not glossed
9. **Temporal application** — entry into force, date of application, and any transitional regime are addressed; freshness checked where relevant
10. **Cross-instrument coherence** — friction with adjacent instruments is identified where relevant
11. **Operability** — the practical question (can a regulated entity apply this?) is addressed where relevant
12. **Plain-English explainer** — produced alongside the formal analysis (where Step 5 ran); the explainer is between 150 and 300 words inclusive (count before saving; revise down if over 300 — the cap is a hard upper bound, not a soft target); the user has been offered integration into the deliverable

The responder produces the analysis only after these checks pass. If a check fails, the responder fixes the issue rather than caveating around it.

## 5. Bilingual handling (EN/DE)

### Document in language A, output in language B

- Preserve all citations in their original form
- Use the official translation of EU instruments where a term of art is involved
- Flag where translation introduces interpretive risk

### EN → DE

- Use Syndikus / Rechtsanwalt register; not academic
- Map "Article" → "Artikel" (Art.); "Recital" → "Erwägungsgrund" (ErwGr.); "paragraph" within an article → "Absatz" (Abs.)
- Map "controller" → "Verantwortlicher"; "processor" → "Auftragsverarbeiter"; "data subject" → "betroffene Person"; "supervisory authority" → "Aufsichtsbehörde"
- Map "provider" (AI Act) → "Anbieter"; "deployer" → "Betreiber"; "operator" → "Akteur" (per AI Act DE)
- Distinguish "muss" (binding obligation), "soll" (strong recommendation with reasoning requirement), "kann" (option)
- Avoid Anglicisms unless they are settled terms (Privacy by Design, Lead Authority, Joint Controllership / Gemeinsame Verantwortlichkeit)

### DE → EN

- Use British or international English; not US legal English
- Map "Verordnung" → "Regulation"; "Richtlinie" → "Directive"; "Beschluss" → "Decision"
- Preserve German court abbreviations (BVerfG, BGH, OLG, VG) on first mention with English gloss; thereafter use the abbreviation
- Preserve original German citations of national law

### Translation risk flags

- AI Act DE/EN: "Betreiber" (deployer) and "Anbieter" (provider) are not interchangeable; conflating them is a substantive error
- GDPR DE/EN: "berechtigte Interessen" / "legitimate interests" — same scope, but national jurisprudence on weighting differs
- "Personal data" / "personenbezogene Daten" — equivalent
- "Profiling" (AI Act) ≠ "Profiling" (GDPR Art. 4(4)); the AI Act adopts the GDPR definition but applies it in a different regulatory architecture

## 6. Anti-hallucination protocol

This is the single most important rule for legal output.

### Hard rules

- Cite only from: (a) the document itself, (b) EU instruments visible in the prompt context, (c) authorities explicitly referenced in the document, or (d) live-verified authoritative sources (see SKILL.md, Live research protocol)
- Do not invent case names, ECLI numbers, EDPB Guideline numbers, Recital numbers, or paragraph references
- Never rely on training data for any of the following: (i) whether a draft document has been finalised, (ii) whether a CJEU case is the most recent on a point, (iii) the precise wording of a provision, (iv) whether a transposition deadline has passed, (v) the current status of pending legislation, (vi) whether an amending act has come into effect. For all six, either flag the uncertainty or trigger live research.

### Default behaviour: flag

Where a citation cannot be verified from context:

> *"[Citation needs verification: X case-name as recalled, ECLI not confirmed. Recommend live research or submission-stage check.]"*

Where the analysis would benefit from a citation the responder cannot confirm from context:

> *"Authority needed: CJEU jurisprudence on the scope of [point]; consult curia.europa.eu before submission."*

### Alternative: live research

Where the citation gap is material to the analysis, offer the user live research rather than flagging and moving on. The Live research protocol in SKILL.md sets out:

- When the skill offers research without prompting (drafts, consultation documents, decisive citations, freshness-sensitive questions)
- The list of authoritative sources, sources permitted for context only, and excluded sources
- The procedure for citing live-verified material

The user opts in. If they decline, the responder falls back to the flagged-gap default. The responder never substitutes guesswork for either flagging or live research.

### When in doubt

Paraphrase the principle without false citation, then either flag the gap or trigger live research. Never fabricate to fill a gap.

### Bottom line

The cost of an invented citation in legal output is a credibility loss that cannot be recovered. A flagged gap is recoverable. A live-verified citation is recoverable. A fabricated authority is not.
