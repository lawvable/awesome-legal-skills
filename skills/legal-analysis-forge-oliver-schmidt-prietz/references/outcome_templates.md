# Outcome Templates

Reference for Step 4 (Generate prompt). For each outcome type, provides the role markers, output structure, length target, and register adjustments to embed in the generated prompt.

## A note on length targets

The word counts in each template are **default indications only**. The appropriate length for any analysis depends on the length and density of the source document, the depth of the task, the audience, and the user's stated requirements. A 12-page Commission Guideline and a 200-page Regulation will not produce analyses of the same length under the same template. Treat the numbers as a starting point, not as a constraint, and adjust at Step 3 where the user signals a different scope.

## 1. Stakeholder consultation response

**Use when**: the user is preparing a formal submission to the Commission, EDPB, AI Office, or a national authority in response to a draft instrument or call for evidence.

**Role markers**:
> Senior EU regulatory practitioner with 15+ years' experience in EU tech regulation. Has drafted consultation responses on GDPR, AI Act, Data Act, NIS2, and adjacent instruments. Has appeared before AI Board and EDPB working groups. Distinguishes between what the text says and what the issuer is asserting via guidance.

**Output structure**:

1. Executive summary (max. 200 words; five most consequential findings)
2. Strengths (where the draft adds clarity or resolves ambiguity)
3. Concerns, by paragraph (a) what the draft says (b) the legal or practical problem (c) authority for the critique (d) suggested redraft
4. Gaps (issues the draft should address but does not)
5. Strategic recommendations (3–5 concrete asks for the consultation response, prioritised)

**Length target**: 2,500–4,000 words

**Register notes**: formal, deferential to procedural channel without being deferential to substance. Cite the draft by paragraph number throughout. Cite the underlying act by Article and Recital.

## 2. Internal compliance memo

**Use when**: the user is briefing an in-house team, DPO function, or compliance committee on what the document changes for the organisation.

**Role markers**:
> Senior in-house counsel with operational compliance experience across GDPR, AI Act, and adjacent EU digital instruments. Translates regulatory text into actionable obligations for product, engineering, legal, and procurement teams. Distinguishes legal certainty from legal correctness.

**Output structure**:

1. Bottom line (max. 100 words; what changes for the organisation)
2. Document at a glance (instrument type, status, application date, addressees)
3. What the document says (key provisions in plain language, with citations)
4. What it changes for us (concrete obligations, organised by affected function)
5. Open questions (where the answer depends on facts not yet known)
6. Recommended next steps (with owners and indicative timing)

**Length target**: 1,500–2,500 words

**Register notes**: practical, action-oriented, no hedging where the law is clear. Where uncertainty exists, name it precisely. Use the organisation's own product and function names where known.

## 3. External client memo

**Use when**: the user is briefing an external client on what the document means for them.

**Role markers**:
> Senior external counsel with EU digital regulation practice. Advises providers and deployers across the AI Act, GDPR, Data Act, DSA, and adjacent instruments. Communicates legal risk in business terms without losing legal precision.

**Output structure**:

1. Summary (max. 150 words)
2. Background (the document in context)
3. Analysis (the legal substance with citations)
4. Implications for the client (concrete, prioritised)
5. Recommendations (specific, with timing)
6. Caveats (limitations, assumptions, jurisdictional limits, no-advice clauses where appropriate)

**Length target**: 1,500–3,000 words

**Register notes**: precise, qualified where necessary, decisive where the law allows. The client wants a recommendation, not a survey of views. Cite for credibility; do not pad with citations.

## 4. Public commentary (LinkedIn post / blog post / newsletter entry)

**Use when**: the user is producing public commentary on a regulatory development for a professional audience — LinkedIn post, blog post, newsletter entry, or short opinion piece.

**Role markers**:
> Senior EU digital regulation practitioner publishing for a legal and compliance audience. Writes in a dry practitioner register suitable for client memos. No marketing or thought-leadership tone.

**Output structure**:

1. Lead claim (one sentence, identifies the document and the main point)
2. Three to five paragraphs of substantive analysis, each with a clear claim and citation
3. Closing observation or open question
4. Source list (location depends on platform: first comment for LinkedIn, footnotes for a blog, end-matter for a newsletter)

**Length target (default; adjust to platform and depth)**: 250–600 words for a short post; 800–1,500 words for a longer blog post or newsletter entry.

**Register notes**:
- No em dashes
- No "not just X but Y" reveals
- No rule-of-three escalations
- No identity-claim openers ("As a lawyer who…")
- For DE output: Syndikus / Rechtsanwalt register; avoid Anglicisms unless they are terms of art (e.g. "Privacy by Design")
- For EN output: British or international register; not US legal English
- Source links placed where the platform supports them inline; otherwise listed at the end or in a follow-up comment

## 5. Conference talk preparation

**Use when**: the user is preparing speaking notes for a panel, keynote, or workshop.

**Role markers**:
> Senior practitioner preparing for a 20–45 minute talk to a legal or compliance audience. Has presented at diruj, IAPP, Privacy Symposium, and equivalent venues. Distinguishes what the audience knows from what the talk should add.

**Output structure**:

1. One-line thesis
2. Three to five main points, each with: the claim, the citation, one concrete example, one anticipated objection and rebuttal
3. Slide outline (one slide per main point plus opener, closer, sources)
4. Anticipated Q&A: 5–10 likely questions with prepared answers

**Length target**: 2,000–3,500 words

**Register notes**: speakable. Sentences a person can actually say aloud. No bullet salad. Numbers and statutes called out clearly.

## 6. Internal risk assessment

**Use when**: the user is briefing management, board, or risk function on regulatory risk arising from the document.

**Role markers**:
> Senior risk and compliance practitioner. Quantifies regulatory risk where possible. Distinguishes likelihood from impact. Identifies controls and residual risk.

**Output structure**:

1. Risk summary (one paragraph)
2. Risk register entries (one per discrete risk): risk description, source provision, likelihood (with reasoning), impact (with reasoning), current controls, gap, residual risk, recommended mitigation, owner
3. Aggregate risk view
4. Recommended escalation (if any)

**Length target**: 1,500–3,000 words

**Register notes**: structured, quantified where defensible, hedged where not. Avoid risk-management buzzwords (do not use "holistic", "end-to-end", "robust framework").

## 7. Litigation brief input

**Use when**: the user is preparing material for ongoing or anticipated litigation, including regulatory enforcement proceedings.

**Role markers**:
> Senior litigation counsel with EU digital regulation experience. Builds arguments from primary text, recitals, travaux préparatoires, and case law. Anticipates counter-arguments. Distinguishes the strongest argument from the most defensible one.

**Output structure**:

1. Issue (one sentence, the precise legal question)
2. Short answer (one paragraph)
3. Argument (built from primary text first, then recitals, then case law, then guidance; identify the strongest line of attack and the strongest counter)
4. Counter-arguments and rebuttals
5. Authority table (sorted by weight: CJEU, AG, national supreme courts, lower courts, EDPB binding, EDPB non-binding, Commission guidelines)
6. Risk assessment of the position

**Length target**: 3,000–6,000 words

**Register notes**: adversarial but accurate. The reader is a partner or barrister who will test every claim. No marketing language. No rhetorical questions. No flourishes.

## 8. Comparative analysis

**Use when**: the user wants to compare the new document with a prior instrument, a sister instrument, or non-EU equivalent.

**Role markers**:
> Senior comparative practitioner with cross-instrument expertise across EU digital regulation and, where relevant, US, UK, Canadian, and other comparators. Identifies functional equivalents and material divergences.

**Output structure**:

1. Comparator identification (what is being compared with what, and why)
2. Comparison matrix (rows = legal issues, columns = instruments; cells cite source provision and capture the rule)
3. Material divergences and convergences (prose analysis of the matrix)
4. Practical implications for actors subject to multiple regimes
5. Drafting or strategic recommendations arising from the comparison

**Length target**: 2,500–5,000 words

**Register notes**: precise about what is being compared. Equivalent provisions can be functionally different; flag this. Avoid false equivalences.

---

## 9. Horizon-scan entry

**Use when**: the user is updating an ongoing monitoring deliverable (own horizon scan, client horizon scan, internal newsletter).

**Role markers**:
> Senior practitioner maintaining a regulatory monitoring brief for a defined client or internal audience. Distils each new development into a compact, actionable entry.

**Output structure**:

1. Headline (one line)
2. What happened (2–3 sentences, with citation)
3. Why it matters (2–3 sentences; who is affected, what changes)
4. What to watch (2–3 sentences; next milestones, related developments)
5. Source (with link)

**Length target**: 150–300 words

**Register notes**: dense, declarative, no padding. No "stay tuned" closers. No "what does this mean for you" rhetorical hooks.

---

## 10. Skill input

**Use when**: the user is encoding the document into an existing or new compliance skill (LIA, TIA, ROPA, AI Act, NIS2, Data Act, etc.) for future reuse.

**Role markers**:
> Senior practitioner encoding regulatory expertise into a Claude Code skill. Distinguishes operational rules (executable logic) from background context (loaded as needed). Produces concise, citation-anchored extracts suitable for skill reference files.

**Output structure**:

1. Source identification (full citation, date accessed)
2. Operational rules extracted (each as a discrete, citation-anchored rule)
3. Background context (what the skill needs to know but does not execute on)
4. Cross-references (which other skill modules this affects)
5. Suggested skill location (which file in the target skill the content belongs in)

**Length target**: variable; depends on target skill structure

**Register notes**: extracted text must be reusable as-is. Every rule citation-anchored. No marketing register. No interpretive flourishes — interpretation lives in the skill's body, not in the reference extract.
