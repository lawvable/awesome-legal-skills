# Reg. 64 Kosovo — Jurisprudence map

This document maps the institutional periods, substantive coverage, and case typology of the Regulation 64 Panels under UNMIK and the EULEX successor framework. It is intended as an analytical orientation tool, **not** as an authoritative case index. Always verify specific case references against authoritative archives (`references/authoritative-sources.md`).

## Institutional periods

### Phase 0 — Pre-UNMIK collapse (June 1999 – early 2000)

- UNSC Resolution 1244 adopted 10 June 1999; UNMIK established
- Kosovo judiciary largely collapsed: most Kosovo Serb judges and prosecutors departed; Kosovo Albanian judiciary effectively suspended under prior FRY administration
- UNMIK Reg. 1999/1 of 25 July 1999 vested plenary authority in SRSG; preserved law in force on 22 March 1989 (i.e., pre-revocation of Kosovo's autonomy) as the applicable law, subject to UNMIK regulations
- Limited national-level criminal justice activity; serious bias concerns in early prosecutions of Serbs by reconstituted Kosovo Albanian judiciary

### Phase 1 — Ad hoc international judges (February – December 2000)

- **Mitrovica February 2000 inter-ethnic violence** — catalytic event
- UNMIK appointed **one international judge and one international prosecutor per district court** on ad hoc SRSG authority
- **UNMIK Reg. 2000/6 of 15 February 2000** formalised the appointment and removal framework
- International judges sat alongside Kosovo Albanian judges on ordinary panels — **minority** position; could be outvoted
- Limited effectiveness: international voices outvoted by Kosovo Albanian judges in ethnically-charged cases
- Michael E. Hartmann appointed first international prosecutor (February 2000 – early 2003); his reports document the institutional gap that Reg. 64 was designed to close

### Phase 2 — Reg. 64 Panels operational (December 2000 – February 2008)

- **UNMIK Reg. 2000/64 of 15 December 2000** — operative instrument
- Initial 12-month duration; subsequently extended by amendment
- Case-by-case designation: prosecutor / defence / DJA own-motion petition → DJA recommendation → SRSG approval → DJA designation
- **Panel composition: 3 professional judges, minimum 2 international (i.e., majority international)** — replaces ordinary 5-judge mixed professional/lay panel
- UNMIK Reg. 2001/2 expanded international prosecutors' authority to **resurrect abandoned cases** (addressing ethnic bias in selective prosecution)
- KWECC (Kosovo War and Ethnic Crimes Court) **abandoned in autumn 2000** in favour of Reg. 64 mechanism — institutional choice for **integration over separation**
- 6 July 2003 — Provisional Criminal Code and Provisional CPC adopted (UNMIK Regs 2003/25 and 2003/26), replacing Yugoslav Federal Criminal Code 1976; entered into force 6 April 2004
- 15 May 2001 — Constitutional Framework for Provisional Self-Government in Kosovo (UNMIK Reg. 2001/9) created transitional institutions but **preserved UNMIK's reserved powers**, including international judiciary

### Phase 3 — UNMIK-to-EULEX transition (December 2008 – ongoing)

- **17 February 2008** — Kosovo Assembly declared independence
- **15 June 2008** — Constitution of the Republic of Kosovo entered into force
- **9 December 2008** — EULEX achieved full operational capability under its Executive Mandate (Joint Action 2008/124/CFSP of 4 February 2008)
- **Law No. 2008/03-L053 of 13 March 2008** — Republic of Kosovo statute on the jurisdiction, case selection, and case allocation of EULEX judges and prosecutors — the post-independence successor framework
- EULEX judges continued to sit on panels in Kosovo courts under modified arrangements; gradual transfer to fully national judiciary culminated in EULEX's transition to a "Strengthening Mission" with monitoring/mentoring focus (post-2018) and eventually phase-out

## Substantive coverage by Reg. 64 Panels

### War crimes (1998-1999 conflict)

- War crimes committed during the 1998-1999 Kosovo war by:
  - Federal Republic of Yugoslavia / Serbian forces (armed forces, MUP, paramilitaries)
  - Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) — though structural limitations and witness intimidation severely constrained this category
- Categorical legal basis: war crimes provisions of the Yugoslav Federal Criminal Code 1976 (Chapter XVI — Criminal Acts Against Humanity and International Law), then from 6 April 2004 the Provisional Criminal Code of Kosovo
- **Important methodological note:** witness intimidation and difficulty obtaining cooperation in cases against KLA-affiliated defendants is widely documented in the academic and NGO literature (USIP, OSCE, ICTJ, Human Rights Watch reports). This structural limitation underlies the eventual creation of the **Kosovo Specialist Chambers (KSC) in 2015** as a separate, relocated tribunal addressing KLA crimes — institutionally distinct from Reg. 64 Panels and **NOT covered by this skill**

### Inter-ethnic crimes (post-war, primarily 1999-2004)

- Crimes committed in the immediate post-war period against Kosovo Serbs, Roma, Ashkali, Egyptians, Goranis, and other minorities
- **March 2004 inter-ethnic riots** — particularly significant: dozens of Serbian Orthodox churches and properties destroyed, ~30 deaths, thousands displaced; significant subsequent prosecution effort under Reg. 64 designation
- Pre-Provisional Criminal Code applications relied on Yugoslav Federal Criminal Code provisions

### Organised crime and corruption (selectively)

- Although primarily designed for ethnic crimes, the Reg. 64 mechanism was also used in serious organised crime and corruption cases where impartiality concerns arose
- Less central to the institutional rationale but documented in UNMIK and OSCE reports

### Terrorism cases

- Limited but present in the case record; particularly in border/frontier-zone matters

## Case typology — analytical categories

- **Resurrection cases** — abandoned by Kosovan prosecutors, revived under UNMIK Reg. 2001/2 by international prosecutors
- **Venue-change cases** — moved between districts under Reg. 2000/64 to address witness intimidation or impartiality concerns
- **Mixed panels with national appellate review** — first-instance Reg. 64 Panel with appeal to Supreme Court of Kosovo (which itself could be Reg. 64-designated)
- **Retrial cases** — particularly after ECHR review or following procedural challenges
- **Joint indictments** — multiple defendants from different ethnic communities

## Substantive doctrinal contributions

The Reg. 64 Panels' doctrinal contributions are dispersed across individual decisions rather than systematized, but the following themes recur in academic literature (Cerone, Reidy, Hartmann, Strohmeyer, Cohen, OSCE Mission in Kosovo, EULEX legal officers' reflections):

- **Applicable law in post-conflict transition** — the interaction of pre-1989 Yugoslav law, UNMIK regulations, and (from 2004) the Provisional Criminal Code; doctrines of continuity and non-retroactivity (relevant Article 7 ECHR concerns parallel those that arose in WCC-BiH — see `wcc-bih` skill)
- **Command responsibility under domestic law informed by international standards** — selective importation of ICTY jurisprudence into national-level proceedings
- **Procedural protections in mixed-language proceedings** — Albanian, Serbian, English; interpretation standards
- **Witness protection and security in territories with persistent inter-ethnic tension** — institutional limits revealed
- **The integration model itself** — Reg. 64 as a case study in "graduated hybridity" (see Romano/Nouwen/Stahn taxonomies) showing the possibilities and limits of judicial functions embedded within ordinary national courts rather than separately established

## Comparative positioning (analytical, not jurisdictional)

- **Compared to East Timor Special Panels (SPSC)** — both operated under UN transitional administration; SPSC was a separate panel structure within Dili District Court, Reg. 64 was a panel designation within existing district courts; both faced structural impunity problems for senior commanders
- **Compared to WCC-BiH** — WCC-BiH became fully national by 2009-2012; Reg. 64 transitioned to EULEX rather than full nationalisation in the same period
- **Compared to KSC (2015-)** — KSC is fundamentally different: separate relocated tribunal at The Hague, addressing a specific subset of KLA-related allegations; **NOT covered by this skill**
- **Compared to ICTY/IRMCT residual functions** — ICTY addressed the wider Yugoslav conflict; Reg. 64 addressed Kosovo-specific national-level cases; in occasional overlap, ICTY case-law informed Reg. 64 decisions but the proceedings were institutionally separate

## Archival status — practical implications

The archival state of Reg. 64 Panel jurisprudence is **fragmented**:

- **UNMIK legacy archive** (unmik.unmissions.org) — primary repository for regulations and SRSG-level documents; less comprehensive for individual case files
- **EULEX archive** — partial coverage of 2008+ continuation cases
- **Legal Tools Database (legal-tools.org)** — selective coverage
- **OSCE Mission in Kosovo reports** — extensive trial monitoring reports during the operational period
- **USIP / academic monographs** — case-by-case analyses (Hartmann, Cerone, Reidy, Cohen)
- **HRW, Amnesty, ICTJ** — selected high-profile cases

**Practical implication:** when verifying a specific Reg. 64 case, expect to consult **multiple sources** to confirm a citation; do not expect a single authoritative case index comparable to ICC's CourtRecords or ICTY's Records Database. Flag verification level (existence / content / paragraph) explicitly in output.
