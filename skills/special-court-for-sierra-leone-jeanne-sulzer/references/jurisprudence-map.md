# Jurisprudence map — SCSL/RSCSL

A topic-by-topic map of the SCSL's principal landmark holdings.

## 1. Head of State immunity (landmark)

**Decision on Immunity from Jurisdiction**, *Prosecutor v. Charles Ghankay Taylor*, Case No. SCSL-03-01-I, Appeals Chamber, **31 May 2004**.

The Appeals Chamber rejected Taylor's challenge to the SCSL's jurisdiction — he had argued he enjoyed sovereign immunity as a sitting head of State of Liberia (the status he held when the indictment was confirmed on 7 March 2003; by the date of this decision, 31 May 2004, he had already left office, having gone into exile in August 2003). The Chamber relied on:
- SCSL Statute Art. 6(2) (no immunity for heads of State, modelled on ICTY Art. 7(2), ICTR Art. 6(2), and Rome Statute Art. 27)
- The character of the SCSL as an "international tribunal" acting on behalf of the international community (a contested characterisation given the SCSL is treaty-based, not Chapter VII)
- Customary international law authorising the prosecution of heads of State for international crimes

This decision is among the most-cited SCSL holdings and forms part of the doctrinal foundation of head-of-state non-immunity, alongside Pinochet (UK Lords 1999), Yerodia (ICJ 2002), and subsequent ICC decisions.

## 2. Conscripting / enlisting children under 15 as a war crime (landmark)

**Statutory basis:** SCSL Statute Art. 4(c) — "Conscripting or enlisting children under the age of 15 years into armed forces or groups or using them to participate actively in hostilities."

**Principal holdings:**
- *Prosecutor v. Brima, Kamara and Kanu* (**AFRC**), Trial Judgement of 20 June 2007 and Appeal Judgement of 22 February 2008
- *Prosecutor v. Fofana and Kondewa* (**CDF**), Trial Judgement of 2 August 2007 and Appeal Judgement of 28 May 2008
- *Prosecutor v. Sesay, Kallon and Gbao* (**RUF**), Trial Judgement of 2 March 2009 and Appeal Judgement of 26 October 2009
- *Prosecutor v. Taylor*, Trial Judgement of 18 May 2012 and Appeal Judgement of 26 September 2013

The SCSL is the **modern foundation** of child-soldier prosecution under international criminal law. The Chamber's findings on the elements of "conscripting", "enlisting", "using to participate actively in hostilities", and the age threshold are influential and have been cited in subsequent international and national prosecutions (including the ICC's *Lubanga* Trial Judgment of 14 March 2012).

A preliminary motion in the *Norman* case (subsequently *Fofana and Kondewa*) — **Decision on Preliminary Motion Based on Lack of Jurisdiction (Child Recruitment)**, *Prosecutor v. Norman*, Case No. SCSL-04-14-AR72(E), Appeals Chamber, **31 May 2004** — held that the recruitment of children under 15 was a crime under customary international law by November 1996 (the start of the SCSL's temporal jurisdiction). A foundational decision.

## 3. Forced marriage as a crime against humanity (landmark)

**AFRC Appeal Judgement**, *Prosecutor v. Brima, Kamara and Kanu*, Case No. SCSL-04-16-A, Appeals Chamber, **22 February 2008**, paras 175-203.

The **first international tribunal decision to recognise forced marriage as a distinct "other inhumane act"** under Statute Art. 2(i) (crimes against humanity). The Trial Chamber had subsumed forced marriage into sexual slavery; the Appeals Chamber held that forced marriage involves distinct conduct — including the imposition of a conjugal relationship with attendant duties (forced labour, forced childbearing, social stigma) — that is not exhausted by sexual slavery.

**Subsequently affirmed and developed in:**
- *RUF Trial Judgement* (2 March 2009)
- *Taylor Trial Judgement* (18 May 2012)
- Influential globally — applied at the **ECCC Case 002/02** Trial Judgement (16 November 2018, forced marriage during Democratic Kampuchea), the **ICC** (*Ongwen* Trial Judgment of 4 February 2021, the first ICC conviction for forced marriage), and the **KSC** (developing).

## 4. Sexual slavery and sexual violence

Major holdings across the AFRC, CDF, RUF and Taylor cases. The SCSL applied:
- Statute Art. 2(g) — rape and sexual slavery as crimes against humanity
- Statute Art. 3(e) — outrages upon personal dignity (including rape and indecent assault) as war crimes
- Specific elements of sexual slavery: the perpetrator exercised any or all of the powers attaching to the right of ownership; the perpetrator caused the victim(s) to engage in sexual acts.

The **RUF Trial Judgement** (2 March 2009) and the **AFRC Trial Judgement** (20 June 2007) are particularly significant for elaborating the elements of sexual slavery in armed conflict.

## 5. Aiding and abetting and the "substantial effect" standard

**Taylor Appeal Judgement**, *Prosecutor v. Taylor*, Case No. SCSL-03-01-A, Appeals Chamber, **26 September 2013**.

Taylor was convicted of **planning** and **aiding and abetting** the crimes committed by the RUF and AFRC in Sierra Leone. The Trial Chamber had previously held (and the Appeals Chamber affirmed) that:
- The *actus reus* of aiding and abetting requires conduct that had a "substantial effect" on the commission of the crime
- "Specific direction" (as articulated by the ICTY Appeals Chamber in *Perišić*, 28 February 2013) is **not** a required element

The Taylor standard is in **doctrinal tension** with *Perišić* and aligns with the ICTY Appeals Chamber's subsequent course-correction in *Šainović et al.* (23 January 2014).

## 6. Joint Criminal Enterprise (JCE)

The SCSL applied JCE I, II and III across the AFRC, CDF, RUF and Taylor cases. The framework derives from *Tadić* (ICTY Appeal, 15 July 1999) but is elaborated in the SCSL judgments with attention to the Sierra Leone-specific factual context.

The CDF case in particular raised questions about the application of JCE to the activities of pro-government militia in a context of widespread atrocity.

## 7. Destruction of religious or cultural property

**CDF case** (Norman, Fofana, Kondewa) — Statute Art. 3(d) (destruction of buildings dedicated to religion, education, art or charitable purposes, hospitals, etc., not used for military purposes). The CDF case produced significant findings on the destruction of religious sites by the Kamajor militia.

## 8. The "greatest responsibility" formulation

**SCSL Statute Art. 1.** The Court has competence over persons "who bear the greatest responsibility". The Appeals Chamber held in *Fofana and Kondewa* (Decision on Preliminary Motion Based on Lack of Jurisdiction, 25 May 2004 — and subsequent decisions) that the formula is a **prosecutorial discretion guide**, not a jurisdictional bar.

## 9. The RSCSL and ongoing functions

The Residual Special Court for Sierra Leone (RSCSL) inherited the SCSL legacy on **2 December 2013**. RSCSL functions:
- Supervision of sentences (Taylor in the UK since 15 October 2013; other convicted persons in various enforcement States)
- Witness protection
- Maintenance of the archives
- Prosecution of contempt and false testimony (if any arises)
- Responding to claims for compensation and assistance requests

The RSCSL has not issued substantive new jurisprudence on the merits — its role is residual.
