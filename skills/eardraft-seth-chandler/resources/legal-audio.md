# Legal Texts for the Ear

Load this whenever the source is a brief, judicial opinion, statute, regulation, contract, memo, client letter, CLE script, casebook note, or exam outline — even if the user does not say the material is legal.

## Why legal prose defeats text-to-speech

Legal writing carries much of its meaning in a *print apparatus* that has no audio equivalent. Italics mark case names. Quotation marks fence the exact words of an authority. Footnote numbers offload citations. Subsection lettering encodes hierarchy. Reporter citations exist to be looked up, not heard. Read literally by a synthetic voice, "*Brown v. Bd. of Educ.*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954)" is noise; read aloud by a person, it is a mouthful nobody would say.

The general transformation rules in the core file all apply. This file governs what the core file cannot see.

## The one rule that overrides everything

**Never rewrite quoted authority.** The core skill's job is recasting sentences for the ear. Applied to a block quote from an opinion, a statutory provision, or a contract term, that produces a misquotation — and a misquotation of a holding is a professional problem, not a stylistic one.

Quoted material is reproduced exactly. Where an exact quotation is genuinely unlistenable, you have three honest options and one forbidden one:

- Frame it verbally and read it as written, letting the framing carry the structure.
- Shorten it by *ellipsis* — cutting is permitted, altering is not — and say so: "the Court said, in relevant part."
- Paraphrase openly, signposted as paraphrase: "the Court's point, in substance, was that…" The audience must never be able to mistake your paraphrase for the Court's words.
- **Forbidden:** silently smoothing a quotation so it sounds better aloud.

The same applies to operative contract language and to statutory text. Describe the structure; do not restate the rule and present it as the rule.

## Citations

Citations are for retrieval. A listener cannot retrieve anything. Default to stripping them down to the minimum that keeps the reference intelligible.

**Full case citations.** *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) becomes "Brown versus Board of Education, decided in 1954." Drop the reporter volume, the page, and the pincite. Read "v." as "versus" for United States material; English practice says "and" or "against" for civil cases, so follow the source's own jurisdiction.

**Short forms.** *Id.*, *supra*, *infra*, and short cites are invisible to the ear — a listener cannot look back to see what "id." refers to. Delete them and repeat the case name. Repetition that would be clumsy in print is correct in audio.

**String citations.** Never read one aloud. "Accord Smith, 12 F.3d 100; Jones, 14 F.3d 220; Lee, 19 F.3d 55" becomes "several courts of appeals have agreed." Name at most two cases, and only if a name is doing work later.

**When the listener does need the cite.** CLE audio offered for credit, or a lecture where the audience is expected to take the reference down, is the exception. Read the citation in full once, at first mention, slowly — then use the short name for the rest of the piece. Ask which situation applies if it is not obvious.

## Symbols and abbreviations

Expand everything. A synthetic voice will either skip these or mangle them.

| Written | Spoken |
|---------|--------|
| § 1983 | "section 1983" |
| §§ 12–14 | "sections 12 through 14" |
| 42 U.S.C. § 1983 | "section 1983" — add "of title 42 of the U.S. Code" only if the title matters |
| Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6) | "Rule 12(b)(6)", spoken "rule twelve b six" |
| ¶ 14 / ¶¶ 14–16 | "paragraph 14" / "paragraphs 14 to 16" |
| J. / C.J. / JJ. | "Justice" / "Chief Justice" / "Justices" |
| e.g. / i.e. / cf. | "for example" / "that is" / "compare" |
| et seq. | "and following" |
| cert. denied | "the Supreme Court denied review" |

**Subsection strings.** "(a)(2)(B)(iv)" is unlistenable as a run of letters and numbers. Where precision is required, expand fully: "subsection (a), paragraph 2, subparagraph B, clause four." Where it is not — and usually it is not — describe the provision instead: "the fourth clause of the definition."

## Italics, quotation marks, and footnotes

**Italics.** Case names are italicised in print and unmarked in speech. Signpost them verbally on first mention: "in the *Chevron* decision," "the Court in *Rahimi* held." After that the bare name is clear.

**Quotation boundaries.** Print fences a quotation; audio does not. Whenever the exact words matter — and in legal audio they usually do — mark both ends. Either the explicit form ("quote… end quote") or a verbal frame with a pause: "The Court put it this way." … "That is the whole of the holding." Choose one convention and hold it for the entire piece; switching mid-document is worse than either.

**Footnotes.** Legal writing is footnote-dense and audio has no footnotes.

- Pure citation footnotes: delete. Never read a footnote number aloud.
- Substantive footnotes: either fold into the sentence they attach to, or gather at the end of the section behind a verbal marker — "a qualification on that point." Folding is better where the note is short.
- A footnote carrying a genuine caveat on the main claim must not be dropped. That is the one class where deletion changes the law being described.

## Numbers, money, and dates

Read as a person would say them. `$1,250,000` becomes "one and a quarter million dollars" in narrative prose — but a statutory threshold, a jurisdictional amount, or a damages award is read exactly, because the figure *is* the point. Dates spoken naturally: "March 3rd, 2026," not the numeric form.

## Party and procedural shorthand

Expand on first use and then keep it consistent: plaintiff-appellant, movant, petitioner, the government, the defendant below. The core file's rule against synonym variation matters more here than anywhere else — a listener who loses track of which party is "appellant" has lost the argument.

## Latin

Terms of art in ordinary professional use — *res judicata*, *certiorari*, *stare decisis*, *ejusdem generis*, *prima facie*, *voir dire* — are kept for a legal audience and glossed once for a lay one: "res judicata, the rule that a matter already decided cannot be relitigated." Do not gloss for lawyers; it is condescending, and the core file's warning against talking down applies.

## Register by use case

| Use | Register |
|-----|----------|
| A brief or opinion for commute listening | Keep the technical vocabulary; strip the citation apparatus hard. |
| Oral-argument preparation | Preserve the argument's own structure — the point is to hear where it is unclear, not to smooth it. |
| CLE audio | Keep terminology; read key citations once in full; signpost sections clearly for people taking notes. |
| Client-facing explanation | Strip jargon, gloss every term of art, and keep the caveats the print version carried. |
| Student outlines and case briefs | Enumerate explicitly; repeat holdings; the self-refreshing-argument rule earns its keep. |

## Before delivering

Check four things:

1. Every quotation reproduces the source exactly, or is openly marked as a paraphrase.
2. No holding has been strengthened. "The Court suggested" must not have become "the Court held."
3. Caveats and qualifications survived the transformation — these are the first casualties of writing for flow.
4. Client-facing audio carries whatever disclaimer the written version carried. Losing it in the conversion is a real risk, because disclaimers are exactly the kind of dry paragraph a listening-optimised rewrite wants to cut.

## Jurisdiction

The examples here are United States. The principles — strip retrieval apparatus, mark quotation boundaries, expand symbols, never alter quoted authority — hold anywhere. The citation formats and abbreviations do not. Working with material from another system, keep the principles and substitute the local conventions.
