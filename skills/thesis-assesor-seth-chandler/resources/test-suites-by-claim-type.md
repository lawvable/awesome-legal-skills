# Test Suites by Claim Type

Choose the smallest suite that can expose the thesis's material defects. Combine modules for
mixed claims. Do not run every test mechanically.

## Doctrinal or normative claim

Use five to seven of these for a mature claim:

1. **Core case:** the strongest intended application.
2. **Easy extension:** a nearby application testing useful reach.
3. **Hard case:** facts exposing ambiguity in the rule or standard.
4. **Political mirror or role reversal:** the same principle under reversed valence or party
   position.
5. **Best counterexample:** the strongest fair case against the thesis.
6. **Institutional stress:** implementation with weak information, incentives, or capacity.
7. **Boundary case:** a case just outside the proposed rule.

Treat core-case failure as potentially fatal, mirror failure as a consistency problem,
hard-case uncertainty as a drafting problem, and an arbitrary boundary as a scope problem.

## Empirical or causal claim

Select from:

- **Construct validity:** does the measure capture the concept?
- **Identification:** can the evidence distinguish causation from correlation?
- **Alternative explanation:** what rival mechanism fits the observations?
- **Selection and missingness:** who or what is absent from the data?
- **External validity:** where should the result stop generalizing?
- **Replication or triangulation:** do different data or methods converge?
- **Legal translation:** even if the finding is true, does it support the legal conclusion?

## Historical claim

Select from:

- **Source survival:** would missing or destroyed sources change the account?
- **Selection:** were sources chosen because they support the thesis?
- **Anachronism:** does the claim import modern categories into the period?
- **Representativeness:** do elite texts or unusual disputes represent ordinary practice?
- **Rival account:** can the same record support a materially different explanation?
- **Change over time:** does the thesis flatten distinct periods?
- **Doctrinal payoff:** does the history bear the interpretive weight assigned to it?

## Theoretical, interpretive, or taxonomic claim

Select from:

- **Counterexample:** what accepted case resists the account?
- **Boundary:** does the category include too much or too little?
- **Explanatory gain:** what does the theory explain better than its rivals?
- **Compression versus distortion:** is simplicity purchased by erasing relevant difference?
- **Prediction or retrodiction:** what result should follow that rivals would not expect?
- **Falsifier:** what evidence or case would make the author abandon or narrow the claim?
- **Use test:** what later scholar or interpreter can do with the category?

## Comparative claim

Select from:

- **Functional equivalence:** do the compared institutions solve the same problem?
- **Context:** do constitutional structure, procedure, culture, or political economy explain
  the apparent difference?
- **Selection:** why were these jurisdictions or periods chosen?
- **Translation:** are similar terms masking different legal functions?
- **Transplantability:** would the proposed borrowing work under the receiving system's
  institutions and incentives?
- **Negative case:** does an omitted jurisdiction undermine the claimed pattern?

## Early-stage screen

For a hunch or seminar-paper idea, use only three tests:

1. the strongest intended example;
2. the strongest fair counterexample or rival account; and
3. the boundary, measurement, or source problem most likely to defeat the project.

Expand the suite only if the claim survives and further precision will affect the verdict.
