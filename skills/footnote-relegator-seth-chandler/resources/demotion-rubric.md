# Demotion Rubric

Classify every sentence (or tight sentence cluster) as **protected** or
**relegable**. When in doubt, protect — a slightly less condensed body is a
smaller failure than a broken argument.

## Protected — never demote

1. **Thesis moves.** The claim of the article, of a Part, of a Section; any
   sentence that states what the author is arguing.
2. **Chain-of-reasoning steps.** If the argument is "A, therefore B, therefore
   C," none of A, B, C may leave the body — even if one of them looks like a
   detail. Test: delete the sentence and read the paragraph; if the conclusion
   now arrives unearned, the sentence was a link.
3. **Definitions the body uses later.** A term defined once and relied on in
   three sections must be defined in the body. (A *refinement* of the
   definition — edge cases, competing usages set aside — is relegable.)
4. **Primary evidence for a contested claim.** The key case, the central
   statistic, the crucial document. Supporting or cumulative evidence is
   relegable; the evidence the claim stands on is not.
5. **Topic and framing sentences.** The first sentence of a paragraph usually
   tells the reader what the paragraph does; moving it decapitates the
   paragraph.
6. **Roadmaps and signposts.** "Part II traces…, Part III argues…" — dull, but
   structural. (If they are redundant, flag them in the memo as deletion
   candidates *for the author*; do not move or delete them yourself.)
7. **Anything already in a footnote.** Existing notes are already subordinate;
   this skill never demotes note text further or promotes it.

## Relegable — the taxonomy

These categories share vocabulary with the tentacle-footnote-finder skill,
deliberately: they are the kinds of surplus a body sentence carries that a
careful reader will accept finding below the line.

1. **Qualifications and caveats.** "This holds only where…, though some
   jurisdictions…" — when the qualification does not change the argument's
   conclusion, it can qualify from a footnote. If the caveat *guts* the claim,
   it is not a caveat; it is part of the claim. Protect it.
2. **Compressed history and genealogy.** How the doctrine evolved, where the
   idea came from, whose phrasing is echoed — when the argument needs only the
   current state, the path there is enrichment.
3. **Literature placement.** Sentences locating the argument among other
   scholars ("Unlike X, who…; and in contrast to Y's account…"). The body
   needs the author's position; the seating chart can move down.
4. **Source criticism and methodological asides.** Archival thinness, coding
   choices, why one dataset over another — essential to scrutiny, not to the
   argument's flow.
5. **Counterexamples, exceptions, comparisons.** The case that complicates
   the generalization, the foreign analogue, the near-miss — *when the body
   claim is stated with appropriate modesty without them*. If the exception is
   what the paragraph is about, protect it.
6. **Secondary and cumulative examples.** Where three examples make the point,
   the first (or strongest) stays; the rest move as "further examples" notes.
   Keep the example the surrounding prose actually discusses.
7. **Definitional refinements.** Competing usages, boundary cases, why a term
   is used one way here — the working definition stays up; the seminar on it
   goes down.
8. **Asides and digressions.** The paragraph-long tangent the author could not
   bear to cut. This is the classic tentacle footnote; move it whole.

## Micro-examples

**Relegable qualification.** Body: "Detention under § 1226(c) is mandatory.
*To be sure, a handful of district courts have implied a durational limit, but
no circuit has adopted one.*" → The italicized sentences move; the claim
stands, the hedge hedges from below.

**Protected chain link (looks like detail, is not).** "The statute's 1996
amendment removed the discretionary-release clause. *That removal* is why the
canon of constitutional avoidance cannot do the work the government asks of
it." → The first sentence looks like compressed history (category 2), but the
second sentence's argument depends on it. Protect.

**Relegable literature placement.** "Scholars have treated the two doctrines
in isolation. *Ablavsky examines the first at length; Nash the second; only
Allread gestures at their connection, in a different register.*" → The claim
of isolation stays; the roll call moves.

**Secondary examples.** "State courts resisted. *The Ohio Supreme Court
refused to enforce…; Wisconsin went further, holding…; and Vermont's
legislature…*" → Keep whichever example later prose relies on; move the rest
as one "further instances" note. If none is relied on later, keep the
strongest one.

## Cluster guidance

Prefer moving whole sentences and 2–3 sentence clusters. Clause-level surgery
("move the subordinate clause of this sentence") produces choppy bodies and
fragmentary notes; do it only when a single clause is unmistakably a
free-standing caveat and the sentence reads cleanly without it.

When two adjacent sentences are individually marginal but jointly relegable
(a claim plus its own qualification, an example plus its complication), treat
the cluster as one ledger unit with one destination note.
